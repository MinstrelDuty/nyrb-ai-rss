# 正文

Joan Barfoot’s​ first novel came to her as she was driving back to Toronto in a snowstorm after spending Christmas with her family. It was the mid-1970s and Barfoot was working as a newspaper journalist. Her work exposed her to stories of human error, misjudgment, courage, luck. ‘I am interested in extremities,’ she later wrote, ‘since that’s where a human’s actions and reactions will be most true and acute.’ As she drove, the situation for a story emerged: a woman called Abra, living by herself in a cabin in the woods. Did Abra simply walk out of her life? What did she leave behind?

Barfoot began writing with no plan in mind. She didn’t have notes, just Abra, making sense of her past and her reasons for abandoning her children. When the novel was published (as *Abra* in Canada in 1978 and *Gaining Ground* in Britain two years later), Barfoot was astonished by the response. At events, audience members attacked Abra’s selfishness and were perplexed or troubled by her courage. One person asked who would look after Abra in her old age.

*Gaining Ground* can be read alongside other novels about women’s liberation by Canadian authors such as Alice Munro, Margaret Atwood or Carol Shields. Like those books, it reminds us that a generation of feminists argued for the destruction of the nuclear family as the key to women’s freedom, and to their sanity. But the future Barfoot imagined for Abra is stranger and sparer. What would it look like, she asks, for a woman to refuse to fulfil the needs of her children, reject all attachments and obligations, and live only according to her instincts?

At the start of the novel, some years after Abra’s disappearance, a young woman arrives at the cabin in the woods. This ‘very pale person’ interrupts her in the garden where she grows her food. Abra is aware of the way she must appear to visitors: middle-aged, with unevenly cut hair and brown, weathered skin, crouching among the lettuces. Only after a moment or two does she realise the visitor is her daughter, Katie. She tries not to scare her, but also suppresses an impulse to pick up a hoe, ‘not to hurt her, but because I feel defenceless, naked, standing here against her’.

Katie has come to the cabin to understand why Abra disappeared from her life. ‘I don’t think I can help you,’ Abra tells her. ‘If it’s explanations you want, I can’t give you any.’ But she feels compassion for the person in front of her, and decides to let Katie return each day until her college course starts in the autumn. Katie is angry, but also curious. Abra finds her a challenging presence: ‘I can feel her waiting, the pressure of her waiting.’ Later, we’re told about a squirrel Abra rescued and then released back into the forest: ‘His bright eyes watched me move around the cabin, and I was conscious of his watching. I was lulled by his company, comforted by it, felt a seductive pleasure in caring for him; I recognised the danger of the false comfort.’

The only person from her past who knows of Abra’s existence in the woods is her husband, Stephen. He finds her when the man who sold Abra her cabin calls him to ask if they like their new place; later he needs her address to divorce her. He doesn’t tell their children – Katie and her brother, Elliott – why their mother acted as she did, out of respect for Abra’s privacy and a rather more desperate fear for her state of mind. (He calls her lack of feeling for her children ‘grotesque’.) Katie has to do her own digging to discover her mother’s whereabouts. During one of their stilted conversations, she tells Abra she plans to study archaeology, drawn to the idea of working ‘a fraction of an inch at a time’ to uncover the past. Abra thinks she understands: ‘It is like my garden, slow, delicate, careful work, protective work, and in the end something may come of it and something may not.’

Abra may be equivocal about the importance of examining the past, but she is better at it than she realises. Much of the book is taken up with her meditations on her present life and the one she left behind. As she remembers the past, her sentences steady and her voice, disjointed at the novel’s opening, falls gracefully and evenly. She is a natural narrator, rigorously self-examining. She tries to be precise and truthful, unafraid.

One of the consequences of Katie’s appearance is to make Abra self-aware again. It’s a trick Barfoot deploys in many of her books: a character stepping or floating out of her body to look at the person she’s become. Sometimes a character is confronted by a vision of her younger self and subjected to a cool appraisal. Abra finds these apparitions turn up in moments of crisis or predicament, when her resolve is weakening. But the scrutiny goes both ways. She is able to line them up: the young bride, the new mother, the housewife sliding into depression and despair. The effect is one of continuous dissociation. In each of these figures, she detects ‘a void, a blank … I do not feel related.’ In middle age, she continues to be disconnected from her own experience:

> Odd – I see that remembering has separated me from it; that looking back at myself as an object of my memory has made everything I saw then an object as well, so that I have lost the unity. It is part of my confusion now, my disorientation. At this moment I don’t belong, although the knowledge that I am home has not changed.

Some of her old helplessness remains and Katie won’t necessarily find the answers she’s looking for. Where Abra might be expected to show sorrow or regret, there is only blankness. The most she can do is raise her hands in a gesture of openness, which is also a shrug.

Barfoot has said that she’s interested in lapses. Her characters reach middle age feeling that they were too young or too old to join their era’s struggle for progress and are frustrated to have missed out on some of its achievements. (Shields wrote a novel about this, *Happenstance*, in which a middle-aged housewife feels ‘cheated by time’.) In Barfoot’s second novel, *Dancing in the Dark* (1982), Edna watches women only a little younger than herself protesting on television and is unsettled by the changing tone of the magazines she reads. In *Duet for Three* (1986), Aggie realises that her mother had it tougher than she does and was stronger as a result, even though Aggie is a hard-working single parent. Later, she is disappointed by the choices of her own daughter (another ‘pale and hostile child’), who seems ‘determined to make her life small’. Only Susannah, in *Family News* (199o), seizes the freedoms available to her, choosing to raise a child on her own, though she becomes estranged from her family as a result.

Are these women partly to blame for their circumstances? As Susannah says, ‘there have always been brave women.’ Looking back on her marriage at nineteen, Abra perceives a failure of heart. ‘I know that I was not unintelligent, but I did not control. I was not insensitive, but I could not see. I was, perhaps, simply too young, and so I committed a capitulation.’ Other capitulations followed: she gave up her job when Stephen finished university; she accepted the inevitability of motherhood. She recalls a feeling of powerlessness, an inability to recognise other options. ‘What would I do? I did not feel it could be my choice; I did not know how to make a choice.’ The problem seems to be that she never allowed herself to imagine the future she wanted. Instead, she was carried along by ‘an assumption … a vague, undetailed picture’ of children, husband, home. Later, she wonders if her failure was due to her meagre education.

The life Abra leaves is a good one. Stephen is doing well as a stockbroker. They live in a large house with an acre of garden. Their children are happy (though Elliott is more guarded) and Abra doesn’t need to work. She has no real friends, but she speaks to her mother on the phone. Barfoot traces the paradoxes of the suburban housewife: limited but also sheltered, exclusive, coddled. Abra concedes: ‘It was, I thought, the best life I could have hoped for, better than most people dare to hope for.’ Why, then, her sense of ‘rootling unease’?

Her unravelling begins when the children are small. Her experience of motherhood is of endless questions to be answered carefully, of small forms ‘leaning against me, touching me, tugging at me, talking to me’. When Stephen takes the children for a few hours on weekend mornings, she readies herself for their return, ‘always a pinch of dread in my stomach, gearing up for the performance’. ‘Where was the “I” in all of that?’ she asks. ‘I was the lover, the giver, the doer, the mother, the wife, and that is where the “I” was. Which makes me sound martyred, only of course it wasn’t like that.’ There are moments of everyday pleasure: trips to the zoo, movies, sex on the sofa when the children are in bed. But Abra regards her happiness uneasily. When Stephen notices her restlessness, he is sympathetic. Would it help her to go to college, or get a job? What Abra needs is not distraction, however, but something intangible. She needs to believe that her existence has importance beyond her small household.

As she struggles, Abra’s view of her children hardens. She watches their moods, assessing their ability to respond to the uncertainties of the adult world. Katie, easy-going and intuitive, is ‘a better survivor than Elliott’, a mistrustful boy given to cruel jokes and ‘inheld hatred’. Abra knows they will make it to adulthood, and absolves herself of responsibility. She considers the possibility that her leaving will make them stronger, even ‘terribly powerful’. ‘You’re eighteen, you say?’ she asks Katie when she arrives at the cabin. ‘Then you’ve survived. Why don’t you leave it at that?’ Elliott’s bitterness operates, more dangerously, off the page.

At this point, I should mention an episode, little short of a horror story, which occurs midway through the novel. The narrative has jumped back in time to Abra’s first winter in the cabin. In preparation, she drives to the nearby town for ‘the big winter shopping’. At the hardware shop, the proprietor, eyed by his son at the till, tells her about the cabin’s previous inhabitants: a hopeful farmer, his wife and their baby. The first spring, they grazed cattle on the rocky pasture, and in the autumn the herd sold. But the second summer was hot, and the herd failed. From then on, the family were seen sporadically and, during the following winter, not at all. A neighbouring farmer discovered their bodies: the man collapsed over his shotgun; the baby bundled in its cot with a hole through its middle. It’s a story of hideous misery (Barfoot also tells it in *Family News*, when Susannah unearths a newspaper clipping), but it’s also cautionary. We notice the shopkeeper’s description of the farmer’s wife, worn to the bone, holding her shrinking baby. ‘Her eyes were crazy-like, they wouldn’t keep still, wandered all over the place, and she couldn’t talk very well, couldn’t get sentences out, it seemed. It made people edgy, the way she looked, and maybe they kept away from her a bit.’ Is this what will happen to Abra if she stays alone in the cabin? Or is this what she has escaped?

Barfoot has said​ that she read *The Golden Notebook* once a year, until she couldn’t read it again. The long section describing Abra’s disintegration recalls the rising panic of Anna Wulf’s notebooks (the blue one, for craziness), but is closer still to the coolness of that other novel of breakdown, Penelope Mortimer’s *The Pumpkin Eater* (1962). (Barfoot admires ‘the several British Penelopes’.) Abra is remembering from a distance of years, and she stands outside her own experience, detached from the story she is bringing to conclusion. What happens to her happens subtly, ‘like a slow-forming concrete’. ‘Somewhere in me was some deep-buried worm that gnawed its way through the hidden places of all the parts of my life, so gently, so delicately, that I could not tell, until the hidden places had all been eaten away and there was nothing left to root me to what I had or what I was.’

In winter, she watches at the window for the children’s return from school, dreading – almost willing – that a car might skid round the icy bend and kill them both. Hearing of the suicide of a school friend (a wife, a mother of three), she goes down to the basement and checks she has all the necessary equipment to hang herself. These are fantasies, but they signal an end is near. It comes swiftly: ‘I swear I loved them all, and I did the best I could. And then I left them, left all of it.’ Seeing an advert for the cabin, two hundred miles from the city, she makes secret arrangements (cash from an inheritance transferred to the lawyer’s office, the disposal of clothes and belongings, a neighbour to watch the children, a letter to Stephen), before slipping out of her life. Towards her children, she registers only a final indifference: ‘There were no justifications, no reasonings and no guilt; they failed to move me.’ A day later, at the cabin, what feels like her own death flares up before her, irrevocably, in ‘the brilliant last moment’.

And what is Abra granted, if not a second life? At the cabin, she is unburdened, rinsed of her past. She pulses with a new sensuousness, a ‘wish to touch all young things’ – lettuces in the damp earth, the soft silk around the corn cobs. For all her strangeness and power, Abra is a person of modest desires. There comes a moment when she realises her solitude: ‘I kept feeling spasms of joy, instants of realising, and it happened when I saw the bowl and the saucepan to be washed, all there was of my meal, just my own, from now on.’

Something else arrives in Abra’s life with the cabin – vocation. From the first, the cabin emerges as an idea, ‘a private vision, and just the sound, the presence of these people, any people, might take it away; it could be frightened, would make itself a secret again, and I would not find it with them.’ Abra takes the cabin to heart: it becomes her purpose, the discipline she lives by. At first, there are ‘many days of work, no thoughts, just the labour’, but in time, she achieves a rhythm, spending her days in ‘a mysterious intensity of concentration’. She lives without mirrors or clocks, learns to grow her own food, chop wood, eat and sleep when she needs to: the sorts of task that induce self-forgetfulness, like writing. The satisfaction she discovers is also Barfoot’s, the young writer in her room in Toronto, working on the first of many novels, falling in love with her work, building her life around it. Perhaps Barfoot had read *The Little Virtues* (1962), in which Natalia Ginzburg cautions against motherhood as a vocation:

> The birth and development of a vocation needs space, space and silence, the free silence of space. Our relationship with our children should be a living exchange of thoughts and feelings, but it should also include deep areas of silence: it should be an intimate relationship but it must not violently intrude on their privacy; it should be a just balance between silence and words.

Ginzburg described her own vocation as a writer as her salvation.

Abra is 43 when Katie comes back into her life. It is a magic number in Barfoot’s books: Edna is 43 when she finds herself in an institution, having murdered her husband; Susannah is 43 when she decides to see her mother again after their long separation; and Nora, the protagonist of *Luck* (2005), is 43 when she is widowed. It functions as a marker, of the end of one life and the beginning of another, the point at which these women take the measure of their freedom. There are possibilities but also limits. ‘Well, though, beyond ripping up the garden and the house,’ Aggie thinks after her husband’s death, ‘there were thirty, forty years.’ ‘A medieval lifetime,’ Edna reflects, staring down the barrel of her new, albeit diminished life, ‘a whole pure future.’

In a new afterword to *Gaining Ground*, Barfoot, who is eighty, writes that Abra lives in ‘the pure moment’. She has removed herself from the constraints of measured time, and exists beyond the contingencies of history. Doing some rough maths, Abra would be 91 this year, though perhaps, with the reissue of the novel, her clock (not that she has one) has been reset, and she has been granted another half-lifetime of living as she pleases.
