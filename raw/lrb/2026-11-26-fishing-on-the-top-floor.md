# 正文

Buildings​ tend to outlast the historical forces that conjure them into being. In November 2024, a six-year-old girl called Frankie James leaned her full weight on a detonator, triggering the demolition of Hexham House, the fifteen-storey tower block in Newcastle in which she had lived all her life. Along with two other children, Frankie was chosen after entering an art competition; she submitted a cardboard replica of her former home decorated with rainbows. The tower’s demolition was advertised by Newcastle City Council, which livestreamed drone footage from multiple angles and advised crowds on where they could safely assemble to watch.

Across much of the 20th century, there was a dramatic expansion of public housing in Britain. As recently as 1980, one in three families lived in council housing. In England, an estimated 1.6 million of the nearly five million council homes were flats rather than houses. These flats were often above shops or stacked together in disaggregated townhouses and terraces, but others were distributed among at least ten thousand high-rise tower blocks, most of them built between 1955 and 1975. Housing departments in almost every city in postwar Britain commissioned architects to design large, sometimes elegant, towers for working-class people to live in. These blocks – like Hexham House, built in 1968 – have become symbolic of a bygone era. The modernist zeal, theories of social interaction and belief in architectural determinism that animated their construction now seem remote.

Planners, politicians, journalists and residents’ groups have tried to account for the rapid emergence and almost universal denigration of council tower blocks. Holly Smith’s *Up in the Air* is a bold foray into the debate. In one sense a straightforward history of high-rises, it also uses them as a vantage point from which to survey the inequalities of British urban life. Smith deliberately ignores the new breed of plasticky private tower developments and focuses instead on those built by municipal authorities. She is interested as much in the way the buildings were shaped by their tenants as by their architects, and argues that during the second half of the 20th century the tower block became a ‘crucible for reimagining the welfare state’. The only comparable history is *Tower Block* by Miles Glendinning and Stefan Muthesius, a sprawling technical compendium published thirty years ago.

Between 1870 and 1940, as the economist Robert J. Gordon wrote, homes in the West were increasingly ‘networked’: plugged into grids of electricity, gas, water, sewerage, transport and communication, a process that transformed domestic labour and reshaped the relationship between the family and the city. Building high-rises before this networking made little sense, and it’s no surprise that the utopias of the early 20th century – garden cities, model villages, paternalistic company towns – were low-rise. Residents of the Katharine Buildings in East London, a five-storey tenement built in 1885 and owned by a philanthropic trust, had to carry water in buckets, to haul coal for heat to the upper floors and to wash in communal bathhouses. Beatrice Webb, who worked as a rent collector in the building, remembered that its toilets ‘became the obtrusively dominant feature of the several staircases, up and down which trooped, morning, noon and night, the six hundred or more inhabitants of the buildings’. For the earnest reformers who wrote about the Victorian city, among them Engels, Mayhew, Mearns and Rowntree, density was a byword for anonymity, transience, disorder and a host of psychic and sexual ills. Slums had to be abolished.

Smith begins her story with Arthur Greenwood, the minister for health in the second Labour government (1929-31), who was determined to improve the living conditions of the poorest households in Britain by clearing slums. The Greenwood Act, passed in 1930, created a subsidy for municipal authorities to build upwards on expensive inner-city land. It resulted in a tentative sprouting of municipal apartment buildings in London, Liverpool, Glasgow and Leeds. For the most part these buildings – the delicate curved courtyard of St Andrew’s Gardens in Liverpool, for example, or the squat, modernist Kensal House flats on Ladbroke Grove in London – were curiosities, paltry counterweights to the sprawling low-rise suburban estates on the peripheries of major towns. But they had a sheen of modernity. Smith quotes a *Times* article from 1934 that predicted babies of the future would be swaddled and left on balconies to drink in the fresh air, becoming ‘kings and queens of infinite space’. Architectural magazines published articles about tower blocks on stilts in vast landscaped gardens; long retractable windows could turn flats into open-air terraces. A spoof handbook from 1936 imagined residents playing golf between balconies and fishing in communal ponds from top-floor windows.

High-rise living became common in the decades following the Second World War thanks to cross-party support for slum clearance, postwar reconstruction and top-down urban planning. Tower blocks on comprehensively planned council estates were a key feature of the housing boom. While many of the buildings were functional and cheaply manufactured, they often enmeshed residents in a web of communally managed amenities – playgrounds, walkways, laundrettes, nurseries, pubs and community centres. This was the apotheosis of the networked home, with many tower block residents enjoying indoor plumbing, fitted kitchens, central heating and electricity for the first time. Some towers offered more baroque services. The soaring Park Hill flats built in Sheffield between 1957 and 1961 used the ‘Garchey’ system of waste disposal: kitchen sinks were connected to large, sinister-sounding drains that channelled refuse into flooded tanks deep within the bowels of the building. Louis Garchey boasted of the system’s ability to dispose of broken glass and hot ash. At Churchill Gardens in Pimlico, surplus boiling water from Battersea Power Station was pumped under the Thames to heat the estate’s 1600 flats in winter. The development included a children’s library, whose metal shelves were warmed with heat from the power station, as well as a mortuary.

The social element in mid-20th-century social democracy found expression in towers designed to shape a group of strangers into a harmonious community. This involved a renewed acknowledgment of the benefits of density. The paternalist architects of Park Hill were determined to replace the city’s ‘Little Chicago’ slums but didn’t want to destroy their dense social world. They hoped an accumulation of encounters between residents on stairwells, or on airborne walkways built wide enough for milk floats, would lead to bonds of reciprocity, solidarity and kinship. They were keen readers of Michael Young and Peter Willmott’s *Family and Kinship in East London* (1957), a book that romanticised the social cohesion of the slums to warn against the isolation of poorly conceived housing estates. As Smith notes, many postwar attempts at social management had a prudish, draconian edge. Most residents of Park Hill were selected for their respectability, and the estate’s architects worried that the temptations of mass consumerism – particularly television – might unpick the social fabric they were carefully trying to stitch. She includes a wonderful photograph of blurry, ungovernable children playing a prohibited game of football in an antiseptically clean walkway hundreds of feet in the sky.

Smith’s narrative hinges on the Ronan Point disaster of 16 May 1968. In the early hours of the morning, Ivy Hodge, a woman on the eighteenth storey of the East London tower block, lit a match in her kitchen to boil a pot of water. Ronan Point had been completed just two months before; it was built using the Danish Larsen-Nielsen system in which a latticework of vast concrete slabs was manufactured offsite and then assembled like an Ikea flatpack by unskilled workers. The block was in Newham, a borough that was energetically building upwards at the time; cheap prefabricated towers had begun to dominate the skyline. Hodge didn’t know there had been a gas leak overnight and the match triggered an explosion large enough to blow out a load-bearing wall of the tower. Ronan Point’s entire south-east corner concertinaed downwards. Four people were killed and seventeen injured, mostly those who were unlucky enough to be in their living rooms at the time of the blast. Despite the weakness in the building exposed by the collapse, Ronan Point was patched up and reopened by Newham Council in 1973.

The disaster and its fallout are often seen as marking a terminal crisis in British architectural modernism. *Up in the Air* argues otherwise. Smith has unearthed a trove of letters sent by tower block residents to the subsequent inquiry into prefabricated high-rises. Many of the letter-writers were critical of the cheap, easy-to-build towers then in vogue, but they defended high-rise living. One respondent called for the introduction of communal ping-pong tables, coffee mornings and playgrounds on every tower block floor and was an advocate of ‘real multis, ones which could kick the present-day horrible efforts into a cocked hat’. Another letter-writer urged councils to investigate fibreglass as a building material and argued for towers with wider bases to prevent total collapse. A 14-year-old boy sent in diagrams of proposed joint fixtures. Smith suggests that tower block residents were not, for the most part, alienated conservatives who longed for suburban three-bed semis. They were thoughtful modernist subjects, people with high expectations who cared about the quality of their buildings. As the architect Sam Webb put it, blaming Le Corbusier for Ronan Point was ‘like blaming Mozart for Muzak’. *Up in the Air* argues that the disaster was the result of the state trying to fob off a generally hopeful and progressive working class with cheap and hastily built social infrastructure. In 1986 Ronan Point was finally demolished following a decades-long campaign by residents frustrated with the lack of meaningful improvements. An autopsy of the building found that its steel joints had been packed with cement bags, newspapers, cigarette boxes and even a tin of corned beef.

Webb described the successful campaign to demolish Ronan Point as a victory against ‘the might of Thatcher’. Why did he identify Thatcher with mass high-rise housing built by a Labour council? The political battle lines that flash into view in *Up in the Air* trouble the usual interpretation of postwar British history. On one side was a multiracial coalition of working-class council residents, sympathetic modernist architects and an ageing cohort of mid-century government planners. On the other, the often officious, callous housing departments of Labour councils, a corrupt network of building contractors and right-wing politicians. One of the biggest champions of prefabricated towers in the 1960s was Keith Joseph, who would become an intellectual powerhouse of Thatcherism. It helped that Joseph’s family owned the construction firm Bovis.

By the end of the 1980s, a new relationship between architecture, housing and the state had been forged, thanks to the Right to Buy scheme introduced in the 1980 Housing Act. This dealt a serious blow to state-owned housing. Millions of tenants exercised their Right to Buy, but most of them lived in low-rise suburban council homes: individual flats in tower blocks were more difficult to sell. While Conservative-run boroughs such as Wandsworth in South-West London hoped to turn high-rises into little republics of homeowners, their plans were consistently foiled by elements within the estates that resisted privatisation. Flat owners continued to rely on local governments to clean windows, repair lifts, change lightbulbs and manage centralised boilers. A technical and legal quagmire ensued. By 1985 Birmingham City Council had sold just 314 of its 55,372 flats.

At the same time, the theories about the relationship between architecture and society that inspired the mass production of tower blocks were effectively challenged. This is evident from the success of *Utopia on Trial*, a book published in 1985 by Alice Coleman, a geography professor who had worked on the Second Land Use Survey. She argued that high-density housing incentivised homosexuality and that the manufacturing of shells by limpets was proof of a trans-species biological drive towards private, ‘defensible space’. Coleman also held that crime rates increased when people felt no responsibility for the area in which they lived. She is almost certainly the only person to have been fêted by Thatcher, King Charles and Derek Hatton, the Trotskyist deputy leader of Liverpool City Council, whose regime built thousands of detached and semi-detached ‘Hatton houses’. Coleman was even suspicious of communal areas in council estates: walkways, courtyards, stairwells and playgrounds. After winning a large government grant following a meeting with Thatcher, she toured the country overseeing the retrofitting of tower blocks: courtyards were demolished, communal entrances bricked up, public areas divided by fences and electronic entry phone systems installed. These seemingly minor alterations abolished much of the shared space that architects had hoped would incubate a harmonious social body. Everywhere in the 1980s, mass housing estates, planned as coherent totalities, were torn apart and their services privatised.

As​ *Up in the Air* makes clear, however, none of this was inevitable. Until the 1980s, many tenants in high-rise towers were opposed to private homeownership. Smith gives the example of the Stephen and Matilda Tenants’ Co-Operative, which wrested control of the St Katharine’s Estate in Wapping in 1978 after a three-year battle with local and national governments. The estate, built in 1937, was once an elegant brick walk-up but had been half-abandoned in the 1970s following the closure of the docks. The co-operative was an experiment in democratic collective management – the brainchild of an assortment of local trade unionists, squatters and anti-regeneration groups. While co-operatives already existed in the private sector, this was the first in a municipal estate. There was something joyous and unruly about Stephen and Matilda’s early years. Its members proposed a newsletter called ORGASM (Organ of Stephen and Matilda). They held discos, arranged cinema trips, organised camping holidays and sent their children to a renovated ‘playflat’. Members were required to spend eight hours a week on various tasks, refurbishing empty flats as well as gardening, book-keeping, newsletter writing and driving the co-op minibus. This activism might have been unusual, but it represented social democracy in renewal rather than crisis. To a minority in the late 1970s, it seemed as though collective management rather than private homeownership might be the future of council estates.

Thousands of high-rises persisted into the late 20th century and beyond. When dozens of Liverpool’s tower blocks were earmarked for demolition at the turn of the millennium, some of their residents, mostly elderly Scousers born before the Second World War, took to the early internet to commemorate their homes. Between 1999 and 2012, Tenantspin, a digital art collective, produced live webcasts about high-rise living, many of which were introduced with a snippet of the Joy Division song ‘Transmission’. These events were accompanied by bustling chat room conversations in which tenants shared memories and debated politics and architecture – digital echoes of the world-making that tower block architects had once envisaged.

If Ronan Point represented a crisis of social democracy, the Grenfell fire, which killed 72 people in 2017, demonstrated the cost of decades of privatisation, deregulation and declining public accountability. Built in 1974 as part of a modernisation and slum clearance project in Notting Dale, the building was originally owned and managed by Kensington and Chelsea Council. In 1996 responsibility for managing the 24-storey tower was transferred to the Kensington and Chelsea Tenant Management Organisation, whose board comprised tenants, leaseholders, council appointees and independent members. A refurbishment in 2016 was outsourced to Rydon, a private construction company. Rydon, in turn, outsourced the tower’s cladding to another company, Harley Facades, which failed to check the safety of the panels it installed. In the years before the fire, the Grenfell Action Group, formed by local residents, repeatedly raised concerns about the management of the tower and the TMO’s approach to fire safety, but its warnings went unheeded.

There is an alternative history of high-rise public housing in Britain – one that is only implicit in *Up in the Air*. It concerns race. The biggest surge in tower block construction was roughly concurrent with the end of empire. As hundreds of thousands of colonial and postcolonial migrants moved into British cities in the 1950s and 1960s, they were effectively excluded from new, high-quality council housing. In 1961, just 4.5 per cent of Commonwealth migrant households in urban areas lived in council housing, compared with 23 per cent of all households across Britain. Housing departments marked down Black and Asian applicants, fearing their presence would disturb the communities they were attempting to create. As one anonymous housing officer in the West Midlands in the 1960s put it, ‘I wouldn’t put coloured people on a new estate mixed up with all the young couples. It’s got to go slowly.’ Some of those who did manage to get a home abandoned it following harassment by white residents. For the Black sociologist Sidney Jacobs, writing in 1985, council housing was ‘the British road to apartheid’.

In the 1970s, this pattern changed. Following race relations legislation and a new housing act, people of colour began to be housed by local authorities in significant numbers. But Black and Asian families were disproportionately housed in prefabricated, systems-built tower blocks. A survey of council tenants in the 1980s found that 48 per cent of Britons of Caribbean descent were living in flats in medium-rise estates and 21 per cent were living in high tower blocks (compared with 33 per cent and 7 per cent of white tenants). An unofficial colour line even ran through the stairwells of tower blocks. Black and Asian families were routinely allocated the least desirable flats in towers – usually on the bottom or top floors. At the time of the Grenfell fire, children who lived above the fourth floor in English tower blocks were more likely to be Black or Asian than white: 85 per cent of the deaths at Grenfell were of people of colour.
