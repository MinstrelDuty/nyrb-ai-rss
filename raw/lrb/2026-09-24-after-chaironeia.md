# 正文

The ancient​ Greek city-state, or polis, gave rise to the first systematic attempts at political thought. Similar city-states existed elsewhere, but it was the polis of the ancient Greek world that inspired Plato and Aristotle’s theories about the ideal state. Poleis preceded the classical Greek period of the fifth and fourth centuries BC, and in either vigorous or diminished form extended into the Hellenistic period (after the death of Alexander in 323 BC) and beyond, well into the Roman Empire. Classical Athens was a polis, but so was a tiny Arcadian town such as Orchomenos. In the second century AD, the obscure hamlet of Naryka petitioned the Emperor Hadrian to agree it was a polis, offering as proof its institutions and its mythical past as the supposed birthplace of the lesser Ajax in the *Iliad* (he acquiesced).

There was extraordinary variation in poleis across the eastern Mediterranean, and across the centuries. They included Athens and Sparta, developing in conscious opposition to each other; settlements on the Black Sea such as Olbia; Stagira in the Northern Aegean (the birthplace of Aristotle); Sicilian cities, often deracinated or destroyed, as Kamarina was in 484 by the tyrant Gelon; and the vigorous cities of Asia Minor (now western Turkey) – Pergamon, Ephesus (famous for its cult of Artemis), Miletus (the centre of the Ionian Enlightenment and early Greek philosophy) and Byzantium itself. But each community, large or small, had a clear identity, a culture and political life which did much to encourage artistic and literary creativity.

A polis may be most easily understood as a concentrated settlement, a small or larger town surrounded by its farmed territory, with superior status and political rights (more or less) for its male citizens, elements of self-government and leaders supervised by the political community. But this is the ideal type. The ‘polis’ could be a whole society, including women, or the male citizens exclusively. And Greeks could use the word to refer to any urban settlement, from a trading post in Egypt (Herodotus) to the town of Hykkara in Sicily (Thucydides). Aristotle in the *Politics* included Carthage in his survey of major constitutions.

The Copenhagen Polis Centre spent ten years (from 1993 to 2003) charting and analysing 1035 city-states. John Ma’s book, in some respects a monumental update of A.M.H. Jones’s *The Greek City* (1940), draws on the Copenhagen research as well as decades of work by French epigraphists such as Philippe Gauthier and Louis Robert on the vast number of inscriptions left by poleis. Historians used to think that the free polis died when Philip of Macedon defeated Athens and its allies at the Battle of Chaironeia in 338. But Robert’s judgment that ‘the Greek polis did not die at Chaironeia’ is now widely accepted. It is the leitmotif of Ma’s book, which in dense prose works through the ways that city-states continued to be self-governing, if not necessarily powerful. They negotiated with larger powers, absorbed nearby communities or engaged in elaborate diplomatic exchanges with one another, and they left thousands of public inscriptions. Ma’s study concentrates on the elements of self-government – the Aristotelian study of institutions – and there is much to be said for this; he wishes to stress a community’s self-governance for its common good, what he calls the ‘communitarian’ aspects of the post-classical polis. Questions remain, however, about how far ‘democracy’ and ‘autonomy’ flourished, and what democracy at the city level meant under a powerful overarching monarchy.

The consolidation of communities on the Greek mainland and Asia Minor began in the mid to late ‘Dark Ages’ (900-700 BC), with fortification, conquest and the establishment of borders, marked by shrines. The development of political communities is visible in the earliest written laws, especially in Crete, where – astonishingly early – polis communities tried to limit the powers of their high officials. As well as the patchwork of written evidence, there are material remains of growing communities, such as the warrior burial on the Cycladic island of Paros, a unique mass grave of the ashes of 120 young men in 140 funerary urns, carefully deposited in two stone cists – a late eighth-century communal state honour after a terrible military loss. Some places encroached on their neighbours, taking over or enslaving the *perioikoi* (‘dwellers around’). State institutions, laws and officials developed along growing anxiety over individuals or groups seizing power.

An ideology of the ‘common good’ emerged, with the polis determining what was to be done (even if this was, in fact, decided by elite leaders). In archaic Mytilene on the island of Lesbos, for instance, amid a succession of tyrants and changing alliances, the poet and politician Alcaeus laments the success of his rival Pittakos and mocks the hope that Pittakos will save the polis that elected him. Fines and taxes start to be imposed – in contrast to the random demands by dominant leaders one finds in Homer – as a sign of the polis acting as a recognisable proto-state. Formal citizenship becomes distinguished in some places from residency; a hierarchy of political rights is devised. Enslaving societies, such as Sparta, had huge subordinate populations and a small elite that held full citizenship and led a vigorous life of military training.

Competition between cities was one of the most productive aspects of late archaic and classical Greece, leading to a proliferation of sculpture, temples and religious festivals, where competitions were held in poetry, drama or athletics, of which the Olympic games were the largest. Ma analyses all this in terms of power-sharing between elites and the wider community in the late archaic period, from the factional fighting of Alcaeus and his peers on Lesbos to greater levels of harmony in Argos, Sicyon or (with tight rigidity) in Sparta. Sparta separated full citizens, ‘equals’, who did no physical labour, from the *perioikoi* and helots, a strategy that was ultimately unsustainable and led to some strange distortions. The world of classical Greek city-states, with their varied constitutions (democracy, oligarchy, tyranny and everything in between), civil strife and inter-polis wars, emerged through a process of rebalancing.

Missing from Ma’s account, however, as from Aristotle’s *Politics*, are the vitality of cultural life and religious worship – which itself encouraged literary, artistic and architectural innovation. Radical change was a function of rivalry between city-states. Athens and Thebes, close neighbours, developed very different polities with different cultures, dialects and alphabets, and Athenian drama loved to use gruesome Theban myths to offer an anti-type to Athens. Religious festivals in honour of different patron deities varied between cities, and since these festivals always involved singing, poetry or athletic competitions, polis identity was both displayed and perpetuated at them. Sparta characteristically pushed its festival activities towards feats of endurance for Apollo or Artemis, while Athens honoured Dionysos through theatre and wine-drinking. The great panhellenic festivals at Olympia and Delphi saw intense competition between athletes whose victories would be proclaimed for their cities and themselves. Victory could be a declaration of political ambition: the Athenian aristocrat Cimon, whose team won the chariot race at consecutive games, was murdered by the city’s ruling tyrants after his third victory.

The period after the Persian Wars, which came to an end with the defeat of Xerxes at the battle of Plataea in 479 BC, saw an alliance of maritime states turn into the Athenian Empire. Wars between Athens and Sparta followed, with struggles for hegemony across the Greek mainland and the Aegean, until Philip of Macedon gained overall control of the mainland Greek city-states. Ma provocatively labels this period, the age of most classical literature and philosophy, ‘The Hundred Years War’ (*c*.462-362 BC). He dislikes the ‘fetishisation’ of texts and artefacts from this time, and contrasts its disastrous military struggles, which exhausted the most powerful states, with the stability of poleis in the Hellenistic period and what he calls the ‘Great Convergence’ of accepted polis characteristics. The ‘Hundred Years’ in fact, as Ma admits, saw innovative and rational attempts by city-states to group together in amity or at least mutually beneficial calculus: there were federal states and multi-polis alliances, not to mention the Athenian Empire itself (a form, in Ma’s baroque jargon, of ‘supra-local structuration and negotiation’). But Ma sees these as less interesting or successful, for rather unclear reasons, than the alliances and negotiations conducted by city-states with the Hellenistic kingdoms into which Alexander’s empire was divided after his death.

The claim that the poleis of the Hellenistic period were unusually peaceful can be misleading, since wars, violence and imperial aggression continued, though we lack the perceptive and tragic analysis of a historian like Thucydides to draw this out. Athens and other poleis tried to regain their freedom from Macedonian hegemony through armed struggle in 323-22 and 267-61 BC. But Ma argues that, as powerful city-states gave up their quest for hegemony under the Macedonian and Hellenistic kingdoms, the oscillation of the smaller states between subordination and self-rule stabilised into a golden age for polis harmony. The King’s Peace of 386 BC – a treaty guaranteed by the Persian king Artaxerxes, which brought Sparta’s war against Corinth, Thebes, Athens and Argos to an end – emphasised autonomy, a concept that remained central to polis identity into the Hellenistic period. Civil strife became rarer, Ma claims, and, under the great Hellenistic kingdoms, the widespread introduction of ‘democracy’ began.

The emphasis on polis autonomy in 386 BC was in part a ploy by Sparta and Artaxerxes to prevent other city-states from uniting in leagues, and it too became a diplomatic weapon. Still, Ma argues that poleis in the period of the ‘Great Convergence’ (roughly from the late fourth to the third century BC) would negotiate with other states and the Hellenistic kings to maintain their autonomy and democracy. There are examples of violent change and loss of liberty in this period however: Kolophon in Asia Minor, for instance, built huge fortifications at great expense but was destroyed by Lysimachus, one of Alexander’s successors. It later regained its polis status, joining in political union with its port, Notium, which managed to resist the Seleucid king Antiochus III. When the neighbouring islands of Kos and Kalymnos merged, the citizens of Kalymnos had to swear not to establish oligarchy or tyranny, to remain in alliance with Ptolemy of Egypt and to remain loyal to the democracy – a fine example of a public statement of both the common good and political subordination.

The Hellenistic kings continued to use the language of autonomy and democracy throughout the fourth century. Ma admits that the terms might have been mere slogans, but argues that they helped garner support at a polis level for the dynasts and kings who claimed to grant them, and generally spread the idea that *demokratia* was a desirable system. The ‘democracy’ installed by Alexander the Great on the island of Chios co-existed with a Macedonian garrison, military contributions and personal interference from the Macedonian king. Yet the growing uniformity of poleis (assembly, agora, theatre, public buildings), the convergence of institutions in city after city across the Greek-speaking world and the spread of polis-forms well into the Near East would later make it possible for the Roman Empire to rule through largely self-governing cities in the Greek East.

The spread of city-states almost defies overarching analysis, and certainly the meaning of polis-hood had changed significantly by the second century BC, with the growing dominance of Roman power. Ma’s emphasis on elements of statehood is compelling; there is evidence of continuity in the form of public inscriptions that record the decisions of assemblies and councils, listing officials, procedures, fines and penalties, which survive from the sixth century BC or earlier right through the Hellenistic period. Other poleis, though, may have had less state apparatus, or left no inscriptions to show it: the Cycladic island of Melos was a polis that Athens destroyed in 415 BC and we would know little about it if Thucydides hadn’t focused on the atrocity. In his account, the Melians appeal to hope, justice and the gods, while the Athenian ambassadors tell them, in a line often quoted by ‘realists’, that ‘the strong do what they can and the weak suffer what they must.’

Ma rejects the Copenhagen Polis Centre’s conclusion that autonomy was not a defining feature of the polis. This depends, though, on what exactly is meant by autonomy, never defined here, and while Ma speaks of the ‘ambiguities’ of autonomy, it would be good to have these spelled out. Statehood is usually taken to include having an army and a foreign policy; if a polis contributed to a larger navy run by another entity, or paid tribute to a larger empire or kingdom, was its autonomy diminished? A minimum requirement might be Aristotle’s *autarchia*, or ‘self-sufficiency’, meaning that a community could feed itself without relying on imports. Or perhaps to be autonomous it was enough to have your own laws and customs. But if autonomy meant a city’s ability to command its own future, free of external interference – full sovereignty, in other words – it’s unclear that any Greek state, apart from Athens or Macedon, could be described as autonomous. Thucydides would have been aware of this tension.

As for democracy, does it denote anything more than internal self-governance? Assemblies met and voted, and some inscriptions even give the number of citizen voters, but were they voting on anything of substance? Was democracy – again never properly defined by Ma – merely a name to describe certain formalities (assembly, council, officials, law courts) rather than substantive realities? Ma’s argument that there was full democracy occasionally slips between later Hellenistic examples and the radical Athenian democracy of the fifth and fourth centuries. Sparta resolutely kept its oligarchic system. A rich crop of inscriptions shows us what poleis such as Xanthos, Miletus or Iasos wished to display, and these public documents surely masked the messy social and political manoeuvrings within the cities. In the Hellenistic period, *demokratia* could mean a democratic regime or simply freedom from external control.

The role of the elite also needs to be considered. There are inscriptions from the city of Iasos (in what is now south-west Turkey), for instance, thanking rich donors who gave willingly to the public grain fund, ‘wishing to increase the democracy as much as possible’ – a curious conjunction. There are many records of decisions to honour very rich citizens for making donations, taking office at their private expense or providing interest-free loans to the city in straitened periods. The chronological distribution and significance of this euergetism, with cities relying on wealthy benefactors, has been vigorously debated. Ma believes that euergetism proper is a feature only of the Roman Empire, and that the earlier role of the very rich is part of a balanced negotiation between elite and city to maintain civic values and the common good – certainly these virtues are much paraded in the inscriptions. Other historians see the outsized role of the wealthy as a powerful contradiction of democracy, or at least an insertion of a strong oligarchic element into a nominal democracy.

It was hard enough to maintain the balance between the elite and the masses in the radically democratic Athens of the fifth and fourth centuries. Rich Athenians paying polis-mandated contributions felt entitled to ask for favours in return, and did. Whatever institutions may have been in place in a polis, the social and political reality of extremely rich individuals making large donations will have accorded them extra powers, and might have allowed them to bypass the institutions entirely – but bland public thanks in formulaic inscriptions will not reveal this. To make an Aristotelian point, oligarchy is not so much the rule of the few as the rule of the wealthy. It is a remarkable fact that ‘democracy’ became the default description for a self-regulating polis community after the classical period, while oligarchy and tyranny were its undesirable opposites. In practice, however, democracy must have covered a wide spectrum of systems, some more democratic (in ancient or modern terms) than others.
