# 正文

The Roman Empire​ has left its mark on the West as a model of power, governance and society. Since its founding, the United States has styled itself as a republic, complete with Senate, checks and balances; modern European empires attempted infrastructure projects reminiscent of the ancient engineering works that carved straight roads and aqueducts through alien territories, and claimed a similar ‘civilising’ or even God-given mission. But Rome hasn’t only inspired emulation of its ordered governance and world domination. It has also offered examples of opposition (Spartacus, Jesus, Simon bar Kokhba, Attila the Hun), glimmerings of class conflict in the Struggle of the Orders of the early to mid-Republican period, and a long history of resistance fighters turned leaders of nations (including Vercingetorix, whose story was an inspiration for Asterix).

Kim Bowes’s *Surviving Rome* explores working people’s economic experiences and their approaches to surviving, and sometimes thriving, in the heyday of the empire. Sarah E. Bond’s *Strike* is an alternative history of Rome that foregrounds the various kinds of grassroots group that proliferated in the empire, some of which people joined willingly and others they were forced into. Bowes avoids writing of ‘the working class’, and even of ‘peasants’ – a description that is still common among premodern historians (including Bowes herself, the co-director of the Roman Peasant Project) – on the basis that it removes the agency of poor subjects: peasants are always there, fading into the landscape. The pejorative term ‘mob’, which does appear frequently in its Greek and Latin equivalents, is also out for obvious reasons. Bowes opts for the deliberately anachronistic ‘the 90 per cent’, a useful imprecision that is reminiscent of Occupy Wall Street without exactly mirroring it, and thus largely avoids its ideological baggage. Bond, meanwhile, refers to her subjects as ‘workers’, ‘regular Romans’ and ‘ordinary Romans’ to distinguish them from the society’s ‘elites’.

Literature, written almost exclusively by upper-class Roman men, only gets us so far. In rare cases, ordinary people are depicted with realism and even compassion. In Apuleius’ second-century ce novel, *The Golden Ass*, the young, upper-class Corinthian protagonist, Lucius, is magically transformed into an ass and describes his encounters with the humble inhabitants of the towns and villages of Roman Achaea and Thessaly, including a robber band, itinerant Syrian priests and enslaved mill workers. More often, ordinary people are only glimpsed; where they are foregrounded, they tend to be treated with disparagement, pity or pomposity. They were, however, made use of in literary works to represent types or values. Roman authors liked to find moral lessons in poverty (especially the picturesque rural kind) and even enslavement. Horace wrote a version of ‘The Town Mouse and the Country Mouse’ that captured a moral trend encouraging the upper classes to seek contentment with little. St Paul and Seneca the Younger, in different ways, considered slavery a universal condition – we could be slaves of God or slaves of our passions. But their recognition of slavery’s universality did not prompt calls for its abolition. Roman authors sometimes even ventriloquised the voices of ‘barbarians’ talking back to the Roman Empire, most memorably in the lines that Tacitus gives Calgacus in *Agricola* before he leads the Caledonians against the Roman army at the Battle of Mons Graupius in the late first century ce: ‘They make a wasteland and they call it peace.’

These literary portraits formed the backbone of social histories that focused on Roman ‘daily life’, an area that still provides light relief in Latin textbooks. But there are better ways of illuminating the lives of ordinary people in the Roman Empire than through literary texts written by the upper classes. The wide array of ancient source material now available and accessible includes ledger accounts and army logs, notes, letters and notarised documents; there are papyri preserved by the arid conditions of Egypt and, at the other extreme, wooden tablets that survived in the waterlogged, anaerobic conditions of the military fort of Vindolanda on Hadrian’s Wall. Recent archaeological initiatives carried out during the construction of Disneyland Paris, among other projects, have expanded our understanding of rural life in Roman northern Europe to an extraordinary degree.

Excavations from Pompeii and Herculaneum, which began in the 18th century, have unearthed everything from loaves of bread, fish bones and food served in ancient cookshops to shackles and sleeping quarters that doubled as places to store farm equipment, not to speak of the bodies of individuals and groups, along with whatever they were carrying. Our understanding of ordinary Roman lives has advanced significantly thanks to the analysis and interpretation of bodily remains. For example, we can now gauge Romans’ relative consumption of fish and meat by measuring isotope values for carbon and nitrogen in their bones. We can see the physical toll of manual labour on the bodies of imperial subjects, including children, and the terrible damage done by lifting and carrying, mining, scything and hoeing without the aid of machines. Such evidence enables us to tell new stories, ones based on decades or centuries of meticulous, unglamorous authentication, classification, decipherment and analysis.

As this material is synthesised, it can provide new answers to old questions. The once dominant thesis of Moses Finley’s *Ancient Economy*, published in 1973, is put to the test in *Surviving Rome*. Finley’s central argument was that the ancient Mediterranean economy was primitive – after all, the Greeks and Romans didn’t even have a word for it. The Greek noun *oikonomia*, from which ‘economy’ is derived, meant ‘household management’. In classical antiquity, being the head of a household might inspire meditations on leadership on a greater scale, but the term did not come near to signifying a field of scientific analysis. It is both a real merit and a considerable flaw of *The Ancient Economy* that Finley took the perspectives of ancient literary works so seriously. He saw in them a lack of interest in themes with which the modern field of economics had been preoccupied, such as scale, growth, risk and trade routes. Instead, they were concerned with status, hierarchy and prestige. The trouble is that this version of the ancient world was based on the view of upper-class individuals far removed from the nuts and bolts of business. Insulated by inherited wealth and large estates, they were able alternately to romanticise those who worked for a living and to indulge their prejudices against them. Following the testimony of ancient authors, Finley relegated small farmers (the most common occupation in the ancient world) to a ‘subsistence’ existence, focused on survival and incompatible with anything approaching complex decision-making or strategic thinking.

Scholars of the ancient economy are today reasonably confident when talking about scale (even when they disagree), and more generally in using the language of modern economics to discuss the ancient world. The downside of this is that the lives and experiences of inhabitants of the Roman Empire and the ways in which they made sense of their world can get lost. The greater availability and increasingly sophisticated analysis of a broader range of ancient evidence, coupled with recent revolutions in the economic questions that are being asked of the modern world, seem to invite promising new approaches, however. The winners of the 2015 and 2019 Nobel Prizes in economics, for example, focus on people rather than abstractions, and take seriously as a subject of inquiry the sophisticated strategies used by ordinary people, including the very poor, to get by.

Bowes reconstructs the strategies used by working people in the Roman world. The sources here are fragmentary, but the vignettes that open each chapter succeed in grounding her analysis in storytelling. An Italian inn sign encouraged visitors with a picture and a racy text – a joke about the relative costs of wine, food, a sex worker and hay for the mule. A couple, accompanied by their slave, took a trip to Pompeii and jotted down on a wall the amount they spent each day on food and other small purchases. The ‘archive’ of Soterichos, an Egyptian smallholder who died at the end of the first century ce, shows the fluctuations of the fortune he amassed and then lost. A Pompeian graffito jokes that its target needs only to add ‘oral sex provider’ to complete the list of all the jobs he has held – people in the towns of the Roman Empire often depended on several different jobs. Artemidorus’ interpretations of dreams in the second century ce reveal the preoccupation of ordinary people with money, and the connection he draws between loans, debts and death suggests the existential nature of this anxiety. The body of a young mother who died in the eruption of Vesuvius has injuries on her back and shoulders, presumably because she had to carry heavy loads.

Once we cease to be fixated on modern economists’ criteria for ‘advanced’ economic behaviour, such as double-entry bookkeeping, we can begin to appreciate the Romans’ distinctive relationship with money. There is abundant evidence of habits and systems that closely calculated and tracked money. Finger-counting systems, illustrated in ancient reliefs and paintings, enabled transparent transactions and complex arithmetic. The table abacus could be set on any flat surface or could be improvised with pebbles or other small objects as counters. Ordinary people in the Roman Empire rarely tracked incoming and outgoing amounts in the same place, but they did assiduously record them, scribbling numbers on scraps of papyri, potsherds, wood tablets and walls, or in more formal documents that were written and notarised by the village scribe.

Some of this care was a result of living under the watchful eye of the imperial authorities, and a consequent nervousness about proving that taxes had been paid. For many who lived in the Roman Empire, interaction with Roman army units and officers, whether experienced as peacekeeping or as a hostile occupation, also led to assiduous accounting practices. The records of Roman army bases, particularly well preserved at Vindolanda and in Egypt, suggest that the army was almost as keen to tally and reckon as it was to threaten and subdue. Other forces, too, encouraged the habit of financial record-keeping: everything happened under the watchful eyes of the gods. The remarkable curse tablets recovered from Sulis Minerva’s sacred spring in Roman Bath beg for her aid in recovering money by blinding offenders or driving them insane.

Bowes’s Romans bear little resemblance either to the ascetic figures evoked in moralising Roman texts or those conjured up by mainstream economic historians, eking out an existence on ‘subsistence’ rations. Roman labourers needed a large amount of food just to keep going. Poorer inhabitants of the empire might depend more heavily on the grain doled out to citizens, and the truly destitute on soup kitchens, like the Jewish one in Galilee that Bowes discusses. Less fortunate working people were paid mainly in food; this was the case for both free and enslaved miners in the quarries of Mons Claudianus in the Eastern Desert of Egypt. People tried to add to the basic diet: the Egyptian miners probably ate very little meat or fish but supplemented their rations with vegetables grown in the quarry gardens. Those with a little more money to spend occasionally splurged on food, while the range of goods available suggests a thriving global economy. Smaller and less perishable goods tend to survive: dishes and glasses, jewellery, toys, counters and dice. The soldiers stationed at Vindolanda loved bacon, as we know from accounts logged on the surviving wooden tablets, while the couple who itemised their expenses on their trip to Pompeii splashed out on a fancy dinner with sausages and steak, but otherwise ate bread, cheese and leeks (along with wine and oil). ‘Bread for slave’ was separately itemised. They also bought incense, a cooking pot and a lamp.

Clothes were an important and expensive part of people’s lives, and were more likely to be bought than homespun. Cato the Elder’s second-century bce treatise *On Agriculture* recommends that slaves be provided with a tunic, a cloak and a pair of clogs every other year, while their masters should be sure to take the old clothes away to make patchwork out of them. For those who had more choice, clothes were an obvious measure of status, and the desire for many sets of clothing in the finest possible materials is attested by dowry agreements and wills, as well as by the thriving markets for new and second-hand clothes and the surprisingly brightly coloured and patterned socks, hats, tunics and cloaks preserved in the drier parts of ancient Judaea and Egypt.

Adding in taxes (lower than might be expected), rent (on the high side), services and amenities (a trip to the baths could cost a small fortune), it was expensive to maintain a decent lifestyle, and ordinary Romans typically managed it by juggling resources, making the most of what the land produced and holding multiple jobs. Farmers like Soterichos, the Egyptian smallholder, might grow crops and keep animals on several small plots, dodging and playing off their landlords, boosting their yields by rotating crops and keeping fields manured, and in a good year selling wine, meat or fruit at the local market. The livelihood of farming families was precarious and any men, women and children of the household who were able to earn helped supplement it; sources of income probably included manual labour on neighbouring farms or construction sites, spinning (always in demand, with all those clothes and blankets to be made) and perhaps sex work. For children, there might be an apprenticeship that covered their keep while they learned a trade such as weaving.

Bowes evokes an unromanticised but recognisable world of hustling, risk and exploitation. None of her subjects is reduced to a type, and we aren’t invited to trace any broad narrative of change over time. There are occasional glimpses of different values and perspectives, such as Shabbat restrictions or the Jewish charity running a soup kitchen, but many things are constant, such as the desire to improve one’s home – evident in country-dwellers as well as in those living in towns. Although the empire’s interest in counting and monitoring people and their property was occasionally refracted in ordinary people’s concern with their own accounts, we don’t really see Bowes’s subjects actively participating in politics, even in well-documented initiatives such as petitioning Roman officials or advocating for the election of local magistrates.

Sarah Bond’s focus​ also skews towards the ordinary end of Roman society, but the scope of her book is different: this is a history of Rome, beginning with the Twelve Tables of the mid-fifth century bce, a set of laws that established legal procedures for all Roman citizens, and ending with a foiled plot by the silversmiths to kill the emperor Justinian, restorer of the western empire. This history is told in chronological order and often through familiar episodes, with glances at the wars of conquest, the political development of the Roman state and its statesmen. Her emphasis, however, is on the interlinked themes of collective organisation, resistance and strikes.

Within this story, several kinds of non-state organisation play an important role. One is the voluntary associations that were often called *collegia* in Latin, but which can be referred to by a number of other Latin and Greek terms. Another is the *familiae*, a term that can designate a household or a group of enslaved people brought together as a labour force, especially as farm and construction workers, shepherds, gladiators and miners; this is the sense in which Bond uses *familiae*.

*Collegia* and similar voluntary groups often emphasised their common worship of a particular god, place or occupation. They could have officials, a dedicated meeting place, a membership roll and by-laws. Membership cut across social groups, and included ordinary working people, freed people and sometimes slaves, with prominent citizens or occasionally even members of the imperial family serving as patrons. Such groups allowed members to benefit from opportunities normally enjoyed only by the richest in a community, ranging from sharing banquets and honouring rulers in monuments and inscriptions to reserving blocks of theatre seats and exclusive burial plots. It is unclear whether to translate *collegium* as voluntary association, club, congregation, assembly, cult or guild. On occasion, we see occupation-based associations engaging in protest, collective action and bargaining: this is one of the main reasons for Bond’s description of them as unions. The most famous example of such behaviour is the Ephesian silversmiths’ riot related in *Acts*, after Paul’s condemnation of idolatry threatened their trade in small shrines to Artemis and souvenirs for pilgrims, and the goddess herself.

While *collegia* and *familiae* loom large in Bond’s account, she also includes instances of collective action in which these groups were not involved. Like Bowes, she tries not to be constrained by the institutional, legal and social categories that have framed more traditional socioeconomic history. The Struggle of the Orders, a series of conflicts between patricians and plebeians between the fifth and third centuries bce, resulted in increased protections and power for plebeians. Our main sources for these conflicts are much later literary accounts that overlaid hazy memories of early Rome with more recent experience of popular politics in the second to first centuries bce. These accounts describe patrician abuses of power, including debt bondage and rape, against which the plebeians protested by withholding military service and occupying the hills of Rome. The basic details of the Struggle of the Orders are murky: even the Romans weren’t sure what had originally distinguished patricians from plebeians, but that distinction was not simply class-based. By the later Republic, when these literary accounts were written, certain religious and political offices were still reserved for patricians or plebeians, a distinction conferred by birth, but many plebeians held the highest offices of state, including Gaius Marius, Cicero and Pompey. Additionally (and confusingly for us), the terms ‘plebs’ and ‘plebeian’ were also regularly used during the later Republic to refer to ordinary people. This class-based usage may have influenced Roman histories of the Struggle of the Orders.

Bond’s second episode is the revolt in 73-71 bce led by Spartacus. Sources for his life are not abundant, but it is known that he was an enslaved Thracian gladiator who had served as a soldier in Rome’s non-citizen troops (where barbarians’ toughness was sought after) and had, therefore, been trained to kill in two different but quintessentially Roman settings. He is thought to have mobilised around a hundred thousand men, starting with *familiae* of enslaved gladiators and augmented by farm workers and herdsmen. They overran Italy, and had ambitions to cross into Sicily (the site of two major slave wars in the later second century bce), until they were finally overcome and the survivors crucified.

Bond describes the heightened political polarisation of the late Republic, and the orchestration of popular sentiment, violence and even elements of class warfare, as illustrated by the prolific writings of Cicero. One of his opponents was Publius Clodius Pulcher, who was born a patrician (Clodius was a deliberately ‘popular’ spelling of the patrician family name Claudius), but had himself adopted into a plebeian family so that he was eligible to be elected a tribune of the people for 58 bce. Amid increasing senatorial suspicion of the potential power and seditious intent of voluntary associations, Clodius reorganised and mobilised the *collegia*, focusing especially on shopkeepers, freedmen and slaves, and used *familiae* of gladiators to intimidate his opponents. He forced Cicero into exile on the basis that, as consul in 63, Cicero had ordered citizens to be executed without trial after the Catiline conspiracy. Clodius also radically expanded the grain dole. After he was murdered by Milo in January 52, he was given a suitably populist send-off, with his body set alight in the senate house. His populism was idiosyncratic, but Bond emphasises its connections to earlier examples of collective action and organisation. Most immediately, the history of upper-class tribunes of the people mobilising and orchestrating the popular will goes back to the Gracchi brothers in the later 2nd century bce; she also suggests Clodius may have been inspired by examples such as the revolt of Spartacus.

The first and second centuries ce saw periodic bouts of unrest. In 59 ce there were riots between spectators from Nuceria and locals in the amphitheatre of Pompeii, while in Alexandria the Jewish community was targeted during the reigns of Caligula and Claudius. Across the empire youth groups were often associated with trouble. It isn’t always clear how far associations were the instigators, but a lingering suspicion of organised groups that might include freed and even enslaved people led to regular clampdowns. These sometimes took the form of intricately drafted legislation that attempted to distinguish between useful and disruptive associations, healthy and seditious behaviour. At other times there was violent suppression, and sometimes outright bans. Just as often, there is evidence of voluntary associations advancing the interests of their members. In the Flavian era, demolition workers and Pergamene doctors sought permissions and exceptions for their associations, while the emperor Hadrian received a petition from an Ephesian wrestler who wanted to build a clubhouse. The perceived effectiveness of associations in fostering a sense of belonging and prestige encouraged their proliferation during the ‘high’ empire of the first to third centuries ce: in Pompeii alone, there were 45 associations at the time of the eruption of Vesuvius.

Groups had to carry out a delicate balancing act to improve their lot with the state without being perceived to threaten the social order. By the time of the later Roman Empire, between the third and sixth centuries, the increasingly centralised state adopted a pragmatic approach, sponsoring and patronising associations in order to meet the need for an extraordinary volume of supplies, especially food (the dole now included pork and olive oil along with bread), coins and clothing and equipment for the army.

Bond’s​ book proposes a direct connection between the ancient and modern worlds, a continuous history of collective action. She rejects the hostile commentary of ancient elite writers and much traditional scholarship on mobs and gangs of working people, sectarian religious riots and the hooliganism of chariot-racing fans. One of the paradoxes of *Strike* is that its scope is so capacious that we don’t hear very much from the members of these groups themselves. This is partly a consequence of the pattern of the surviving evidence. Inscriptions put up by associations and individual participants tell us a bit about structures, membership and dedications, but the evidence is rarely like for like and isn’t all that helpful in countering the dominant narrative of cult intrigue and gang violence.

There can be no doubt however that Rome’s cosmopolitanism and the ambivalent stance on immigration that went along with it are in evidence from its earliest history, through trade in goods, craftsmanship and slaves. The prominent place of artists and performers in the Roman world attests to its particular combination of patronage and cultural capital. The Roman authorities’ periodic paranoia about associations says much about the precariousness of the social order and the dynamics of power, especially during the trumpeted peace of the early empire. Ordinary people might be stirred to action by violence at the games, by the utter cruelty of slavers, or by perceived threats to the gods and the social order – the early Christians, too, organised themselves in voluntary associations, which they often referred to as *ekklēsiai*, or ‘assemblies’.

Bond takes considerable care to highlight differences, not least when comparing *collegia* to modern labour unions. Associations of performers regularly had monarchs and other political leaders as patrons. Delos, the tax-free commercial centre of the Aegean under Roman rule, was notable for its voluntary associations of traders, whose activities were closer to those of a chamber of commerce than a trade union. The best parts of the book hint at intriguing micro-histories. The Christian communities of fifth-century ce Egypt are especially fertile ground. Associations of funeral workers and stretcher-bearers were mobilised by Cyril, the patriarch of Alexandria, in violent religious feuds. We are offered a glimpse of the thriving businesses in the pilgrimage town of Nitria in the Nile Delta (possibly named for its production of natron, a key component in preserving mummies), whose basilica and icon painters graffitied their names as they worked. A people’s history is at its most compelling when it brings us as close as possible to these lives and voices.
