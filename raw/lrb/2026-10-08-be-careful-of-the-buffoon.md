# 正文

Democracies​ aren’t doing well. The Brexit referendum, Donald Trump’s electoral victories and the rise of far-right parties across Europe have created a sense of democratic disarray. Yet, publicly at least, no one has dared to call into question universal suffrage as the source of political legitimacy. Imagine an electoral system in which the franchise would require a university degree. Nearly half of adults would still be able to vote, but according to available estimates, Remain would have won close to 70 per cent of the vote in 2016. Hillary Clinton and Kamala Harris would have received nearly 60 per cent of the popular vote in 2016 and 2024, making victory in the electoral college all but certain. The Rassemblement National wouldn’t be the favourite to win the French presidential election next year. Perhaps an academic voting restriction would resolve the West’s political crisis.

This proposal sounds absurd because an unrestricted franchise is now seen as a prerequisite of democracy. But it is worth considering how we came to view universal suffrage as a potent, almost irresistible, source of legitimacy. The great architects of representative government were scathing about the evils of direct mass suffrage. Alexander Hamilton advocated indirect suffrage as a check on ‘the little arts of popularity’. Edmund Burke felt revulsion for the rule of the ‘swinish multitude’. In France, the great advocate of a restricted franchise was François Guizot, a historian of England at the Sorbonne, who was the nation’s leading minister from 1840 until the fall of the liberal July Monarchy in 1848. As late as 1847, Guizot dismissed the clamour for a broader franchise as ‘absurd’: ‘The day of universal suffrage will never dawn. There will never be a time when all human beings, whoever they are, will be called on to exercise political rights.’

Eleven months later, Parisian insurgents overthrew Guizot and the July Monarchy. A provisional republican government almost immediately granted the right to vote to all Frenchmen aged 21 or over. The socialist and feminist Jeanne Deroin coined the phrase ‘male universal suffrage’ to highlight the fact that women were still excluded. She could have added that indigenous Jews and Muslims in Algeria were also barred from voting, although French nationals. But free men of colour and, after the abolition of slavery on 27 April 1848, former male slaves, were granted the right to vote in other French colonies, from Martinique to Pondicherry.

While the provisional government of the Second Republic pioneered electoral enfranchisement on an awesome scale, the First Republic of 1792 until 1804 had used indirect elections and a moderate franchise to limit the power of voters; very low turnouts had further rendered electoral politics meaningless. The 1867 Reform Act was labelled ‘a leap in the dark’ because it increased the number of eligible voters in Britain from one to two million, out of seven million adult men. The Second Republic had increased the number of eligible voters from 250,000 to ten million.

Rejecting the US model, the Second Republic also provided for the direct election of a president. On 10 and 11 December 1848, France held its first presidential election. The turnout was high (75 per cent) and the result stunning. Louis Bonaparte, nephew of Napoleon, won 74 per cent of the vote. The runner-up, General Eugène Cavaignac, a moderate republican, polled only 19 per cent, and Alexandre Ledru-Rollin, a democrat-socialist lawyer who came third, only 5 per cent. The newly enfranchised masses elected a 40-year-old adventurer who had left France aged seven and spoke French with a Swiss-German accent. His main claim to fame was his last name. But few will regret the defeat of Cavaignac, who oversaw gruesome atrocities during the conquest of Algeria, and whose own fame relied on the savage repression of a Paris workers’ insurrection in June 1848.

In *The Class Struggles in France* (1850), Marx described this election result as ‘a world-historic piece of buffoonery and an indecipherable hieroglyphic for the understanding of the civilised’, because it revealed the idiotic preferences of the numerically dominant peasantry, ‘the class that represents barbarism within civilisation’. Soon, the new president came into conflict with a Legislative Assembly dominated by conservative royalists. On 2 December 1851, he resolved the deadlock by carrying out a coup and adopting a new constitution that gave him extensive powers. A year later, he was proclaimed emperor, under the name Napoleon III. (The putative Napoleon II was his cousin, Napoleon’s legitimate son, who died in 1832.) Louis Bonaparte’s coup purported to be democratic, since its official justification was the restoration of universal suffrage, after the royalist Assembly had passed a law to reduce the number of eligible voters by one third. The coup and imperial restoration were both sanctioned by plebiscites, in which 92 per cent and then 97 per cent of the votes were in favour, on turnouts on both occasions of 82 per cent.

Napoleon III reconciled conservatism with capitalism and democracy. His regime’s skilful management of public opinion demonstrated how conservatives could reap the benefits of mass politics, inspiring Disraeli in Britain and Bismarck in Germany. Its relentless pursuit of economic modernisation, through easier access to credit and investment in infrastructure, made it a model for other countries seeking to catch up with Britain’s economic achievements. France’s second Napoleonic emperor also succeeded in upending the Vienna settlement of 1815, bringing about Italy’s unification and, unwittingly, Germany’s. (French victory against Austria in 1859 helped the king of Piedmont-Sardinia, Victor Emmanuel II, become king of Italy in 1861, while French defeat against a Prussian-led German coalition in 1870 paved the way for the king of Prussia, Wilhelm I, to become emperor of Germany in 1871.) And Napoleon III helped usher in modern globalisation. In partnership with Britain, the Second Empire opened new markets for European capitalists in the Middle East and East Asia, while free trade policies and the building of the Suez Canal accelerated global economic integration.

Nevertheless, Louis Bonaparte was and remains widely perceived as a buffoon. The liberal statesman Adolphe Thiers allegedly dismissed him as ‘a simpleton we can lead by the nose’. The republican poet Victor Hugo dubbed him ‘Napoleon the Little’. His rise to power inspired Marx’s mordant line in *The Eighteenth Brumaire of Louis Bonaparte* (1852) about history repeating itself as farce. Before his election, Louis had made two attempts to seize power by force, at Strasbourg in 1836 and Boulogne in 1840; both ended in embarrassment. Imprisoned at the Ham fortress after his second attempt, he escaped in 1846 disguised as a mason and using the false name Badinguet: the clunky, working-class name became a humiliating sobriquet used by his well-bred opponents. He was estranged from his own father, who had served as king of Holland during the First French Empire and who found his son ridiculous. Napoleon III’s own wife, the Spanish-born Empress Eugénie, on learning of his capture by the German army at the Battle of Sedan in 1870, wished he had got himself killed – a more honourable end that might have enabled their son to succeed him and prevented the proclamation of a new republic. Only his mother, Hortense, the daughter of Napoleon’s first wife, Joséphine de Beauharnais, loved him. But perhaps it was because her two older sons had died young, one in infancy and the other fighting for Italian independence in 1831.

Edward Shawcross’s new biography is in many ways an attempt to rescue the reputation of a man he calls the ‘people’s emperor’. It reappraises Napoleon III’s many achievements, yet even Shawcross can’t resist sarcasm when he writes of Louis’s ‘keen perspicacity’. The book gives a detailed description of the second putsch attempt – involving the purchase of a tame vulture in Gravesend, before a band of sixty inebriated Bonapartists crossed the Channel and were swiftly apprehended near Boulogne – which makes it obvious that the enterprise was ‘beyond farcical’. But all this only thickens the mystery. How could such a character become France’s longest-serving leader in the modern era? He was head of state for nearly 22 years, longer than either his uncle or Charles de Gaulle.

Louis’s political beliefs were confused and his regime’s policies confusing. In his youth he dabbled in socialist ideas – of the Saint-Simonian, technocratic sort – but once in power he promoted an aggressive capitalism that turned the Paris Bourse into a serious rival to the London Stock Exchange. He proclaimed that ‘empire means peace’ before launching wars against Russia, Austria, China and Prussia and an invasion of Mexico that challenged US supremacy in North America. Despite these bellicose policies, he was profoundly shocked by the horrors of war. Although a notorious unbeliever and a supporter of Italian unification, he prevented Italy from annexing Rome and abolishing the pope’s temporal power until 1870. The 1851 coup and the governing methods of the Second Empire pioneered aspects of modern authoritarianism, but to the dismay of his own supporters he also embarked on the liberal reforms that transformed his regime into Europe’s first parliamentary democracy.

Hegelians will admire the encapsulation of the era’s contradictions in a single individual. Most of us will be puzzled by his fickleness. Louis Bonaparte’s personal life reinforces the sense of his shallow inconsistency. He was a philanderer, but one who neither felt nor elicited great passion. One of his conquests remarked on the swiftness of the rendezvous: ‘It had taken only half an hour to make me an empress.’ He cared a lot about his appearance. Short-legged, he looked more elegant on horseback, and despite suffering from haemorrhoids and later from a bladder stone that rendered riding excruciating, he appeared that way as often as possible. It was in order to be able to ride again, and to attempt an improbable return to power, that in 1873 he risked the dangerous operation to remove the stone from his bladder which would kill him.

Republican historians​ damned Louis Bonaparte as the assassin of the Second Republic and royalist historians dismissed him as a vulgar usurper. Bonapartism was never an intellectual’s creed and I can’t think of a single Bonapartist historian, though Gaullist statesmen and historians sought to rehabilitate him in the 20th century, highlighting his concern with national unity and economic modernisation. Shawcross praises Louis’s realisation that ‘the art of politics lay in anticipating what was to come,’ not least the need to tame mass democracy. This turns on its head Marx’s jibe that Napoleonic restoration demonstrated the hold of the past on the political imagination. Shawcross admits that the Second Empire could be described as an ‘illiberal democracy’. But the operative word for him is ‘democracy’, and he stresses that it became more liberal over time.

Shawcross’s reappraisal doesn’t extend to foreign policy: the strange attempt to turn the Mexican republic into a monarchy and a French client state ended in humiliating disaster (his previous book, *The Last Emperor of Mexico*, dealt with this affair), while France’s ill-fated declaration of war on Prussia resulted in swift defeat and the regime’s collapse in 1870. There is also something distinctly English about his sympathetic treatment. Contemporary observers such as Bagehot and historians such as A.J.P. Taylor have always expressed greater understanding than French intellectuals of the supposed need to suspend liberty among unruly peoples such as the French. (Bagehot, before taking over as editor of the *Economist*, defended Louis’s coup on the grounds that the ‘excitable’ French ‘national character’ did not constitute ‘a fit basis for national freedom’.) The Second Empire was an unmitigated blessing for Britain. Not only did the France of Napoleon III prove a valuable ally against Russia, China and other powers resisting Western expansion, but hyped-up fears of a French invasion justified increases in naval expenditure that fuelled British overseas dominance. Napoleon III was the first and perhaps only French leader to speak perfect, if accented, English. It is fitting that his remains should still be in England (at Farnborough in Hampshire) and that his only son, Napoleon-Eugène, died fighting for the British Empire during the Anglo-Zulu War of 1879.

The demand of the far-right Rassemblement National that Louis’s remains be returned to France – at the Reform party conference last month Nigel Farage declared himself ready to accede to this request should he become prime minister – therefore seems bizarre. It cannot be grounded in memories of national greatness. Rather, it probably seeks to convey the message that when faced with the danger of socialist revolution – today in the guise of Jean-Luc Mélenchon’s La France Insoumise – it is acceptable for the centrist bourgeoisie to suspend liberal politics.

Shawcross is right that comparisons of the Second Empire with the totalitarian regimes of the 20th century are nonsensical. By the standards of 19th-century French politics, Louis’s rule was not particularly violent. Repression after the 1851 coup killed around five hundred (though ten thousand more were deported to Algeria and Guiana). The Terror of 1793-94, by contrast, killed between thirty and forty thousand, the repression of the Parisian workers’ insurrection in June 1848 killed three thousand and the crushing of the Paris Commune in May 1871 killed up to ten thousand people. And as the historian Sudhir Hazareesingh pointed out, it was under Napoleon III that Frenchmen learned to become citizens, making the Second Empire the regime that established French ‘modern democracy’. Despite its reliance on the Catholic Church to command legitimacy in the countryside, the Second Empire stands out for its benevolence towards religious minorities, not least Jews, who became accepted in high society, not only as financiers, but also as statesmen, scholars and artists: Jacques Offenbach’s Parisian operetta made him a global celebrity; Isaac Strauss (my great-great-great-great-grandfather) served as master of ceremonies for balls at the imperial court between 1854 and 1869.

Yet the Second Empire did not turn Frenchwomen into citizens. If anything, the era saw an increase in political and civil inequality between the sexes. On 23 June 1851, a Legislative Assembly bitterly divided between socialists, republicans, Bonapartists and royalists unanimously abrogated the right of women to submit petitions to parliament. Raymond-Théodore Troplong, the main drafter of the imperial constitution of 1852 and president of the Senate from 1852 until his death in 1869, was a specialist in matrimonial law. His treatise, *Du contrat de mariage et des droits respectifs des époux* upheld ‘the husband’s superiority’ in all civil matters: ‘Such is the nature of good democratic laws.’ Second Empire fashion marked the peak of a clothing style designed to constrain the bodies of aristocratic and bourgeois women, with tight corsets and crinolines that made it difficult for them to breathe or move.

Misogyny was not solely the preserve of conservatives. In 1858, the anarchist-socialist Pierre-Joseph Proudhon defended women’s subordination as a condition of political and social progress: ‘Unproductive by nature, inert, without industry or understanding, without justice or modesty, [the woman] needs a father, a brother, a lover, a husband, a master, a man, in short, to give her, if I may put it that way, the magnetism that makes her capable of masculine virtues, of social and intellectual faculties.’ Universal male suffrage was facilitated by the almost total exclusion of women from the political sphere, just as in the early 19th century the lowering of the franchise in the US had come with new restrictions for free Blacks. These exclusions proved long lasting: Frenchwomen obtained the right to vote only in 1944; African Americans saw theirs strictly enforced only after the passage of the Voting Rights Act of 1965. In a report arguing against granting women the right to vote in 1919, the republican senator Alexandre Bérard noted that ‘women’s suffrage would be a tremendous leap into the unknown. It could lead to the election of a new Bonaparte, just as universal suffrage had in 1848, and subsequently bring about another Sedan’ – the battle during the Franco-Prussian War that led to Louis’s fall.

Besides his last name, Louis’s great asset was his reluctance to speak. Zola noted his ‘eternal silence’ in public. When he spoke, he rarely finished his sentences. Some people, including Queen Victoria, appreciated his British-style reserve: ‘The emperor is so very quiet, his voice low, and soft.’ But Zola and others saw it as typical of a man without strong convictions, who preferred plotting in secret to serious and honest governing. Bismarck is said to have quipped that he was ‘a sphinx without a riddle’.

If Louis Bonaparte had one fervent belief, it was in the virtue of male universal suffrage. Shawcross points out that as early as 1834, Louis wrote to his mother: ‘As long as universal suffrage is not one of the fundamental laws of state, national representation will only be the representation of particular interests.’ Obtaining the support of a mass electorate was for him a goal in itself. The belated transformation of his regime into a parliamentary system reflected his admiration of British constitutionalism, but also his desire to win another plebiscite approving his reforms; he duly did this, on 8 May 1870, with 83 per cent voting in support on a turnout of 82 per cent. After his regime’s collapse and six months of captivity at Schloss Wilhelmshöhe in Kassel, Louis went into exile in Chislehurst in Kent. In interviews with British newspapers, he asserted that he remained ‘the legitimate sovereign of France’, until ‘by a direct vote, the entire nation will have chosen a definitive government.’

The Louis Bonaparte experiment placed the French left in a predicament that has resurfaced in recent years. How can it claim to represent the people against the privileged few when a significant majority of voters reject its parties in the polls, and when its own voters tend to be better off and better educated than the average citizen? Faced with this dilemma in the mid-19th century, the French left split. A minority reasserted the primacy of revolutionary politics. Yet the crushing of the Paris Commune by a recently elected National Assembly confirmed the irresistible attraction of universal suffrage. Most republicans learned instead to speak to the concerns of the rural majority and won decisive electoral victories in the late 1870s. The price was the establishment of a very conservative kind of republic. Germany and Britain laid the foundations of a welfare state and expanded women’s rights well before the French Third Republic.

The left of 1848 discovered the peasants. Today’s left is confronted by a not entirely dissimilar ‘barbarism within civilisation’: a very large share of the citizenry that is unhappy with progressive politics, and out of anger and hope votes for nationalistic buffoons. But the election of Louis Bonaparte demonstrates that we despise buffoons at our peril. They play a useful role, because they reveal our blind spots and the limits of generous idealism. From Marx to Bismarck, the smartest analysts of mid-19th-century politics learned a lot from studying Napoleon III. We need to take buffoons seriously and understand their methods and their appeal. In French, a *bouffon* is also a king’s fool. We should be wary of mocking the fool, because he might end up having the last word: ‘I am a fool, thou art nothing.’
