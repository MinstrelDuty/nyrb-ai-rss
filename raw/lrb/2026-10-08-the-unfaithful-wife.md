# 正文

How can you​ be a serial killer if you have only one person to kill? How can two films be so different when they sound as if they ought to be exactly the same? These are questions prompted by Claude Chabrol’s films. There are plenty of other questions too, and this is a good time to ask them: *The Unfaithful Wife* (1969) is back in cinemas in the UK and there’s currently a Chabrol season at the BFI.

Chabrol (1930-2010) has long been billed as the French Hitchcock, and this is not a bad introduction. Peter Bradshaw subtly suggests that Chabrol is closer to Buñuel, and Chabrol himself, in a 1977 interview, evoked Fritz Lang: ‘It was *Mabuse*, which I saw early on at a ciné-club, that made me want to direct in the first place. That’s true, not just sentiment … I love that mode of reflection about the world that Lang offers us in his films.’ This affection inspired his remake of *Mabuse* in 1990. There is also an important story about Chabrol’s work, I think, in the list of authors whose works he has turned into movies: Patricia Highsmith, Ed McBain, Ellery Queen, Georges Simenon, Ruth Rendell. All classics of a certain kind.

In another interview Chabrol suggests that ‘real writers’ – real crime writers, that is – ‘are interested in the behaviour of those who kill others. That is not the case with the authors of noir, who are content to tell the stories of murders.’ We could hardly arrive more tidily at *The Unfaithful Wife*. The writer in this case is Chabrol himself*.*

The film opens in the garden of a large house in the countryside, not far from Paris. Charles Desvallées (Michel Bouquet) and his wife, Hélène (Stéphane Audran), live here with their young son. A visit from Charles’s mother is coming to an end. When she forgets her car keys, Charles runs into the house to get them and surprises Hélène on a phone call she would rather keep private. He smiles slyly, as if registering a truth he has been entertaining for a while. No one says anything more about this. Charles goes to Paris for work (he’s an insurance broker) and Hélène is never where she’s supposed to be when he calls on her. He keeps trying, with something short of success, to look as if he is unbothered. That’s how we know how bothered he is. We get a sense of his sceptical style when a colleague tells him some paintings they were interested in are fakes. Charles smiles and says: ‘Sure, the certificates were real but the paintings were false.’

After a few more days of ordinary life, including a trip to a nightclub where several people get drunk and Charles continues to fail to look cool, he decides to take action. He hires a private detective to find out where Hélène has been going. The detective returns with a sheet of information: the man’s name, his address and phone number. Somewhat to our surprise (to my surprise anyway), Charles decides to visit the man, who’s called Victor Pegala (Maurice Ronet). Charles is very amiable to Victor, who tells him about his life, his divorce and his two children. Only when Victor shows Charles around the apartment, and the bedroom of assumed misdemeanours comes into view, does Charles begin to look as if he is suffering. Within minutes he has hit Victor with a heavy object and killed him. What follows, in great detail, is Charles’s long task of cleaning up: blood, broken furniture, wrapping the corpse in a sheet and carrying it to his car. There’s a snag on the road when a car behind him crashes into his boot and makes it unopenable. The suspense is just a distraction, as is the brilliant moment when the corpse, thrown into a lake, briefly refuses to sink. The moment passes; the body is gone.

The action of the rest of the film involves two policemen who come to interrogate Charles and Hélène. The suspects lie rather unconvincingly, but the policemen seem satisfied – and satisfied again when they question them a second time. What will Charles do with his freedom, and has Hélène worked out what happened? We don’t know because the film ends with the policemen on their way to paying the couple a third visit. New information?

The acting, in this and most of Chabrol’s films, is amazing. Audran (Chabrol’s wife) is elegant and aloof. Bouquet manages to seem smug and scared at the same time. The performance is important because the jealous man in the movie we are about to look at is so different. An effect of the writing and the story, of course, but also, significantly, of François Cluzet’s acting.

The backstory is interesting. In 1964 Henri-Georges Clouzot started to make a film called *L’Enfer* or *Inferno* when he and his main actor, Serge Reggiani, became ill and couldn’t continue. Some time later Chabrol picked up Clouzot’s screenplay, adapted it and directed his own version of the film. It was released in 1994. Paul and Nelly (Cluzet and Emmanuelle Béart) are the owners of a hotel in the French countryside, and the opening of the film shows their wedding and a life that seems to be a kind of paradise. But not for long. Paul can’t live without Nelly. They keep quarrelling and then forgiving each other. The forgiving is harder each time, and it’s clear that Paul can’t live without his jealousy either. It defines him. Eventually it hardens into a genuine, extreme paranoia. He believes – or needs to believe – that Nelly’s betrayals are more or less infinite and accuses her of sleeping with every male guest at the hotel.

Is there truth in any of this? Sort of. Nelly has had at least one sexual encounter with another man, but she seems to regard it as entertainment rather than betrayal, and her devotion to Paul doesn’t waver until his violent moments late in the film. Béart in this part displays a lightness of spirit which is almost credible. What’s fascinating about the movie is the combination of Paul’s craziness and his magical belief in his own sanity. He sees himself as the victim of his wife’s recklessness and he never for a moment thinks he himself has done anything wrong. I’m dying of all this, he says, ‘j’en crève’; but it, whatever it is, is also what he lives for. His last words (‘What is happening to me? What have I done? Let’s see … but we’re still here’) seem loyal to the styles both of Clouzot and Chabrol. An answer to the questions I began with might be to watch more good films, let ourselves be surprised by their reflections on the world, and perhaps unwind our expectations about those who kill others. *L’Enfer*, like many films, closes with the word ‘end’. But there is another word before it: ‘Without’. *Sans fin*.
