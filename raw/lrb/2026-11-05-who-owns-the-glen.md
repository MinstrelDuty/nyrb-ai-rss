# 正文

In​ March 1973, before taking his new play, *The Cheviot, the Stag and the Black, Black Oil*, around the village halls of the Highlands, John McGrath accepted a last-minute invitation to perform it at a leftist conference in Edinburgh. This was tricky: he was still writing on the morning of the performance. Bill Paterson recalls McGrath handing out notes to the cast as they stepped on stage. A fiddler took up the tune as the lights dimmed in the lecture theatre and a few of the company led the singing of a sentimental ballad:

> For these are my mountains  
> And this is my glen  
> The braes of my childhood  
> Will know me again.

A huge pop-up book designed by John Byrne was opened at its first page, showing a mountain scene. ‘We’re going to be telling a story,’ Paterson told the crowd. ‘It’s a story of what’s been happening here. In the Highlands. It’s a story that has a beginning, a middle, but, as yet, no end.’

The story told in the play, about the Highland Clearances and the energy economy, set the frame for the way many people in Scotland think about land. McGrath’s 7:84 theatre company (named for the statistic that 7 per cent of the UK population owned 84 per cent of the wealth) performed the play a hundred times across Scotland. Audiences loved it: in Edinburgh ‘the place fucking erupted,’ one of the actors, John Bett, told a journalist. *The Cheviot, the Stag and the Black, Black Oil* told of Culloden and its aftermath, of communities replaced by sheep and sheep giving way to deer, of lairdism and landlessness, oil wealth and depopulation. McGrath looked beyond the usual villains – such as the Duke of Sutherland and his factor Patrick Sellar – to accuse the system behind the Clearances: capitalism. The play, available on BBC iPlayer, continues to influence the politics of Scottish land and energy. The underlying issues persist in the renewables debate. Who is this infrastructure for? Why do we need it? Where should it be located? How, and to whom, will the benefits be distributed?

The scale of renewable technology can be difficult to appreciate if you don’t live in the Highlands: onshore wind, offshore wind, conventional hydro, pumped storage hydro, battery energy storage, switching stations, substations, power converters, synchronous condensers, port facilities, manufacturing hubs, subsea cables, high-voltage corridors and the growing ancillary infrastructure of workers’ camps, lorry parks and loading bays. Last year, the Highland Council put online a Renewable Energy Mapping Tool, which showed 1305 applications for renewables infrastructure, some already built but most at the planning stage. Many communities that already have a wind farm nearby are being asked to accept another, only bigger. Some of the turbines are 230 metres high – the height of One Canada Square in Canary Wharf. Hundreds of kilometres of new pylons, 60 metres high, will then take the electricity where it’s needed – elsewhere.

The first use of pumped storage hydro in Scotland was the Cruachan Power Station, which opened in 1965, followed by Foyers a decade later. Then nothing until now, with three projects provisionally approved by Ofgem this year, after it introduced a new ‘cap and floor’ system that guarantees a minimum and maximum return. On windy days the Highlands are producing vastly more electricity than the grid can handle. Until the transmission infrastructure is upgraded, wind farm operators are being paid to switch off turbines via ‘constraint payments’ from the UK government’s National Energy System Operator (NESO). Pumped storage hydro would allow the surplus to be stored, like a landscape-scale battery.

Loch Kemp in the hills above Loch Ness is one of the three schemes. The loch will soon be dammed and enlarged so that water can be released at times of peak energy demand to four turbines 160 metres below in a powerhouse built on ancient woodland on the southern shore of Loch Ness. This will generate up to 600MW of electricity: enough, according to the project’s website, to supply ‘over one million homes with instantaneous clean electricity for up to fifteen hours’. When energy demand is low, excess wind or solar power can be used to pump water up from Loch Ness to Loch Kemp, ready to be released again when needed. On the other side of Loch Ness, an even bigger scheme is going through the planning process: the £3 billion damming of Loch nam Breac Dearga, the loch of the red speckled trout. With an enviable head height of 475 metres above the Great Glen, it would be able to produce more power with less water than a lower reservoir. The scheme could generate 1.8GW of electricity from storage, though at the cost of creating a moonscape around the upper reservoir as it empties and fills like a high-mounted Shanks cistern. The level of Loch Ness itself will vary – up to 52 cm according to Glen Earrach Energy, and much more according to local conservation bodies, especially if there is more than one plant in operation – damaging the shoreline ecology, juvenile salmon and insects.

It’s hard to say that these schemes aren’t needed, however. Even if we seem to be breezing past the 1.5º target agreed in Paris in 2015, the UK has made a legal commitment to achieve net zero emissions by 2050. Labour wants clean energy to account for 95 per cent of electricity production by 2030, which will require more than doubling wind and solar power. The geographical tension is obvious: much of the UK’s renewable resources lie in the Highlands and Islands, while electricity demand is largely in the south. Generation and transmission infrastructure has to go somewhere, and one of the many somewheres is the catchment of Strathglass and Beauly, west of Inverness.

*Borrowed Land* starts the same way as *The Cheviot, the Stag and the Black, Black Oil*: this is my glen. It gives a portrait of Strathglass as a stronghold of 18th-century Jacobitism, emptied by 19th-century ‘improvement’ and filled with 20th-century hydro power. Kapka Kassabova recounts the re-engineering of its tributary glens – Strathfarrar, Cannich and Affric – which has resulted in a modified landscape of towering dams and ancient pine forests, buried lochs and underground torrents. Her story is also about the current inhabitants of Strathglass and tells how Kassabova ‘came to call it my glen. *Mo ghleann*. In the Gaelic worldview, *mo* expresses affection more than possession.’

Kassabova joins a long list of writers who have fallen in love with some corner of the Highlands and Islands, only for the affair to end in heartbreak, disenchantment and a book that tells the story. Alasdair Alpin MacGregor, for example, one of the most crepuscular voices of the Celtic Twilight, loved the Hebrides and Hebrideans until suddenly he didn’t, and made that apparent in *The Western Isles* (1949)*.* Compton Mackenzie lampooned him in *Whisky Galore* as Hector Hamish Mackay, the author of *Faerie Lands Forlorn*, yet Mackenzie had the same malady. Inevitably he, too, became disillusioned when the people of South Uist did not adequately oppose a missile testing range (‘this means death for the Outer Isles that were a refuge from this machine-ridden world of today’).

Kassabova’s complaint is not about the place itself so much as what is being done to it. The development of renewable power is a ‘deployment of industry in a delicate ecology’, she writes, that ‘increasingly feels like a war on nature and communities’. Her home is in exactly the wrong place. The village of Beauly, downstream from the power stations and dams of the Affric-Beauly scheme, is near the planned site of a vast switching station, gathering offshore and onshore electricity from the Western Isles, the Highlands and Shetland for transmission east to Peterhead, then south along a high-voltage direct current subsea cable to Drax in North Yorkshire.

Works are already underway on the section from the Isle of Lewis to Beauly. It can’t be enjoyable to witness: the dust, the machinery, the felling of woodland, the earth-moving, the lost wildlife and seismic tremors. Kassabova writes of renewable power as being ‘spread over the clean mouth of the glen like a cancer’. Glen Cannich is ‘brain-damaged, as after a lobotomy. Its coherence is gone.’ Her book criticises the ‘practice and mindset’ of the green industrial revolution that is changing Scotland through ‘the mechanical and technological extraction of resources on a large scale for profit’.

Kassabova would include 20th-century hydro here, though it was never designed to produce a profit. ‘The postwar era brought mass industrialisation to the most pristine places,’ she says of the work of the North of Scotland Hydro-Electric Board, which was responsible for possibly the biggest social advance in modern Highland history – hardly anyone in the Highlands had electricity when it was founded in 1943. It seems a little odd that she doesn’t name the man responsible for bringing power to the Highlands, the journalist, Labour MP and wartime secretary of state for Scotland, Tom Johnston. There isn’t another elected representative – Gladstone aside, through the Crofters Act – who has done more for the region, yet he appears here only as ‘a politician and industrial zealot’. Kassabova claims that Highlanders were promised free electricity (they weren’t) and were then let down by the state. In fact, at Johnston’s insistence, the Hydro-Electric Development (Scotland) Act of 1943 enabled the electrification of the Highlands at heavily subsidised connection rates.

Girning about hydro power in Strathglass is a bit like moving to Soho and complaining about the noisy nightlife. This energy landscape isn’t ideal if you’re in search of wildness. ‘Going north set me free,’ Kassabova writes of her first visit. ‘Something lifted me up and made me fly.’ She reminisces about those first months in ‘the silent stoic glens’: ‘no streetlamps, just stars and the outlines of the forest’. And now: ‘electric lights everywhere!’

For Kassabova, the present upheaval is a further instance of ‘industrial ways’ being imposed on native pastoralism. The history she gives is uncertain, in part because it has been vox-popped. I can find no archival evidence, for instance, for her assertion that landowners ‘burned forests during the Clearances, just to be mean’, though there are examples of the opposite: the woodland at Coille Dalavil on Skye, now protected for its Atlantic lichens, was planted to prevent a cleared community from returning. Kassabova is keen on the ‘last ancient oak communities’ on the west coast – verdant temperate rainforests that drip with ferns and lichens – because they are survivors (‘their remoteness saved them’). Here again, her history is upside down: it was charcoal production for industrial smelting and gunpowder that protected these woods. Oak *was* industry.

Kassabova’s​ intense pastoral longing makes me wary, especially when it’s pressed into an attack on both decarbonisation and industry. There is no ideal Highland past. Look behind the picturesque wildness and you’ll find long-standing patterns of extractivism. John Gordon of Cluny – a 19th-century Tory MP, estate-owner and slave-holder – knew all about the control of energy infrastructure. He smashed the quern stones used by his Hebridean tenants to mill corn and built a vast water mill in South Uist. This not only allowed him to take his ‘peck’ of flour, it also functioned as an accounting machine: the productivity of the land – and thus the level of rent – could be evaluated from the quantity of meal extracted. This story is remembered in placenames: the volume of processed grain and its windblown debris was so great that the loch at Milton came to be known as Loch Eilean an Staoir, loch of the island of the dust. Three miles north lies Loch nam Bràithntean, loch of the querns, where the tenants’ broken stones were dumped.

The Highlands have been subject to the same economic forces as urban areas. Patrick Sellar, the hired goon for the Sutherland Clearances, described Cheviot sheep as ‘machines for converting Highland pastures into wool and mutton’. Even the remote archipelago of St Kilda – an exemplar of wildness – was an industrialised landscape. The islanders probably survived the clearances that devastated the rest of the western seaboard because the landowners took their rent in the form of feathers (used for bedding) that were collected in a large-scale extractive fowling operation. There were so many birds that their carcasses polluted St Kilda’s arable soil with zinc.

There are also many more recognisable forms of industrialisation, some successful, others less so: iron ore mining on Raasay, coal at Brora, copper at Sanachan and lead at Strontian. Aluminium smelting took place at Foyers, Fort William, Kinlochleven and Invergordon; pulp and paper production at Corpach; five nuclear reactors operated at Dounreay. At Loch Kishorn in Wester Ross, three thousand workers built the Ninian Central oil platform (then the largest human-made moveable object, for use in the North Sea) against the backdrop of Bealach na Bà, the mountain pass to Applecross. The same site is now being reconfigured for offshore wind construction.

The hydro-electric infrastructure of the Highlands was a matter of particular pride in my family. My father chose the Laggan dam, built to provide electricity for the aluminium smelter in Fort William, as the subject of his high school photography project. His uncle was a labourer on what Kassabova describes as the ‘brain-damaged’ Mullardoch dam, part of the Affric-Beauly scheme. In the 1950s, another uncle and aunt used to drive from Inverness to Fasnakyle Power Station for picnics. It felt as if these places belonged to them, that they belonged to all of us. I doubt they noticed the Pictish-style carvings on the power station; according to Kassabova, ‘the stag has an erection, to show the thrust of industry through the glens.’

When Construction Scheme No. 7, the first stage of the Affric-Beauly scheme, was proposed in August 1946, there were no objections – literally none – on the grounds of visual impact, though concerns were later raised by climbers and other visitors. ‘If you objected to the industrialisation of the Highlands,’ Kassabova writes of this era, ‘you were instantly accused of being a “scenic sentimentalist”.’ She doesn’t mention who made this accusation, perhaps because it was a Highland writer, Neil M. Gunn: he didn’t just advocate for hydro, he moved to Strathglass to live next door to Fasnakyle.

A banner hanging outside the gallery of Kassabova’s partner, Tony, in the tiny village of Kilmorack near Beauly, has the words ‘Communities B4 Power Companies’ on it and directs its readers to a website where a picture of a young woman is captioned: ‘What kind of a thug scars Highland beauty? Just say no!’ The site links to various clips, one from Advance Australia, an anti-net-zero lobby group funded by the conservative mining magnate Gina Rinehart, another by Net Zero Watch, a Tufton Street outfit whose parent organisation used to be chaired by Nigel Lawson. ‘I see the mouth of the glen smashed by thugs. I speed past it. I want to see something beautiful and true and alive. Without it I will die,’ Kassabova writes. She repeatedly draws on the metaphor of sexual violence. Wind turbines are ‘the industrial rape of the land’. Balblair quarry made the sound of ‘“Rape”, Tony said’. Energy giants struggle to find land that hasn’t already been ‘raped to death’. Culligran Power Station – which is underground and remote-controlled – is described as ‘a labour camp for water’.

There’s a reasonable argument to be made that the job of decarbonising our economy has been handed to corporate bodies that have no financial incentive to choose anything other than the cheapest locations for infrastructure. Little wonder that local people feel disenfranchised. Living in the glen, people must feel as if the end of the world is happening, and being justified as climate salvation. But isn’t this a paradox of scale? I wouldn’t choose to live beside a switching station, but the alternative to decarbonising our energy is a horror of another magnitude. It’s hard not to juxtapose the environmental impact of renewables with this summer’s wildfires in the Cairngorms, not far from Kassabova’s glen, which caused the evacuation of Nethy Bridge. Last year’s fire in Dava and Carrbridge, on the edge of the Cairngorms, was the UK’s largest ever wildfire. Where I live in Edinburgh, Arthur’s Seat has been reminding us of its volcanic origin: it burned this year, as it did last.

I don’t think Scotland has adjusted to having a bushfire season. We certainly haven’t reckoned with all that is being lost: 84 per cent of emissions from the Dava fire, a staggering 32,500 metric tons of carbon, came from peat. According to the Cambridge ecologist Johanna Schoenecker, ‘these losses’ are ‘effectively irreversible on any timescale relevant to climate action’. In Scotland, peat has usually been wet enough to stop any fires burning down into it. Not anymore. The subterranean fires are hard to put out and easily reignite.

The ‘Highland story’​ of *Borrowed Land* presents a picturesque visual order disfigured by modernity, but it’s McGrath’s story in *The Cheviot, the Stag and the Black, Black Oil –* a class analysis of land ownership and the corporate control of energy – that clarifies the underlying problem with renewables. Perhaps the most obvious issue is the distribution of wealth: our present system of land-holding means that a new pumped storage hydro scheme can turn a trout lochan into a gold mine.

The land around Beauly has been owned by the same family since the Reformation. The laird is not named in Kassabova’s book, though Simon Fraser, 16th Lord Lovat and current chief of Clan Fraser, shares her opposition to a proposed battery storage plant near Kilmorack. His complaint was on amenity grounds, but I note that the plant was to be sited on land that the Lovat Estates didn’t own. Fraser then set up a company called LG-B-50A Ltd, applied for permission to site a 49.9MW battery storage plant on his own land nearby, and sold the company when the planning consent came through.

McGrath had no qualms about naming the lairds. ‘Who owns the land?’ the actress Dolina MacLennan asked in the play. ‘The same families – the Macleods, the Lovats, the Argylls, the MacDonalds, the Sinclairs, the Crichton-Stewarts and the Sutherlands,’ Paterson responded. Then other cast members interjected: ‘Plus the property dealers’, ‘the shipowners’, ‘the construction men’, ‘the distillers, the brewers, the textile men’, ‘the sauce-makers’, ‘the mustard kings’, and ‘the merchant bankers’. The cast then sang: ‘We are the men who own your glen, though you won’t see us there.’

If you own a glen, wind generation can pay well. Only 1 per cent of installed onshore wind capacity in Scotland is community-owned. In Denmark, the government insists that developers of new wind farms must offer communities a 20 per cent stake. Ownership matters. Where communities are owners, the benefits to local people are higher by an order of magnitude: a report commissioned by Point and Sandwick Development Trust put community benefit at 34 times that of the present voluntary arrangement. But they can still face problems: the community-owned Galson Estate on Lewis struggles to export electricity from its three wind turbines to the mainland. Most of the capacity of the new 1.8GW subsea cable, currently being laid, has been allocated to large offshore wind farms. In an open energy market, there’s only so much that community ownership can do to offset the power of a FTSE 100 company like SSE plc, which now owns the assets of Johnston’s hydro board and decides on the location of new infrastructure. All the plausible sites for the switching station at Beauly are owned by Fraser of Lovat.

But the backlash to renewables isn’t progressive either. In August last year a Convention on the Impact of Major Energy Infrastructure gathered 500 people from 56 Highland community councils to press politicians from all parties to debate the matter at Holyrood and Westminster. The convention was chaired by a Conservative councillor, Helen Crawford, and it was Edward Mountain, Conservative MSP for the Highlands and Islands until April this year, who got the biggest cheer for saying: ‘We are, in the Highlands, being used for industrialisation, we are being sacrificed on the altar of net zero.’

*Borrowed Land* gives a similar account. Since it does not recognise past industry it does not recognise the ways in which we have adapted to it. It’s part of the national story – Scotland or Britain, take your pick – that we re-engineer landscapes to suit our wider purposes: forest clearance, field creation, mining, land reclamation, reforestation, roads, canals, railways, reservoirs, ports, spaceports. Like every other region, the Highlands have their share of this, but even the most monumental infrastructure can be integrated into local systems of meaning. The lives of the inhabitants of Heiskeir in the low-lying Monach Isles, west of North Uist, were transformed by the construction in 1864 of a forty-metre Stevenson lighthouse to facilitate transatlantic shipping. The crisis came not when the lighthouse was switched on but with its decommission in 1942. ‘It was the loss of the lighthouse that broke them,’ Lachie Morrison, the last person to leave, told the writer Timothy Neat. ‘The lighthouse gave the Heiskeir people a feeling of security.’

The market may be an unreliable and unfair mechanism, but there isn’t a viable alternative to decarbonisation if we want a life after oil and gas, and it will depend on re-engineering that is unavoidably industrial. We could do more to meet the design challenges of this moment (contrast the stolid lattice steel SSE400 pylons used in Scotland with National Grid’s Danish-designed T-pylons, which run from Hinkley Point across Somerset). And it’s dispiriting to see the consensus around net zero undermined by the rush towards the construction of speculative and energy-hungry data centres. But net zero is no more threatening to the Highlands than delusions of wildness. ‘How I long to taste the essence of Glen Cannich! Is it still possible?’ Kassabova asks. ‘Only if I don’t go to the dam.’
