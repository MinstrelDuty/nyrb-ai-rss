# 正文

Have​ Danny Tommo (aka Daniel Thomas) and his followers in Patriot Platform read Luke Kennard’s new novel, which has just been shortlisted for the Booker Prize? Even the dust jacket might be enough to persuade a Stop the Boats protester that covering yourself head-to-ankle in a black bag is an advance on black balaclavas and Mosleyite black shirts. Patriot Platform claims to embrace Christian values, which apparently include ridding the UK of migrants. Looking at photographs of its recent protest at the port of Dover I was reminded of a moment late in the novel when a group of men dressed in black bags gather in a warehouse to sit on ‘padded wooden low-church chairs’ while being addressed as brethren (bros) from a dais. This is what a male cult looks like.

In the novel they’re more of a ‘self-flagellating men’s group’ than a hate mob. *Black Bag* isn’t ostensibly about politics, but good fiction taps into the zeitgeist. Earlier in the book there’s a telling conversation about masculinity: ‘There’s a whole contingent of men who are lost and dissatisfied, who weren’t popular at school and never got over it … They’re mostly young men. They’re confused about what that should mean. They’ve lost their identities.’

The narrator of *Black Bag* is no Patriot Platformer. With his jeans and hoop earrings, he’d be bohemian if bohemianism were affordable rather than ‘cosplay for bored kids with private incomes’. He needs to make £1400 a month to pay the rent on his dingy flat, and no matter how frugal his diet (porridge, rye bread, dried fruit, borlotti beans) it’s tough going, all the more so since he makes his living, if you can call it that, as an actor. When the novel opens, he has had no paid work for three months.

‘Good plays are probably my favourite thing in all the world,’ he says, but that doesn’t include corporate murder-mystery dinner theatre (an occasional source of revenue for him) or a drama about climate catastrophe, an audition for which goes awry when he’s asked to imagine breaking the news of a flood and can think of nothing else but to ‘release a single, undulating note’ and then denounce the very idea of theatre pieces on the topic. He looks back fondly on his time in a comedy improv group that performed in a pub attic and on other parts he has played, from Gratiano in *The Merchant of Venice* to Buttons in a regional pantomime. Back then he thought he’d made it.

The latest roles advertised on the ‘weird-acting-jobs forum’ range from professional mourner to toilet-paper hand model, none of them inviting. But he keeps the faith (‘I still have a racing heart, still want to show everyone a good time’) and is rewarded when he lucks into a role requiring him to dress up in (or *as*) a black bag for the purposes of an academic psychological inquiry. The black bag is made of leather and hangs down to his ankles, with two tiny eye-slits near the top, a metal zip down the side and gaps for his legs to go through at the bottom. He has to wear it twice weekly, once in a lecture and once in a seminar, and on no account is he to speak to other students. His feet should be bare and any movement of them should be kept to a minimum. His role is to be characterless. Dr Blend, the psychology lecturer, outlines all this – and since universities are sluggish about paying the people who work for them he hands the narrator a wad of twenty-pound notes after the first session.

As Kennard acknowledges, the original black bag experiment was conducted by Charles Goetzinger at Oregon State University in 1967. It explored the way people, when faced with an unfamiliar stimulus object, gradually shift from suspicion and hostility to acceptance, affection and even love. Kennard’s main focus is on the man inside the bag rather than other people’s reactions. He isn’t named, but he’s later given a nickname, bb – firmly lower-case.

The students’ reactions don’t pass unmarked. They range from ‘is that a dead body?’ and ‘should we call it “it”?’ through a softening change of pronoun (‘you’re distracting and you’re unsettling’) to black bag being welcomed as ‘mate’ (‘good to see you’). In the final lecture of term he’s given the standing ovation every actor craves: the students stare at him with joy and adulation. The most grateful of them is a girl called Sophie, who walked into Dr Blend’s office when only bb was there and talked to him about her proposed assignment; though his only response was to stamp his flip-flop, she thinks she owes him her First.

For bb the black bag is a ‘breakthrough role’, his ‘big chance to do absolutely nothing, as thoroughly as possible’. He can’t speak and nobody knows who he is, which gives him ‘a great sense of tranquillity’ and relieves him of the fear of being ‘nothing more than an English suburbanite’. Treating the bag as a home from home, he fashions an inner pocket or storage pouch to carry things with him – debit and credit cards, mixed nuts, a pencil holder – and is resourceful when it comes to drinking coffee inside it. He wears it even when he’s not on duty and feels lost without it, ‘as though I am cheating on myself, lying about who and what I really am’. The virtue of the black bag is that it’s ‘not really reminiscent of anything’ – not religion (despite the hint of a monastic robe), not the military (despite its being a body bag) and not sexual fetishism.

As to the last, he’s put right by Professor Justine Pearce from the Department of Modern Languages and Literature, an attractive post-humanist in her forties who wears black tights, takes him to a BDSM club that’s festooned in black and proposes they have ‘a little side-hustle’ or ‘experimental relationship’. The books on her shelves have titles like *A Non-Anthropocentric Reconceptualisation of Agency* but her engagement with bb is less abstract. Having stripped off in her office, she makes him lie down and sits on top of him: ‘I am a person. You are an absence. Ultimately I would like to fuck nothing. I don’t mean to fuck nothing. I mean to fuck *nothing*. Do you understand the difference?’

He does. And it works, not just for her, using his body inside the leather bag as a sentient sex toy, but for bb as well, who feels ‘more connected to Justine than I have to anyone in my life’. His record with women hasn’t been great. He ended his most serious relationship because he believed his girlfriend planned to kill him (‘A wok to the back of the head while I read the *LRB* at the kitchen island. Poisonous mushrooms in a casserole … Sudden breadknife frenzy’). And we see him screw up a date with another actor when he upends her handbag and puts it over his head. A weird thing to do, but he’d been missing the leathery whiff of black bag. That’s not an issue with Justine, who wants him to stay in the bag even when they’re having sex.

Justine’s research interests include ‘tech bros and their toxic personalities’, and though bb’s lifelong friend Claudio is philanthropic rather than toxic, letting bb stay in his penthouse, buoying him up when he’s down and even standing in for him as black bag on one occasion, he’s a tech bro of sorts, spending his days livestreaming video games to a dedicated fanbase. He’s also relentlessly entrepreneurial. Previous schemes have faltered: a fake takeaway food company, organ donor transportation, Handsome Grandsons Incorporated. But he thinks that by advertising black bag as a live art experience and fitting bb with a tracker, he will attract subscribers and make the two of them rich. With mixed feelings, bb agrees.

After that things go haywire. Claudio tells bb that he ought to give up Justine – she is married, after all, even if that doesn’t exclude polyamory. Then Claudio disappears, to the dismay of his followers and the anxiety of bb, who’s in love with him at least as much as he is with Justine. Dr Blend disappears too; his replacement, Dr Mazower, has never heard of him. Worst of all, bb runs into another black bag in the supermarket, ‘a wretched little understudy’ whom he confronts and berates: ‘I’m not very comfortable with someone else running around being me.’ There are more black bags to come, not only that group of them in the warehouse but all his predecessors in the experiment Dr Blend has run for thirty years. Poor bb is not as unique as he thinks.

A novel centred entirely on the experiment would be minimalist, but *Black Bag* sprawls because Kennard is enjoying himself. Some of the funniest passages – and however dark the subtext it’s essentially a comic novel – come in chapters that have little to do with the main action. There are the erotic phone calls Justine makes to bb describing a man she meets at a wedding and pursues in Paris. There are bb’s daydreams in lectures when, to cope with the boredom of sitting there while Dr Blend discusses Hephaestus or definitions of noumenon, he imagines himself working in a Swedish sawmill and being cared for by an elderly woman called Birgitta. Wackier still are the conversations he has with the family dog, a border collie called Elliott, who speaks in italics and offers a sterner wisdom, not least about masculinity, than any of the humans we meet.

The ones we don’t meet are bb’s parents. He takes three trains to visit them once a month but refuses to subject them to ‘the brutality of description’ and represents them as two stone pillars instead: ‘A disgusting business to describe anyone, really.’ However disgusted, Kennard doesn’t stint in describing Justine, Claudio and Dr Blend. Still, he’s resistant to nailing down character and plot. ‘If you try to nail something down in a novel,’ D.H. Lawrence wrote, ‘either it kills the novel or the novel gets up and walks away with the nail.’

*Black Bag* spans several genres while not belonging to any of them. It’s not quite a campus novel but has things to say about what it’s like to teach at a university (‘We’re supposedly the finest minds in our respective disciplines but we’re required to spend most of our time boring hungover teenagers to sleep’). It’s not quite a satire on the theatre world but says more about acting than Rachel Cusk does in her latest novel, *Life of M*. It’s not a surreal parable but the central bag image is as haunting as Kafka’s *Metamorphosis*. It’s not a state-of-the-nation novel either, but it wittily documents the paraphernalia of our times: chiller cabinets, EVs, VR goggles, datasets, iced coffee cans, bars of soap that look as if they have been ‘carved raw from the side of a cliff’, negronis ‘the colour of healing potions in a video game’, the disadvantages of living in a ground-floor flat, the ‘laryngopharyngeal noises’ of an espresso machine.

‘Nobody knows what to make of the 21st century,’ bb reflects. Kennard’s previous two novels shared this bewilderment. Karl and Genevieve in *The Transition* (2017) belong to a generation of young people who can’t afford to live as their parents did and are mired in debt. Elliott (that name again) and Emily in *The Answer to Everything* (2021) are neighbours whose marriages are threatened by their addictive WhatsApp romance. Both novels feature men who are trying to adapt to the dismantling of gender stereotypes. How self-effacing should they be? How focused on relationships rather than work? How compromised are they by patriarchy? ‘I think you go around in a … cloud of shame,’ Karl is told.

The word comes up in *Black Bag* too. Claudio speaks of ‘the shame that runs under masculinity like a great and powerful river’. And the narrator feels shame when he’s not bb: ‘What am I without black bag? Without black bag I am some pitiful and unremarkable man *going around, doing things*. Shameful.’ The black bags in the warehouse also feel shame, for which they atone by reciting a creed: ‘*I saw what I had become./I saw what I had been./I turned in shame./I turned again.*’ To combat the shame, a Centre for Advanced Studies in Masculinity has been set up in Copenhagen, where detoxing goes on. ‘Men are very lost’ is the message. ‘This is the great reset.’

As Claudio points out, with a glee that nettles bb, men have stopped reading novels. The only men who do ‘are the same men who bewail the lost art of dressing for dinner … It’s a dead hobby. You’d have a bigger audience if you made … bow ties.’ The Patriot Platformers won’t have read *Black Bag,* then, so they’ll have missed its diagnosis of men as melodramatic, childlike, pitiful and dangerous: ‘How like men to turn black bag into a secret society! Membership cards! Clandestine assemblies! … God, they’ve got it all wrong from start to finish.’
