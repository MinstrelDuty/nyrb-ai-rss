# 正文

Some people​ convert to Catholicism to answer their doubt, others to give their doubt shelter. Fanny Howe belonged to the latter party. ‘Did I have faith or was it hype,’ she asks in one poem. And later: ‘Did I believe or was it hope.’ Even within the idiosyncratic terms of her faith she was a refusenik: ‘I am secular. I walk the streets./I feel sorry for everyone.’ Howe converted in 1982, the same year she quit cigarettes. Liberation theology aligned with her social values, negative theology with her philosophical disposition. In one of her earliest books, *Poem from a Single Pallet* (1980), she offered a statement of aspirational vocation:

> But I, too, want to be a poet  
> to erase from my days  
> confusion & poverty  
> fiction & a sharp tongue
>
> To sing again  
> with the tones of adolescence  
> demanding vengeance  
> against my enemies, with words  
> clear & austere
>
> To end this tumultuous quest  
> for reasonable solutions  
> to situations mysterious & sore
>
> To have the height to view  
> myself as I view others  
> with lenience & love

As she later wrote in T*he Winter Sun: Notes on a Vocation* (2009), ‘since early adolescence I have wanted to live the life of a poet. What this meant to me was a life outside the law; it would involve disobedience and uprootedness. I would be at liberty to observe, drift, read, travel, take notes, converse with friends and struggle with form.’

Howe died last year at the age of 84. Well into her seventies, she characterised herself as ‘an arrested adolescent’, remarking that ‘if you keep on the side of the teenager, then you are actually resisting something that is brutal in the world. So, that was the second childhood.’ Her teenage allegiances notwithstanding, Howe may be the most notable poet to have written extensively from the position of a grandmother. In her final, posthumous work, *This Poor Book*, we encounter several such figures – a ‘granny’ in the opening pages, an old woman later on. We also encounter the speaker as traveller, pilgrim, lover, seeker, mother, thinker, visionary, friend, obstinate questioner, persistent child. ‘If I could say I was assigned something at birth,’ Howe told the *Paris Review* in one of her final interviews, ‘it would be to keep the soul fresh and clean, and to not let anything bring it down. And that’s the spirit of childhood, usually.’

Howe’s allegiance to the figure of the child was of a piece with her commitment to the weak, the poor, the disturbed, working people, refugees, prisoners and outcasts. ‘Where did the people get the idea of justice in the first place/if not from the losers, the wheat-carriers and the wounded who could not believe/in an earth without heart?’ She gravitated towards recalcitrant and radical saints: Francis and Clare of Assisi, Brigid of Kildare (about whom she made a film, *Brigid of Murroe: A Wonder Tale*, in 2014). *The Needle’s Eye* (2016), a sequence of essays, stories and poems, is devoted in part to the younger of the two Boston Marathon bombers, Dzhokhar Tsarnaev (the one who survived). Interweaving his life story with that of other ‘idealistic teenager[s]’, including St Francis and a character from an Uzbek folk tale, the book offers a peculiar and persuasive anatomy of alienation, desperation, violence and hope.

In Howe’s poems, as in many folk tales, people are often trying to escape:

> Whenever an old woman is seen going  
> into a dark forest with two children  
> you know they are escaping.  
> They are not dying. Why else  
> would they be so bright of eye?

What are they escaping? In this poem, war; in other passages, poverty, violent parents or partners, sexual danger, racism, class oppression, physical exhaustion, addiction or all of the above.

Born in 1940, Howe was the child of two singular parents: Mary Manning (an Irish actress, playwright, novelist, critic and co-founder of the Poets’ Theatre in Cambridge, Massachusetts) and Mark DeWolfe Howe (Harvard law professor, biographer of Oliver Wendell Holmes and civil rights activist). Her older sister is the poet Susan Howe, her younger sister the artist Helen Howe Braider. This suggests a network of ramifying achievement and a complex burden of inheritance. On her paternal side, Howe was descended from two prominent New England families: the DeWolfes, who were slave traders and politicians, and the Quincys, who served up two mayors of Boston and a president of Harvard. She spent her early years in Buffalo, while her father was away at war, but grew up mainly in Cambridge. But never mind her background: Howe divested herself of her social capital in her young adulthood and her work refused to celebrate exceptionality, achievement or excellence.

In her essays, Howe presents her younger self as a dreamer and loner, a truant spirit who loved the movies. As a teenager in Paris, she went to the cinema with Samuel Beckett (an old friend of her mother’s). She enrolled at Stanford, but dropped out three times. She married young, to a chilly older microbiologist; she left him soon after the assassination of John F. Kennedy, which seemed to shock her out of her marriage. She experienced what she called a ‘walking breakdown’ for the next few years. Her husband wouldn’t grant her a divorce and forced her to go to Reno. She returned to the East Coast, moving to the Bowery, where she lived for several years, holding down a number of precarious jobs (coat-check girl, go-go dancer, office temp). In California she’d written a couple of pulp novels, which taught her about structure – she considered them her workshop. During her time in New York she occasionally hung out with various luminaries of the Warholian demimonde, but she never romanticised those years and found them desolate in retrospect. She eventually moved back to Boston, working for CORE (the Congress of Racial Equality), collaborating on various little magazines and continuing to write.

After the sudden death of her father in 1967 and the assassination of Robert F. Kennedy (blows she experienced as linked), she married Carl Senna, a charismatic writer and intellectual of African American and Mexican descent. Interracial marriages had just been legalised across the US (thanks to *Loving v. Virginia*), but were highly unusual in deeply segregated Boston, notorious for its racist opposition to school bussing and integration. The marriage was tempestuous and ended in an ugly divorce. Howe raised their three children ‘in a mob of friends and children, all cohabiting’, and briefly found herself on welfare. Such experiences made their way into her work: ‘I think of the labour theory of value//as meaning nothing for mothers./But what isn’t a lie.’

Howe may well have kept her deepest struggles private, however. As she wrote in a later poem, ‘I think disclosure/Is dangerous.’ Despite the hubbub of children and friends, and (one infers) intermittent entanglement with lovers, what comes through in her poems and essays is a profound loneliness – a condition that registers as more animal or ontological than psychological. Poetry seems to have offered her both a mode of inner reflection (‘interior monologue/and some voice’) and a peculiar sociability (‘My vagabondage/is unlonelied by poems’). Her work isn’t conventionally confessional, though it is suffused with autobiographical detail. She wasn’t given to psychological schemata and saw herself as part of an existentialist generation; she was uninterested in the unconscious and preferred to speak of ‘correspondences’. Howe liked ‘sequences of lyrical poems’ with ‘the heave, thrill and murmur of the nomadic heart’. She believed that ‘though they may at first look like static, fixed-place poems with a confessional personal base, [such sequences] hold the narrator up as an idea, even an abstract example, of consciousness shifting in its spatial locations.’

Howe is associated with no poetic school and in *Q* (1998) sent up the idea of such tribal affinities: ‘The neo-neolithic urban nomad school of poetry/is almost for lone mothers only.’ She did, however, have many poet friends: Denise Riley, Jean Valentine, Bernadette Mayer, Rae Armantrout, Eileen Myles, Ilya Kaminsky, Peter Gizzi. For a short time, she seems to have hoped to become a Language poet, but despite her extensive reading in poststructuralist theory, she was most drawn towards filmmakers, philosophers, theologians, fellow converts and outliers: Simone Weil, Edith Stein, Sufi mystics, Giorgio Agamben, Michel de Certeau. She preferred her own idiosyncratic clerisy to that of her fellow experimental voyagers, many of whom – including Howe herself – were by the 1990s securely ensconced in the academy. (This gives a certain edge to her entertaining side-eye in *One Crossed Out* from 1997: ‘Let the academics sneer at the Jesuits, and go on being the hypocritical clerics of the century, she at least dared to believe in retribution.’) The Brontës, Blake, Hardy, Yeats and the Russian symbolist poets were all influences, though her poems also draw on riddles, rhymes, children’s games, ditties, fairy tales, catechisms, prayers and notations:

> What is heavier than lead?  
> The need for bread.  
> What is crueller than a boss?  
> The need for praise.  
> What is shorter than a step?  
> An indrawn breath.
>
> My secret wedding was to whom?  
> A promise not a human.

Howe, who published more than fifty books, was a prolific novelist and short-story writer as well as a poet. For decades her work was put out by small presses open to generic experimentation: Maureen Owen’s Telephone; Lyn Hejinian’s Atelos; Leslie Scalapino’s O Books. The publication of her *Selected Poems* by the University of California Press in 2000 brought her a wider readership. (Since then her poetry has mainly been published by Graywolf in the US.) Howe claimed that she ‘stopped writing fiction deliberately in 2000 with *Indivisible*. I knew it was going to be my last novel,’ yet her reputation as a fiction writer has only grown in the intervening years. Five of her novels were republished by Nightboat Books in 2006 (in a volume called *Radical Love*) and *London-rose* (written in the 1990s) was published by Semiotext(e) in 2022.

During her peripatetic career, Howe taught fiction, not poetry; poetry, like religion, was something she considered ‘unspeakable’. Yet her poems consistently speak to matters of faith, belief and poetic making. In 2019 she told an interviewer that she was interested in ‘the watery area between poetry and theology. I want to move that water around.’ *This Poor Book* wades into those waters. The title suggests both a Franciscan orientation and an elected poverty of means à la Arte Povera; it also carries with it a pre-emptive diminution, a humorous shake of the head at ‘this poor book’.

Howe’s collections typically comprise long, unfolding sequences, usually one short poem per page – a kind of pilgrimage of and in thought. *This Poor Book* is written in this serial mode; it’s a long, unspooling work, with no internal divisions (until the final section, ‘Wood’) but many pivots and unmarked shifts of locale (the sea, the shore, a bedroom, New England, California, London, Limerick, Newfoundland) in the midst of recurring motifs. The opening 25 or so pages are a dazzling launch into the visionary immediacy so distinctive in her work:

> I see a child  
> between a star and a boat.  
> It is a silhouette  
> and there is the child again,  
> now attached to the boat.  
> All black.  
> Look at the weather,  
> the futile wipers beating!  
> I see the child on dry land,  
> now with his granny.  
> Like the boy in a story  
> he sees fire in wood and words  
> in smoke and he is good, too good  
> to be far from anyone old.

Here we have, distilled, many of Howe’s preoccupations: the centrality of the child, the easy movement between the cosmic and the quotidian, the swift cuts between views (‘there is the child again,/now attached to the boat’; ‘I see the child on dry land,/now with his granny’).

*This Poor Book* functions as a shadow, or alternative, selected poems, drawing from collections published over the past 25 years, with a few earlier works also included. It offers an intriguing reshuffling of Howe’s later work, with some core sequences dispersed throughout. (A brief index at the back identifies the source works.) This unfolding aligns with Howe’s interest in what she called the ‘spiral’ poem, widening out in unpredictable recursive waves. (‘To me, the serial poem is a spiral poem. In this poetry circling can take form as sublimations, inversions, echolalia, digressions, glossolalia and rhymes.’) Her notion of the spiral poem was informed by Robert Smithson’s piece of land art *Spiral Jetty* and his essay on site and non-site, which she partly excerpted in her novella *Holy Smoke* (1979). Her swerves and cuts within even brief lyrics take wing from her fascination with Italian neorealist and Soviet films and the work of Robert Bresson. The opening pages of *This Poor Book* are from *Come and See* (2011), which was partly inspired by Elem Klimov’s Soviet anti-war epic of the same name, from 1985.

Iread​ *This Poor Book* several times before discerning the logic of its structure. At first I missed the more conventional arrangement of the *Selected Poems,* with its clear demarcations between excerpts, mainly chronologically arranged. Yet there is something in this volume true to Howe’s recombinatory spirit, her dedication to revision, to ‘strange returns and recognitions’, to what she called ‘recapitulation’. If her early books tended towards concentrated lyrics-in-series, the texture of her later works became increasingly heterogeneous, weaving together mise-en-scène, social reflection, theological meditation, flashing images, arresting notations, sudden psychograms.

Viewed one way, Howe’s work partakes of a long history of devotional poetry. She was capable of a delicate yet commanding lyric sweep. This comes through most powerfully in the passages drawn from *The Lyrics* (2007):

> The sky, my loom, is under me.  
> My foot rests on air.
>
> It’s in my hair  
> The wind is weaving.
>
> Clack and pedal.  
> Propeller and reactor.
>
> My garden droops  
> With bleeding heart.
>
> Thick oily leaves.  
> Brick and buttresses.
>
> Abstract berries.  
> Spikes and watching lights.
>
> Down here is the worker zone.  
> Over there is management.

As if piloting a miraculous aircraft, she gives us an aerial view and swoops across the territory. Here we have the characteristic Howe cut: from incantation and spell-casting to mordant social critique.

Howe was after the numinous but didn’t force it. As recordings and interviews attest, she was witty and full of mischievous humour, but she despised irony. She wrote a scarred devotional poetry and for some years – after her children had grown up – retreated for several weeks each year to Glenstal Abbey in Limerick to live and write quietly among the monks. Her poems are variously and often simultaneously political, theological and erotic:

> On the day I prayed  
> no one answered me  
> On the day I called  
> you didn’t answer me

These lines appear in ‘Wood’, which closes *This Poor Book*. It tracks an anguished love affair and first appeared in *Gone* (2003) under the title ‘The Passion’. Spiritual passion, erotic passion, Jesus’ passion.

Throughout Howe’s oeuvre, we find poems approximating prayer, the poet presenting herself

> aggressively begging: God  
> give me a penitent hairstyle  
> and a cell – not a hospital –
>
> to defend my errors in.  
> And no answers, please, to any of my questions.

For Howe, negative theology chimes with her negative poetics: ‘One definition of the lyric might be that it is a method of searching for something that can’t be found. It is an air that blows and buoys and settles. It says, “Not this, not this,” instead of, “I have it.”’ Thus the rigorous refusal of settled answers:

> I would like a question  
> To my answer. I would like more gods  
> Than one. In a nutshell  
> More songs.

More questions, more gods, more songs. ‘All voices tend towards song,’ she wrote in *The Winter Sun*. Yet throughout *This Poor Book* Howe’s intermittent tendency towards song is punctuated by savage diagnoses: ‘The son is nervous and why not./He spent his childhood on his mother’s bed watching pornography and murder.’ ‘There is a wonderful kidnapped hunted raped and betrayed girl/In fairy tales.’ Her questions are at times pointedly diabolical: ‘Afraid the rape bed/Was comfy?/No pleasure, no me?’ These scarifying moments haunt the work, and perhaps her conversion as well. If her child figures seem unmarked by any trace of original sin or depravity, violence and evil still flicker throughout Howe’s universe. She could pivot in a blink, or across a line break, from ecstatic vision to ferocious attack. Like Blake, she combined a mystic impulse with a satirical streak, a tough-minded materialism. She held to a baseline class analysis underpinned by an often grim anthropology: ‘Everything human is poisoned.’

Howe’s poems station us somewhere between pilgrimage and itinerancy. Pilgrimage implies devotion and destination; itinerancy is governed by precarity and happenstance. This oscillation is characteristic of Howe, who proposed – in what is probably her most famous essay, ‘Bewilderment’ – ‘bewilderment as a poetics and a politics’. Her aim is precisely, paradoxically, to get lost: ‘Into the forest I went walking – to get lost.’ Howe moves ‘on the apophatic path’. If some poems put us on the road – ‘The Road to Ennis’, for example – others invoke actual pilgrimage sites (Medjugorje, Lourdes). Arrivals are brief intervals in a longer journey: ‘But I am just a guest, a woman of the road.’ As she told an interviewer in 2016, ‘I can’t stay anywhere long. I’ve always been getting kicked out of schools, or leaving them. Everywhere fixed is a prison, so any motion away is an act of insolence.’ This ongoing movement is part of her attempt to sustain – or at least to recognise – ‘the soul naked and alone./A runaway from home./Recapitulating.’ At times she shifts into an allegorical register, as if rebooting Blake’s ‘Little Girl Lost’:

> Lost won’t admit she’s lonely or it’s late.  
> Cellophane leaves  
> Orange on the school window panes.   
> Children’s things are stuck on everything.  
> Say it, they’re divine. Go outside.  
> Tear down the crucifix.  
> Stop the folk stories. Peppermills  
> And girls sacrificed for gold.   
> Nail up the sacred heart.  
> You’ve got to start walking again  
> As what you have become  
> Since childhood put you in relation  
> To star, road, rotation.

*This Poor Book* is unsurprisingly valedictory, full of backward glances and summings up:

> I was one of the lucky ones,  
> privileged to live a few decades in peace,  
> if without stature or particular beauty or grace or fame, just watching  
> and taking notes at the company gates.
>
> …
>
> What was our generation’s definition  
> of the way to live? Something about action,  
> ideology, authority.  
> We wanted to kill those who are now us. Grown-ups!

Howe’s work offers a record of ageing, a sustained circling around death and endings. ‘No more nostalgia./You are a farmer in winter now.’ Yet the prospect of death becomes the occasion for a new beginning:

> The trees billow under a vague grey sky.  
> Nearby and not far away, suffering.  
> And the end of me.  
> But if I know I have everything  
> Then I can begin.

One thinks of the Japanese tradition of the death poem (*jisei*), of Bashō composing his last lines, of Masaoka Shiki’s haiku: ‘After I’m dead,/Tell them I was a persimmon eater/Who liked haiku.’ Decades ago, Howe offered her own proleptic testament in *O’Clock* (1995):

> I won’t be able to write from the grave  
> so let me tell you what I love:  
> oil, vinegar, salt, lettuce, brown bread, butter,  
> cheese and wine, a windy day, a fireplace,  
> the children nearby, poems and songs,  
> a friend sleeping in my bed –
>
> and the short northern nights.

Contemplating various versions of the end in *This Poor Book*, Howe offers an anxious catalogue:

> There’s one piece of knowledge, if I don’t have it,  
> How will I get by, go forward, get on  
> Where will the hope come from  
> How will I pay  
> For morphine, swill, phenobarbital  
> The bag for my head, the applesauce, the ticket  
> To the forest to get lost in, the ice floe to ride on,  
> The typhoon, the ledge, the will to starve?

It’s typical of Howe to worry about paying for a chosen death. Typical too that she widens out from the question of assisted suicide to complex, folkloric images of a broader, human vanishing – into the forest, or on an ice floe, like a lone endangered polar bear. Howe answers her own cascading question with a homely inventory, fortifying herself to set out as a pilgrim once more:

> Give me my shawl, my corkscrew  
> And my cloth bag.  
> Give me my hot water bottle and my book.  
> Give me my stick and my water.

Here she permutes Walter Raleigh’s ‘The Passionate Man’s Pilgrimage’, composed as he awaited execution (‘Give me my scallop shell of quiet,/My staff of faith to walk upon’). It’s rare for her to be so conspicuously allusive.

Howe’s is a posture of dispossession. A communal abjection underlines the possibility of solidarity: ‘I was just one more person who had no skills or money./My education was self-inflicted. This was an advantage.’ At times one might feel that she protests too much. Her writing across genres is nothing if not skilful. But mere skill is something she would have scoffed at. And what matters in Howe is the rejection of official hierarchies, the divestiture of privilege (hence the resonance of figures such as St Francis and Weil, dramatic renouncers both). It is also true that the ‘I’ of Howe’s poems is a shimmering semi-allegorical thing, not a clearly biographical figure – ‘an idea, even an abstract example, of consciousness shifting’.

In 2004 Howe envisioned her future reader as a solitary figure encountering one writer’s ‘record’: ‘If someone is alone reading my poems, I hope it would be like reading someone’s notebook. A record. Of a place, beauty, difficulty. A familiar daily struggle.’ An apparently modest gloss on her work, this becomes, on reflection, something more capacious. For all her modelling of poetic encounter as a one-to-one affair, Howe summons a communal ‘we’ throughout *This Poor Book*:

> Will we row across a rubbery sea  
> Or bob in the sky  
> Vomiting, singing, falling over  
> Losing our voices, our wits, our hearing?
>
> Will our lives have meaning?

Unanswered and – by Howe’s stringent lights – unanswerable, at least by us.
