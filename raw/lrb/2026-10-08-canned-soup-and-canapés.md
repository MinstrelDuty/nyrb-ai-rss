# 正文

In 1987​ the American artist Paul Thek heard that his former lover and friend, the photographer Peter Hujar, was dying of complications from Aids. When they were together in 1960s New York, Andrew Durbin writes in his joint biography, other people felt ‘extraneous in the room’; a younger gay man, watching them being extravagantly gay at a time when most homosexuals were in the closet or existed in a ‘semi-neuter’ state, thought that ‘if this is what it’s like being a faggot, I’m cool with it.’ Theirs hadn’t been a monogamous relationship; Hujar was what his friend Fran Lebowitz described as a ‘genius about sex’. If he had ‘five minutes’ he would pop out for an encounter of some sort. By 1970, Hujar estimated that he’d had sex with fifteen thousand men. Even so, for two decades Hujar and Thek seemed inseparably linked. Yet when Thek tried to call after hearing of his friend’s condition, Hujar refused to speak to him. Thek wrote in his notebook: ‘That they had once even known each other was just an annoying reminder of wasted time.’ Hujar died in November 1987. Thek died, also of Aids, the following year.

Hujar was born in New Jersey in 1934. He never knew his father, who was a ‘baker, possibly a bootlegger’, and spent his life searching for him, both literally and metaphorically. In Thek’s last letter to Hujar (or the last that survives), he remarks:

> I liked when you said that perhaps all this while you’ve just been trying to find your father, using your camera. When I think that, I begin to see all the photos of yours that I have seen over the years, and I think at those moments: ‘Peter looked for his father here, and here,’ etc. Like that fish on the sand many years ago, and you and Joe together by the old car on Fire Island beach, and me in my apt., and me on the zebra.

Hujar’s mother was a waitress. She left him as a child with his Ukrainian-speaking grandparents in Ewing Township, in central New Jersey, where the family had a small farm. Hujar’s first language was Ukrainian; he also developed a love of animals, in whose company he ‘could drop his guard’. Cows were early subjects – he told his friend David Wojnarowicz that he’d taken a photo of them from a great distance when he was first experimenting: ‘The cows were like a hundred feet away. I mean, three little black dots … but I knew it was cows in a field.’ As an adult, he took some striking photos of animals: cows, dogs, goats. In his mind, they were no different from his portraits of humans. ‘I don’t look at a cow and see a person,’ Lebowitz said. ‘But Peter did.’

When he was eleven, Hujar was sent back to his mother, who had married again. They lived in a one-bed apartment on East 32nd Street in New York. His mother didn’t want Hujar there, and yet felt guilty for leaving him with her parents in the first place. She stopped speaking to her family when she took him back, and complained bitterly that he cost her money. She was an alcoholic and got Hujar to exchange her empty bottles for money, an experience he found deeply embarrassing. She told him he was ugly – despite his being unusually handsome from a young age, or because of this – and lashed out at him. He lived with her for only five years, walking out when she threw a bottle at his head. Hujar spent the rest of his life angry at her: he would refer to her by her full married name whenever he mentioned her to his friends, who attributed his outbursts of rage, during which he’d smash furniture or his harpsichord, to his upbringing. He made up another mother in the biographical note accompanying the one monograph he published in his lifetime, *Portraits in Life and Death* (1976), describing her as a ‘young starlet who appeared in the chorus of Busby Berkeley musicals’.

At school Hujar’s main interest was photography, which got him into the School of Industrial Art. His former English teacher Daisy Aldan – a lesbian, poet, translator of French literature (Mallarmé in particular) and co-founder of Tiber Press – encouraged him to pursue it as a career. She searched the Yellow Pages and found studios in New York, sending him off to make introductions and lie about his age. Hujar’s portrait of her was, Durbin thinks, ‘probably the earliest photograph Peter ever printed for exhibition’.

Hujar found a cheap studio on Thompson Street in Greenwich Village, at the centre of the city’s burgeoning gay scene, and in 1956 picked up a boyfriend called Joseph Raffael. That winter they took a road trip and stopped in Miami, where Thek, an old friend of Raffael’s, was living. Durbin isn’t able to reconstruct the details of the trip, but Hujar’s contact sheets remain as evidence. In one photograph, the three men pose in a ruined boathouse. But it’s Thek who seems to have attracted Hujar’s interest. He is photographed lying down in a forest in white T-shirt and shorts, propping up his head like a pin-up girl. His look is slightly insolent: he seems to be daring the photographer to come closer. In another, he is lying on a couch, in ‘an early version of what would become a signature Hujar pose’.

By 1958 Thek had moved back to New York. He and Hujar became lovers around 1960. Unusually, there is a gap in Hujar’s otherwise well-kept archive between 1960 and 1962. Of the contact sheets that survive, most are photos of Thek. ‘Photography was Peter’s way of becoming intimate with others,’ Durbin writes. Lebowitz remembers Hujar as ‘such a profoundly alienated person – the most I’ve ever known, a prisoner – there was no way out of it for him. He had absolutely the least confidence of anyone I have ever known.’ During this period he was assisting Keturah Blakely, a medical and industrial photographer, and learning how to enhance his work in the darkroom, adding light and shade. In 1962 he got a Fulbright scholarship to study filmmaking at the Centro Sperimentale di Cinematografia in Rome. Thek joined him after spending the autumn painting in Norway. It was meant to bring them closer; in the end it broke them up. After they had sex one evening, Thek turned to Hujar, put his arms around him and said: ‘You are a beautiful brother.’ Perhaps unsurprisingly, ‘Peter seemed to vanish before his eyes.’

By the end of his first term, Hujar was skipping classes. ‘He preferred to stay in bed and drink coffee, read his horoscope, go to the movies, and wander Rome.’ But in 1963, his photographic archive fills out again. He travelled around Italy, taking photographs of people and places. That year, he and Thek also expanded their scene ‘thanks to Cy Twombly and Felicity Mason, whose friends included famous filmmakers, writers and artists – Federico Fellini, William S. Burroughs and Brion Gysin among them – as well as plenty of beautiful Roman boys’. Mason, nicknamed ‘the randy granny’ by the publisher Anthony Blond, became a close friend of both men. Hujar photographed her having sex with a young man in the shower.

In August 1963, Hujar and Thek went to Sicily. They visited the Capuchin catacombs in Palermo, which exercised a profound influence on both their work. Hujar included several photos taken in the catacombs – despite photography being forbidden – in *Portraits in Life and Death*, while Thek’s later use of wax figures seems to derive from the experience. In the catacombs, Thek opened a glass coffin and ‘picked up what I thought was a piece of paper; it was a piece of dried thigh … I felt strangely relieved and free. It delighted me that bodies could be used to decorate a room, like flowers.’ In one of Hujar’s photographs, a skull is adorned with fabric flowers and covered in a gauzy material, the white ruffles of its clothing stark against the dark. In another the head of a monk is almost lifelike: his eye sockets haven’t sunk in, the embroidery on his hat is still visible, and so too is the moustache around his mouth. Later, an interviewer told Susan Sontag – who wrote the introduction to *Portraits in Life and Death* – that she found it hard to look at Hujar’s catacomb images. Sontag replied that she must look at them, as ‘You *will* die. We’re all going to die. It’s a good thing to know.’ (Sontag wrote the introduction while in hospital, the night before the first operation intended to cure her breast cancer.)

In 1964 Hujar was back in New York, and back working as an assistant – this time at the studio of Harold Krieger, a commercial photographer whose clients included *Harper’s Bazaar*, *McCall’s*, *Esquire* and *Newsweek*. Hujar’s main responsibility was lighting. Krieger wanted his photographs to look like paintings by Manet. Although Hujar preferred what Durbin calls the ‘erotic shadow play’ of Caravaggio, he was good at his job. ‘He was like a giant eye,’ Lebowitz said. ‘He would always say: “Look at the light.”’ (When filming Hujar for one of his *Screen Tests*, Andy Warhol called him the ‘boy that never blinked’.) After a day at the studio, Hujar would visit Sontag, often with Thek. They seemed so close that they could have been mistaken for three lovers; and indeed, Thek did sleep with Sontag now and then.

Hujar started taking more explicitly queer photographs in the mid-1960s. Durbin speculates that he hadn’t done so before because he didn’t want to ‘shoot anything that might have been construed as trashy’, particularly as so few photographers went in for gay male eroticism at the time. (It’s striking that, among the many names in Durbin’s book, Robert Mapplethorpe is not mentioned, even though he and Hujar knew each other and disliked each other’s work. Mapplethorpe said that Hujar’s photographs made him ‘cringe’, while Hujar was scathing about Mapplethorpe’s superficial ‘art look’ and the fact that he didn’t make his own prints.) Perhaps, too, Hujar’s wariness came from a desire not to be pigeonholed. Durbin quotes him as saying that he didn’t want ‘to be remembered as a gay photographer’, though his friend Linda Rosenkrantz recalled that fear with a slightly different emphasis: ‘I hope I’m not remembered as just a gay photographer.’

What we do know is that in 1966 Hujar invited two ‘leather queens’, Jay and Fernando, to his studio. ‘In the session,’ Durbin writes, ‘Jay and Fernando admire each other’s uniforms, gold chains, gruff poises. At ease, turned on, they touch, taste, toy with each other. One guy’s cock is pierced. It rises from his wiry pubic hair in several close-up shots.’ Hujar would often flirt with his subjects, many of whom started out as lovers, drawing them out of their shells of self-protectiveness. (It helped that he preferred to use a Rolleiflex camera, held at his waist rather than at eye level, meaning that his camera did not obscure his face.) He wanted his photographs ‘to be seductive’; of his later erotic photos of men (including Thek) masturbating, he would say: ‘I want people to feel the picture and smell it.’ One of his best-known images, *Orgasmic Man* (1969), is an example of this visceral intimacy. And, almost in exchange for the trustingness of his subjects, Hujar became a different person when he was behind the camera: his rage, which caused one of his close friends to describe him in an obituary as the ‘angriest person’ he knew, would disappear. He was ‘possessed’ by the act of taking photographs.

In late 1966 or early 1967, Hujar himself stripped for the camera. In one photograph from the session, he is doing a wild dance, his arms flung in the air, his hair whipped up. Half of his body is in shadow; the outline of his pubic hair is visible, but the photo is remarkable not for his nakedness but for its sense of movement. His footprints cover the floor: clustered at different points in the room, marking his dancing in circles and his movements to and from camera. He submitted the photographs from this session to a masterclass run by Richard Avedon and Marvin Israel. One of Avedon’s biographers suggests that this may have been a ‘mating ritual’, since Hujar was Avedon’s type. But then Avedon’s work of the 1940s and 1950s was one of Hujar’s most important influences. Either way, he got in.

As part of the masterclass, the students were introduced to a number of established photographers. When Hujar met Diane Arbus, with whom he is now often compared, it didn’t go well.

> ‘I know who you are,’ she said dismissively … When Peter later joined Israel and Arbus for dinner, she didn’t speak to him the whole evening. He was surprised. What had he done? ‘Later, I found out she’d thought I’d ripped her off – copied her way of photographing transvestives – with a black border around the picture. When I first saw Diane’s black-bordered portrait of the Gish sisters in *Bazaar*, I thought she’d copied *me*.’

Hujar described this rejection as the moment when his work diverged from Arbus’s: she pushed him to ‘move some place else’. Where Arbus’s work can sometimes seem cold or even cruel in its attention to the oddness of humanity, Hujar’s photographs are empathetic: they dignify his sitters, and his gentler use of black and white makes his subjects seem, at times, like subjects in a Renaissance portrait (he said that painters influenced him more than photographers). Consider one of his portraits, *Penny*, from 1981. Penny was a ‘transvestive’ in the East Village who would beg for money wearing a 1940s dress and a straw hat, her face covered with Vaseline. Arbus would have photographed her while she was on the street, but Hujar took her to his studio. In the photo she sits in a chair, her left hand on her knee, her long nails visible. Hujar’s light catches the Vaseline on her face, but doesn’t make a mockery of it. She stares into the camera, her lips pursed and her eyes flickering with vulnerability.

Avedon introduced Hujar to the art directors at *Harper’s Bazaar*. This led to other commercial work, which supported him for the next few years. Thek, by contrast, was moving further and further away from anything that could be considered commercial. He had begun experimenting with casting his whole body in wax. *The Tomb* – a life-size wax cast which quickly became known as ‘The Death of a Hippie’, housed in a pink pyramid – was first shown at the Stable Gallery in 1967, to mixed reviews. ‘At 33,’ Thek wrote to Hujar later, ‘I put my corpse up for sale what a story it will all make someday but there were no buyers.’ Soon after, Thek moved to Europe, where he lived itinerantly for the next decade.

By the autumn of 1968 Hujar had a new boyfriend, the anti-war activist Jim Fouratt. He began to search again for his father, getting nowhere. Fouratt thought Hujar was afraid of success and sabotaged his chances because he couldn’t face being rejected. ‘He heard about Peter’s fights with influential magazine editors who might have hired him, watched him spit in the face of the curator Henry Geldzahler over some throwaway comment.’ When Hujar was introduced to Cecil Beaton, he was complimented on his reputation as a ‘very fine photographer’ and replied: ‘I hear the same about you.’ Beaton stalked off. Perhaps this behaviour had something to do with parental neglect; perhaps Hujar was also anticipating rejection by a homophobic society that would never appreciate his work.

In the early hours of 28 June 1969, Fouratt walked past the Stonewall Inn on Christopher Street. He didn’t like it: it sold cheap watered-down booze and ‘was crawling with chicken hawks – men who preyed on young boys – and pickpockets’. (Hujar’s one-time flatmate, Steve Lawrence, had tried to buy the bar a few years earlier, with the idea of turning it into an ‘art-world-adjacent nightclub’, but was outbid by Tony Lauria of the Genovese crime family.) It seemed that a police raid was happening, and yet something was different this time: unlike during most raids, the punters had not fled but were being joined by more and more people.

> As the cops loaded patrons into a holding van, someone finally screamed: ‘Nobody’s going to fuck around with me. I ain’t going to take this shit!’ … A cop shoved a trans woman, who hit him with her purse. As he clubbed her back, the crowd shouted: ‘Pig! Pig! Pig!’ … The crowd hurled itself forward … rattling the police cars holding their friends; they threw whatever they could find, bricks, bottles, coins – they showered the cops with coins.

Fouratt ran to a pay phone to call his friends. Not long after, he helped to found the Gay Liberation Front. Hujar went along to the first meeting but was dismayed to hear some argue that the price for social acceptance was an end to cruising and sex outdoors. Hujar loved to cruise. Many of his non-studio photographs depict the waterfront piers, abandoned buildings and parks where he met men. He stopped going to GLF meetings but would create one of his best-known images for them: a poster depicting a group running down West 19th Street under the words ‘COME OUT!!’ The picture is remarkable not just as an ‘iconic image of the gay rights movement’, as Durbin calls it, but for what it shows about Hujar’s method: the white light blazing behind the group, giving them a happy and hopeful glow, was brought out in the darkroom.

At the time​ of the Stonewall riots, Thek was living in Ponza, between Rome and Naples. When Hujar visited with Fouratt (who hadn’t met Thek before, and thought he was ‘crazy’), they fell out. A pattern was emerging. Some of Thek’s friends worried that he was smoking too much weed, others that he had an undiagnosed psychiatric disorder. Whatever the case, it marked the start of the split between the two men. By 1972, Thek would write to Hujar from Essen: ‘I think I’ve gone mad two or three times by now, if it matters … In any case, I wanted to say how important it is for me that you + I recognise the love between us can sometimes make things more difficult.’

By the 1970s Hujar had mostly stopped doing commercial work. He survived on canapés at parties, canned soup and the ‘generosity of his friends’. (During the novella-length interview conducted in December 1974 and eventually published as *Peter Hujar’s Day*, in which he recounts his previous 24 hours, his interlocutor, Linda Rosenkrantz, exclaims: ‘You didn’t eat all this time? Since dinner? I would’ve eaten six things by now.’) During this period he began to photograph people behind the scenes. There is a photograph of the costume designer and performer Alexis Del Lago, backstage at the Palm Casino Revue: she wears a tuxedo with a large white rose pinned to the lapel, a cigarette in one hand and the hint of a smirk. Or there is the performance artist Richard Gallo, by a fire extinguisher at the Brooklyn Academy of Music: he wears a gimp mask with a zip for a mouth, a fishnet body-suit and a cape. ‘I like people who can transform themselves,’ Hujar said. Towards the end of 1973 he photographed Candy Darling, the Warhol star, the night before she underwent surgery to try to curb an aggressive form of lymphoma. ‘If something happens,’ she told Hujar, ‘I want you to take this photo to the *Village Voice* with the headline “Candy Saying Goodbye to Her Fans” over a black border.’

When Darling died in March 1974, the *Village Voice* declined the photograph. At her funeral Hujar whipped out his camera near the open casket and was scolded by Lebowitz (‘*This is a funeral*’). Around that time he dressed up as Darling for some photographs by Sheyla Baykal, revelling in ‘going against the grain of one’s sex’, as Sontag put it. This enjoyment was a more frequent occurrence than Durbin describes. Lebowitz, for example, recalled meeting Hujar for the first time at a film screening in 1971. He had on a skirt, ‘the sort a librarian would wear’. Cross-dressing was technically illegal; Hujar said to Lebowitz that ‘it was unfair that women could dress like men and he wanted to see what it felt like to wear a skirt.’

In 1975 Hujar asked Thek if he could photograph him for his monograph before Thek flew back to Italy. Thek was reluctant: during the first sitting he ‘stares blankly at Peter, looks away, talks, makes a funny face’. After marking a few photographs on the contact sheet, Hujar asked Thek if he could shoot him again. ‘It was brave of Peter to ask,’ Durbin writes, ‘because he must have known he was courting a fight, some nasty comment.’ Thek wrote in his notebook that he didn’t like the ‘critical scrutiny, that lens’. He thought about saying no: ‘Who in his right mind WANTS his picture taken??’ But in the end he agreed. During the second sitting, according to Durbin, ‘Paul’s face wheels through all his feelings for Peter – his love, his envy, his dismissal, his misunderstandings, his wanting to forget, his wanting to forgive.’ In the final photograph Hujar chose for the book, Thek is looking straight at the camera, mouth ajar and eyes quizzical, head tilted to the left. It is intimate without being loving, and, leaving aside the slightly insolent stare, very different from those early photos of Thek lounging in the woods.

Sontag’s *On Photography* came out in 1977. Hujar told a friend that it made him never want to take another photograph. He’s not mentioned in it – he feared it was because she didn’t think him famous enough. Avedon told Hujar that Sontag might be ‘the enemy’. She paid little attention to the process in a darkroom, writing that the production of an image requires ‘nothing more elaborate than a few trays, a bottle of developer and a bottle of hypo solution’. ‘Susan should have asked Peter about the portrait he made of her for *Portraits in Life and Death*,’ Durbin writes:

> That conversation might have changed her mind. In it, she is lying on his mattress, her hands behind her head, eyes gazing upwards. So much of the meaning of this photograph was refined in the darkroom. Peter brightened the background wall of his loft into a kind of sky while rendering the undulations of the blanket more pronounced. The image is almost a seascape, with Susan drifting on his bed like some grand ship. He also adjusted her hair; much of the grey disappeared from the negative in the final print, with only a few strands showing near her temple, a prophetic salute to the famous white streak of later years. She is more Susan Sontag than Susan Sontag ever was in 1975.

Hujar and Thek​ used to squabble about Sontag. If Hujar complained about her ‘coldness, lack of humour and pretentiousness’, Thek would relay the moans back to her. The friendship between them frayed; eventually she stopped replying to Thek’s increasingly paranoid letters. In 1978 he wrote calling her a ‘manipulative harridan’ and accusing her of trying to make him straight. In the absence of many other shared intimacies of this kind, Durbin’s biography, while rooted in years of scholarship on the two men, often feels uneven, a bit uneasy. Hujar’s work is so much more arresting than Thek’s, and his life more interesting by virtue of his remaining in New York, being a witness to and a participant in the burgeoning gay rights movement. In attempting to combine two biographies, *The Wonderful World that Almost Was* sometimes feels rushed or sketchy, years flying by in only a few pages.

This sketchiness is most apparent when Durbin comes to the end of Hujar and Thek’s lives, which he covers only in a short epilogue. In his introduction, he writes that the lives of artists who died from Aids are frequently ‘read backwards’, making them seem ‘tragic, twilight figures’. He argues that this tendency is more pronounced in the case of Hujar and Thek, who were both ‘obsessed with death’. Yet in refusing to write at length about their deaths, Durbin cuts their lives short. Hujar railed against his diagnosis. As his friend Vince Aletti wrote in an obituary for the *Village Voice*, Hujar spent much of his life attempting to cure himself of his childhood traumas: he ‘tried, often simultaneously, psychoanalysis, meditation, yoga, fasting, odd cleansing diets, various gurus, charlatans, healers and unconventional therapies’. A numerologist convinced him that by adding an extra letter to his name he might become more successful. But ‘as sex became more and more forbidding’, due to his sense of failing health or fear of infection, he ‘threw himself into faith with new vigour’*,* keeping pictures of Indian mystics, a Buddhist protective charm and a rosary (he was brought up Catholic) by his bedside. ‘What Peter didn’t want to believe, however, was the doctor’s finding that what was making him so tired was not his protracted withdrawal from a lifetime of smoking, but Aids.’ When he was told in early 1987 that he was HIV positive, he stopped taking photographs.

In the 1980s Hujar was closest to Wojnarowicz, whom he’d met in 1980 and stayed friends with after a brief relationship. Wojnarowicz kept Hujar’s letter of diagnosis and drew a picture of two men kissing on it. ‘This guy was like a father,’ Wojnarowicz wrote, ‘but was it really that, because I also saw him as sexual, handsome, beautiful mind, beautiful body, his was similar to mine, even with twenty years’ difference. He could finish my sentences.’ He told his partner that, following Hujar’s diagnosis, he had three priorities: ‘My work, Peter, and you. In that order.’ Durbin writes that Wojnarowicz was the ‘great love’ of Hujar’s life, though Hujar’s executor, Stephen Koch, said that Hujar told him the great love of his life was his work. Either way, the bond between the two men was clear: you can hear it in excerpts from the interview Wojnarowicz conducted with Hujar. And in his private ‘tape journals’ (published as *Weight of the Earth* in 2018), Wojnarowicz repeatedly recalls Hujar.

Yet Durbin’s emphasis on Hujar and Thek’s relationship means that Wojnarowicz is mentioned only fleetingly, and Hujar’s ease with the younger man is overshadowed by his fractious relationship with Thek. This is a pity. Wojnarowicz was diagnosed as HIV positive within a year of Hujar’s death, just before his 34th birthday. He continued seeing and hearing his friend. In his tape journals, Wojnarowicz recalls dreaming of an angry Hujar dying all over again; he feels a dull pang of grief when a Tracy Chapman song comes on the radio and he realises Hujar won’t ever get to hear it. When Thek was dying, Wojnarowicz visited him twice. One friend thought that these meetings allowed Thek to make peace with Hujar. ‘David said to him it was all right … David was the medium.’
