Animation​ was invented in France, but plasticine was invented in Britain. In 1892, Charles-Émile Reynaud showed his Théâtre Optique for the first time in Paris: hundreds of hand-drawn images on gelatin, each depicting the next phase of a story. Attached to a reel and passed in front of a magic lantern, the images projected movement onto a screen. In the mid-1890s, Georges Méliès started to make fantasy films with the ‘stop trick’, where a single change was made to a live-action shot so that the scene changed as if by magic. This technique – pausing the film so that changes could be made – eventually gave rise to stop-motion animation. In 1897, an art teacher called William Harbutt needed a non-toxic modelling clay that wouldn’t dry out. Harbutt’s, the company he founded, manufactured plasticine in Bathampton, Somerset until 1983, a few years after a small animation company named Aardman, which made stop-motion films using plasticine models, set up shop twenty miles away in Bristol. Now Aardman has made eleven feature films, more than twenty TV shows and scores of short films. An exhibition at the Young V&A, *Inside Aardman* (until 15 November), marks the studio’s fiftieth anniversary with a step-by-step look at the way its productions develop from the drawing board to the screen.

Aardman began in 1970 in David Sproxton’s garden shed. He and his friend Peter Lord started experimenting with animation while they were students at Woking County Grammar School for Boys. Peter was interested in cartooning and David in photography; their first attempts used figures cut out from magazines, inspired by Terry Gilliam’s animated sequences for *Monty Python’s Flying Circus*. Sproxton’s father worked at the BBC, and a colleague of his saw some of the boys’ experiments and invited them to make a short animation for the show *Vision On*, which was designed for deaf children. At first they stuck to 2D animation, drawing cartoons onto transparent cels in the manner of Disney and Looney Tunes. Sproxton rang up Halas and Batchelor, the powerhouse studio that had released the first two British animated features, and wangled a tour of their studio. They came up with a signature character for their *Vision On* shorts – a weedy superhero with an underbite. Because Lord liked the word ‘aardvark’, they called him Aardman.

*Vision On* commissioned thirteen short films from Aardman in 1971 and they worked on them during their gap year. But 2D work soon became tedious, and Aardman as a character ‘just wasn’t very compelling or interesting’, in Lord’s words. Their next idea drew on a black and white film they’d seen on TV called *Clay, or The Origin of Species* (1965). It’s eight minutes long and can be watched on YouTube. Against a jazz soundtrack, a lump of clay spreads itself across the screen and is moulded and remoulded by an invisible hand. It becomes a face, a worm, a bigger worm, a seething mass of saurian shapes that mingle, mate and devour one another in constant motion. As the film goes on, more recognisable forms appear: a horse, plants, a tortoise, a spouting whale. The animation is simple but the dynamism and liveliness of the medium are immediately apparent. Excited by the possibilities of clay, Lord and Sproxton began making shorts in plasticine.

Soon they had a character who was as impish and resourceful as they were – Morph, a little man with a simple, round head who messed up Tony Hart’s desk on *Vision On*’s successor, *Take Hart*. Before long Morph had his own show. By 1980, Aardman was a real business with its own studio space in Bristol, and in 1981 *The Amazing Adventures of Morph* was nominated for a Bafta. Some of the characteristics of later Aardman films are already here: the gentle satire of the protagonist’s foibles and the inventive use of everyday objects. Morph and his friends live under Hart’s table in a space cluttered with suitcases, spools of thread, treacle tins and tubes of paint which they sit on, sleep under and play with. (The best episode is the one where the ‘dog’, Nailbrush, goes to a dog show and gets into a fight with the other brushes.) Morph can change into a bouncy ball or a puddle and slide through solid surfaces.

In the early days, Aardman’s work wasn’t entirely centred on children. *Down and Out* (1979) was one of Lord and Sproxton’s first attempts to introduce dialogue to claymation. They placed a hidden microphone in a Salvation Army hostel reception and animated a conversation between a man trying to get a meal ticket and the bemused desk clerk. It’s amorphous, without a proper beginning or end, but the echoing grey space around the two men, complete with real background noise, proves an effective chamber for the tale of bureaucratic complexity. In *Down and Out* and *Morph*, Lord and Sproxton realised something that other animators had yet to grasp fully. Morph is a play character, speaking in squeaks and grunts, but he has a distinctive personality – easily bored, self-absorbed and proud – which provides the comic meat of his adventures. Aardman’s characters, though involved in slapstick scenarios and uninhibited by human physiology, have a recognisable and even subtle psychology, expressed in the face and especially in the eyes. This, too, was a consequence of the medium. The combination of stop-motion and plasticine, and the necessarily tight, static camera angles, meant that tiny facial movements could be implemented and registered on screen. As David Gritten notes in his book *Aardman: An Epic Journey* (2018), *Down and Out* taught Lord and Sproxton that ‘getting the sounds to match the lips was much less important than the movement of eyes and eyebrows.’ This understanding would allow them to create the most subtle comic actor in the Aardman canon, the entirely silent dog Gromit, who can express interest, worry, alarm, relief, irony and forbearance with a few creases of his malleable brow.

Gromit and his owner, the inventor/ astronaut/window-washer/baker/pest-control man Wallace, came to Aardman with their creator, Nick Park, who was a student at the National Film and Television School when he met Lord and Sproxton. (He had blagged a job lot of plasticine from Harbutt’s on condition that he credited the company in his final student project.) Park shared their love for the paraphernalia of postwar English life, but added to it a sensibility drawn from his childhood in Preston – factory chimneys, tartan slippers, camper-van holidays, patterned wallpaper. Wallace is based partly on his father, an amateur inventor, and Gromit’s name came from the rubber grommets that his brother, an electrician, used at work. The theme tune is, of course, by a brass band. Wallace and Gromit’s first adventure, *A Grand Day Out*, took them from the basement of their semi-detached red brick to the surface of the moon, where they sample squishy moon ‘cheese’ on crackers. Each successive Wallace and Gromit film became more dialogue-heavy, but *A Grand Day Out* is almost wordless, relying for its humour on visual jokes (Wallace works on his rocket design at a drawing board; we cut to behind him and realise he’s playing noughts and crosses against himself) and the vivid relationship between the harebrained Wallace and his long-suffering dog. Park excels at making the audience care about characters who are inarticulate or even inanimate: the pathos of the film is provided by an abandoned coin-operated gas cooker on the moon that at first seems menacing but is, it turns out, pining to ski in the Alps. For Wallace’s voice, Park recruited Peter Sallis, who had played Cleggy on *Last of the Summer Wine*. Sallis was southern and had a fruity thespian voice in real life, but the character’s eccentricity became inseparable from his West Yorkshire accent. Cracking cheese, chuck!

Park’s arrival in 1985 brought the total number of full-time Aardman employees to five. Six years later, he was nominated twice in the Animated Short Film category at the Oscars, for *A Grand Day Out* and a five-minute film called *Creature Comforts*. This was another vox-pop film along the lines of *Down and Out*, only the interviews had been conducted especially for it. The subjects are ordinary people describing aspects of their living conditions, and Park decided to put their words into the mouths of zoo animals. A big cat lying on a branch complains about the food he eats as an immigrant, ‘more like dog food than proper’; a hamster in a small hutch with her squeaking young admits she’s struggling with clutter. Park took the vox-pop concept somewhere new, marrying the spontaneity of the subject matter with Aardman’s visual inventiveness. The voices match their avatars perfectly: a lonely woman is a gorilla in a cramped enclosure, a pedantic middle-aged man a tortoise. Somewhat to Park’s chagrin (he had been working on *A Grand Day Out* for nearly ten years), *Creature Comforts* won the Oscar.

*Creature Comforts* was spun off into a popular series of adverts for the Electricity Board, in which tortoises and pandas rave about their new electric radiators and showers. By this point Aardman was making the bulk of its income from advertising, as funding for short stop-motion films had dried up. The arrival of Channel 4 in 1982 made TV advertisers more open-handed and adventurous, and Aardman spent the time between films making ads for Lurpak, Hamlet cigars, Domestos, Access credit cards and the Enterprise 64 computer. Meanwhile, Lord and Sproxton discovered the benefits of merchandising, especially after the third Wallace and Gromit film, *A Close Shave*, introduced the world to the omnivorous prankster Shaun the Sheep, who became an icon when Baby Spice was photographed with a Shaun backpack in 1997. (He now has his own theme park in Japan.) After the Oscars for *Creature Comforts* and the Wallace and Gromit short *The Wrong Trousers*, American studios began to smell money. In the late 1990s, Park got to work on Aardman’s first feature film, to be financed by the new Hollywood studio DreamWorks.

In many ways the collaboration between Aardman and DreamWorks, which lasted until 2007, was a blessing: there was much more money and a chance to make something on a more ambitious scale. Lord and Sproxton knew they didn’t want to repeat the mistakes of Pixar, which had recently been acquired by Disney, and they insisted on retaining the rights to all their characters. But although the main players are scrupulously polite in their accounts of the time, a definite chafing can be detected. Jeffrey Katzenberg, the former head of Disney, became their contact at the studio and made his presence felt, visiting the *Chicken Run* set every few weeks. ‘We wanted *Chicken Run* to feel as British as we possibly could,’ Park told Gritten. ‘And of course Jeffrey wanted to make sure it could be understood by American audiences.’ This meant removing British slang (no ‘wazzock’) and trying to make the characters seem more driven and successful. Looking at the design of a vehicle for the Wallace and Gromit feature *The Curse of the Were-Rabbit* (2005), Katzenberg said: ‘Is the rust essential?’

Park may have got his own back, though, with *Chicken Run*, the story of a resourceful group of Yorkshire hens who escape their hellish egg camp despite, not because of, the American snake-oil salesman rooster who lands in their midst. It was pitched as ‘*The Great Escape* with chickens’, and there are homages aplenty – the movie opens with Ginger, the Steve McQueen of the group, bouncing a Brussels sprout against the wall of her solitary confinement cell – but once again the delight of the film comes from the attention to detail, the tiny visual gags crammed into every shot. Park, Lord and Sproxton are hyper-attentive to visual echoes: a shuttlecock becomes a chicken’s wedding veil, a spoon a spade, brush bristles a dog’s teeth, a discarded door the beginnings of a spaceship. Everything in an Aardman film is at a small scale: there’s a playfulness that nods to its young audience, to the child’s repurposing of household objects for games. These animators spend their days tinkering with plasticine, which for most people is the stuff of childhood. Big, shiny technology is often the source of agitation and danger in the films: think of the robot dog Preston in *A Close Shave* or the labyrinthine pie machine that threatens the chickens or the remote-controlled ‘wrong trousers’, which a malevolent penguin uses to drag Wallace into abetting a jewel theft. In the end, the makeshift and make-do wins the day – Ginger and Rocky stuff a carrot into the right orifice to break the pie machine, and lacking the biological capacity to fly the coop, the chickens build themselves a plane out of the wood from their huts. Eventually DreamWorks railroaded Aardman into making an entirely CG feature, *Flushed Away* (2006). Meddling from Los Angeles in the form of excessive test screenings didn’t stop it from being a disappointment at the box office, and it was understood by both parties – not without some relief – that the collaboration was past its sell-by date.

The V&A exhibition celebrates the tactility of the Aardman world. On display are hand-drawn storyboards, metal moulds for the face of Mrs Tweedy, *Chicken Run*’s villain, Shaun heads on sticks and the largest Aardman puppet ever made, the hydraulic-powered armoured mammoth that carries the Bronze Age tyrant Lord Nooth in *Early Man* (2018). A display case shows 22 disembodied mouths posed in the vowel and consonant shapes needed to make the sentence: ‘Come on lads, don’t just sit there!’ But over time the studio has become more entangled with new technologies, and these are given due attention too. None of the models is made purely of plasticine any more – they contain metal armatures to help them stand upright and silicone foam to make them lighter and less melty. All the films have computer effects and retouching, and the experience of *Flushed Away* didn’t put them off CGI: *Arthur Christmas*, Aardman’s second computer-animated film, came out in 2011. Since 2018 the studio has been employee-owned, insulating it from the worst shocks of movie-industry financialisation, but the future is far from clear. Of late, a note of techno-optimism has crept into the Aardman canon. The most recent Wallace and Gromit film, *Vengeance Most Fowl*, feints towards a Luddite message, as Wallace’s obsession with gadgets threatens Gromit’s peace and the simplicity of their lives. The disruptive techno-force, though, undergoes an eleventh-hour reset to save the day, and the film ends on a note of harmony between man and automaton. But plasticine gets the last word. ‘There are some things,’ Wallace says, patting Gromit’s head, ‘a machine just can’t do.’
