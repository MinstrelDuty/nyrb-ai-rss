# 正文

Richard Wollheim​ once recalled receiving a postcard from Adrian Stokes some time in the early 1960s (the postcard, he said, was ‘tantalisingly dated “Tuesday afternoon”’), which contained the sentence: ‘I shall discipline and keep myself in trim in wondering for what simple reason Palestrina avoided concerning himself with a tone poem on the plangent fountains of Rome.’ I was reminded of this by a passage in *Music’s Odyssey* where, describing the music of Palestrina, Robin Holloway writes: ‘The limits are, so to speak, so audible – what it does *not* do, or does not *care* to.’ I was struck by the way ‘does not care to’ seemed to rhyme with ‘avoided concerning himself’, as though both writers wished us to imagine Palestrina weighing his options – shall I? shan’t I? – and then, Bartleby-like, deciding ‘I would prefer not to.’ A moment’s reflection, and it became clear that Stokes was saying something altogether different, his facetiousness pointing up the immense distance between Palestrina’s style and Respighi’s, as well as the limits within which Palestrina had, perforce, to work. By contrast, Holloway’s choice of words speaks of his jealous commitment, as a composer himself, to creative free will. Within the confines of his style, Palestrina could have done things differently.

Born in 1943, Holloway was one of an exceptional hatch of young British composers – numerous, fertile, diverse of idiom and imagination – who in the 1960s and 1970s struggled to make their voices heard above the din of ideology issuing from Darmstadt and Paris. The fight to compose music incorporating elements of tonality left its scars on him, and he still spits feathers about what he calls ‘historical necessity’, the dogma that the trajectory of music history has been shaped by irresistible though inscrutable laws. Of the unjust neglect of Othmar Schoeck, he writes: ‘Schoeck is a perfect example of a composer ironed out by the factitious notion of “historical necessity”.’ And of Grieg: ‘Still more than Dvořák, Grieg is “historically unnecessary”: his achievement is tiny … Yet a vital line of liberation flows from him, to Delius then Grainger, thence up the nooks and crannies of a 20th century spurned or ignored by Official Progressive Modernism.’ For Holloway and others, the downside of resisting the party line was too much freedom.

By the time he reached maturity, the expansion of the canonical repertoire was unstoppable and the volume of music pressing on the imagination of a composer unprecedented. Where, four hundred years earlier, Palestrina could think his music in a clear, uncluttered aural space, the inner ear of a composer in the late 20th century was beset by a cacophony of past and present music. One solution was to hunker down, ruthlessly to exclude influences so as to open a clearing in the stylistic forest. Alternatively, one could accept the situation, embrace the stylistic free-for-all and ransack the past as an inexhaustible source of ideas and inspiration. This was the path Holloway took, and it’s no surprise to read in *Music’s Odyssey* – his exuberant celebration of musical difference – that, of the composers of the postwar avant-garde, it is Luciano Berio whom he warms to most, singling out *Sinfonia*, his inspired 1968 exercise in musical bricolage, as one of the few works of Official Progressive Modernism likely to endure.

Holloway has been unusual in his generation of composers in being a writer. At Cambridge he read English for Part One, music for Part Two. His PhD thesis on Debussy and Wagner was so good that it was quickly published. In the long teaching career at Cambridge that followed, he turned away from the analytic writing he excelled at to what used to be called the ‘higher journalism’. For many years he wrote a column in the *Spectator*, and there have been two collections of his journalistic miscellanea. *Music’s Odyssey* represents a summation of his achievement in this mode, a great gathering up of all that he has thought and felt about the art that has shaped his life. It is an astonishing book, by any standards staggering in its breadth and depth of knowledge, and commanding in the easy assurance of its countless discriminations and judgments. Whether it is altogether a success is harder to be sure of.

It’s a history of sorts, though it avoids concerning itself with factors and forces external to music: institutional change, shifting patterns of patronage, intellectual currents and so on. Instead, it conceives of history as a succession of remarkable men (of the more than two hundred composers it covers, only seven are women), talented individuals working within a traditional craft handed on from generation to generation. In his introduction, Holloway advises us to use his book as we might a travel guide, dipping in and out to gen up on the sights, and take his advice about what not to miss and what to skip. With this in mind, he addresses each composer in a similar format: a brief summary of the life and work, a top-level critical appraisal – salient characteristics of the style, strengths and weaknesses, reception history – and then a closer look at a selection of representative pieces (blow by blow, even bar by bar, accounts of the music, synopses of opera plots, paraphrases of songs). His preferred style is clipped, impressionistic, note-form. Lists of attributes in sentences without verbs. The model is Arnoldian: the critic’s task is to direct us to the best that has been thought and written – the best composers, the best music of the best composers and the best passages in the best music of the best composers. Evaluation is of the essence, the standard high. To write good music is stratospherically difficult and few attain to it. Composers are forever falling short, disappointing the expectations their sublimities have awakened in us. Not even Bach is perfect.

Holloway has no interest in historicising his appraisals. Everything is to be viewed through the lens of his contemporary sensibility. His judgments are forthright, unapologetic. Though its model does not equate the passage of time with progress – Byrd is not necessarily less great than Britten, Machaut than Messiaen, Rameau than Strauss – this 1400-year history is heavily weighted towards its later phase: we arrive at Beethoven on page 225; from Wagner to the present takes up 800 pages, more than two-thirds of the book. Such disproportion might strike us as egregious. Where Schütz gets short shrift and Schein and Scheidt no mention, lesser creative figures from later periods are given questionable space. Countless interesting and accomplished composers from the Renaissance get no look in (Guerrero, Cipriano de Rore, Willaert, Weelkes etc); whole swathes of the Baroque period are passed over (Buxtehude and Telemann nowhere, only a page on Vivaldi). Holloway might argue that this top-heaviness simply reflects the increasing tilt, post-1800, away from a communal style towards the idiosyncratically individual. To put it another way, the angle of difference between the styles of Byrd and Tomkins or Lassus and Palestrina, even Purcell and Charpentier, is small compared to the difference between Mendelssohn and Berlioz, Schumann and Liszt, let alone Bartók and Rachmaninov, Boulez and Britten. In the later period there’s just more to cover and account for. And in any case, it’s here that his own interest chiefly lies.

*Music’s Odyssey* is a hecatomb of sacred cows: Hildegard of Bingen (‘psychedelic bore’); ‘Lassus, alas. Standard stuff’; Gluck’s music (‘threadbare, flattened, lacking in surprise or personality’); the first movement of Beethoven’s Op. 111 (‘melodrama, histrionics, false pathos, rodomontade’); Brahms’s second piano concerto (‘saggy self-indulgence’); Shostakovich’s Fifth Symphony (a ‘dead-sea fruit’); Philip Glass (‘hapless inanity’). This is fun, whether one protests or sends up a silent cheer. Holloway is candid about his biases. There are works, composers, whole sections of the repertoire with which he can find no affinity: he thinks Schumann’s C major Fantasie is unlovable and overrated, but he accepts that he might be wrong; Verdi’s *Falstaff* leaves him cold (like Coleridge and the sunset, he can see, not feel, how beautiful it is); as to Italian opera, the entire corpus eludes him. This ebullient partiality is the book’s great virtue – it teaches us that we too can have the courage and confidence of our opinions and that we mustn’t be afraid to challenge the pieties of the culture – and it is a potent corrective to the prevailing blandness of the classical music world: outside academia, a critique-free zone.

There is a conspicuous and mildly ostentatious lack of apparatus to the book: an exiguous and offhand bibliography, a skeletal index, no musical examples and no pictures. Quotations are not referenced, nor are borrowings from Holloway’s own previously published work. A mere handful of writers get a positive mention, and Hans Keller isn’t one of them. Holloway has spoken gratefully of Keller as a valued mentor early in his career as a composer, but came to be disappointed in him as a music critic. He wrote of his [disillusionment in an article in the *LRB*](/the-paper/v17/n15/robin-holloway/keller-s-causes) of 3 August 1995, but though he argued his case closely, there was a degree of animus in the piece that suggested a need to cast off Keller, as of a father figure who stood in his path. At heart Keller was a formalist for whom music and words were intrinsically incompatible. Since music neither denoted nor represented anything, it could not be said to have content: form is content; the notes are the notes are the notes. Music might be thought of as a language – it has syntax, argument, narrative – but one that cannot be translated. If we are to speak about it at all, we must confine ourselves to its immanent features. We can say things like ‘the exposition modulates to the mediant rather than the dominant’ or ‘the theme of the scherzo uses the same notes as the melody of the slow movement, but in a different rhythm and sped up.’ We can track tonal patterns or measure the rate of harmonic movement, establish a hierarchy of material from deep structure to foreground or map motivic relationships and so on. Beyond observing demonstrable attributes, there is nothing useful to add. As to expression, Keller didn’t discount it, he just regarded it as self-evident and any characterisation of it necessarily subjective. He thought descriptive criticism tautological; he scoffed at music appreciation. *Music’s Odyssey* is Holloway’s definitive riposte.

‘Music, by natural bent, is that which at once receives an adjective,’ Roland Barthes wrote, disheartened. Holloway is a virtuoso of the epithet. His descriptive vocabulary is like a Pantone colour swatchbook, shaded and graded to allow, in kaleidoscopic permutations, the finest of distinctions and a supple exchange between evocations of what the music may be said to be like and reflexive descriptions of the way it affects us. Imagery blossoms on the page. Metaphors of social encounter, taste and smell predominate. Weber’s style is ‘at once aristocratic – elegant, dapper, Beau Brummell; yet indefinably vulgar, Cockney, sentimental … refined and bumptious’. *Tendresse*, the fifth of the pieces from Fauré’s *Dolly Suite*, is ‘a flawless miniature world of tact, gentleness, humour, sweetness, without anything cheap, faked, condescending’. Beethoven’s *Emperor* concerto is ‘unattractively boastful, assertive, bullying’; Mussorgsky’s song ‘Hobbyhorse’ ‘absolutely unaffected, without whimsy, archness, cuteness, coyness: on a knife-edge of sheer awfulness’. *Hippolyte et Aricie* (Rameau) is ‘ravishing’, ‘delicious’; Fauré’s late songs are ‘odourless and taste-free’; Borodin’s first symphony ‘a bit dry and chewy’; Ravel’s violin and cello sonata ‘a crinkled thing – acrid, rebarbative, acerbic’; Mozart’s last piano concerto ‘not without touches of wryness, even acidity’ (shades of wine writing here). At times the characterisations take off into prose poems. Here are three: of the opening of Ravel’s Concerto for the Left Hand, ‘black swelling waves, so soft as to be near inaudible, through which is perceived a shadowy marine monster groping along the ocean floor’; at the close of Mendelssohn’s *Midsummer Night’s Dream* overture, ‘the vision passes into the distance; overheard, glimpsed down the wrong end of a telescope – crystal-clear yet unfathomably mysterious – through a glass lightly, uncanny without shadow’; and, of the chorus ‘How Lovely Is Thy Dwelling-place’ from the Brahms Requiem: ‘It portrays the delights of the celestial mansion with unabashed worldliness – one seems to tread on carpets, sit on sofas, glance through the potted palms at Johann Strauss and his band.’ Eat your thesaurus, Barthes!

The focus of *Music’s Odyssey* moves between aphoristic sketches and longer vignettes, for which Holloway adopts a more discursive style, and it’s here that his insights are most telling. His portrait of the music of Haydn (a verbatim reprint of his contribution to a 1998 collection) and his case for the greatness of Tchaikovsky are worth the price of the book alone. He is particularly effective in his use of contrast, placing one composer against another to throw both into sharper relief: Victoria and Palestrina, Purcell and Charpentier, Handel and Bach, Tchaikovsky and Mussorgsky, Strauss and Mahler, Schoenberg and Stravinsky, Berg and Webern. Meanwhile, he marks critical turning points in the tradition – the transitions from high polyphony to *seconda prattica*, from the Baroque to the classical style, from late Romantic tonality to the emancipation of dissonance – with sharply etched, synoptic mini-essays.

The nature of *Music’s Odyssey* as a consumer guide (one detects the hand of the publisher here) pulls against this lateral sweep. To use it for occasional reference is to miss some of the best things it has to offer. Yet it stubbornly thwarts any attempt to read it ‘as a book’. The repetitive format quickly tires, the notes-and-observations idiom at times becomes impacted and the running commentary accounts of individual works, opera plots and song cycles are too detailed and too many, as though the background material of the book had seeped into the foreground. Occasionally, Holloway’s love of paradox can lead him into nonsense, as when he says of Janáček that ‘the stretches where he is tedious are indistinguishable from the stretches where he is electrically inspired’ or of Brahms’s songs: ‘This music could be called repressed were it not for the juicy *jouissance* of the actual notes.’ There are lapses in the quality of the prose, understandable over such a long haul, but one wonders why they haven’t been edited out. And the slog shows, especially when the big beasts lumber into view: Beethoven comes over as especially fractious, hot and bothered; Wagner waxes routine.

The structural fault running through *Music’s Odyssey* – its trying to be two things at once – is not unconnected to its uncertainty about who it might be for. Ostensibly, it positions itself as a book for listeners new to classical music: it calls itself an ‘invitation’; it prefaces the text proper with a beginner’s guide to the way music works; there’s a glossary of technical terms at the back; and the ubiquitous character-writing requires no specialist knowledge to enjoy. Yet there are many pages of the book that refer to matters that will be over the heads of most readers, and unless you know your way around the repertoire pretty confidently, Holloway’s flights back and forth between composers and styles will leave you dizzy. His genealogies scintillate with insight (who else, one wonders, would have cross-pollinated Scarlatti and Berio? Or described the music of Stravinsky’s *Agon* as ‘Tchaikovsky’s ballet idiom drawn through the steel mesh of Webern’? Or traced a line from Glinka’s *Kamarinskaya* to *The Rite of Spring* and onward to the music of Steve Reich?) but to get the point of them you need at least an idea of what the music sounds like.

The problem of register is one faced by any book on the history of classical music that hopes to address a general audience. In 1965, Joseph Kerman wrote a polemic bemoaning the lack in academic musicology of a discourse equal in sophistication to literary criticism. Even a light acquaintance with university music studies of the last sixty years suggests that his plea was heard. Outside academia, however, the conversation that has existed for centuries between professional thinkers and amateurs interested in art and literature has found no equivalent where music is concerned. A shared level of comprehension cannot be assumed. Of the few writers that Holloway mentions in *Music’s Odyssey*, it is Charles Rosen and, above all, Donald Francis Tovey whom he seems to respect most. But Rosen and Tovey assumed (perhaps wrongly) a fair level of literacy in their readers; they used musical examples and expected more than just a basic understanding of musical grammar, structure, device and process.

Writing in 2002, Holloway cast a rueful glance back to a time, now passed, when Tovey was ‘a middle-class household name’. But was he? When Holloway was growing up, musical self-improvement was in the air: it was not unusual for concert programmes and liner notes to include expositions of structure and even quotations, and the Third Programme and its successor, Radio Three, took their Reithian responsibilities very seriously (they employed Hans Keller, after all). But the soil in which these hopeful initiatives were planted was shallow and meagre and they didn’t take. Since then, levels of musical literacy have steadily declined. With few exceptions (communist Hungary was one), music has remained an accessory in national curriculums in Europe and America, while music lessons – optional extras for the few – have been understood to be about learning an instrument, rather than learning to compose, improvise or think about music critically.

As one of a cohort of new British composers interviewed by Paul Griffiths for his book *New Sounds, New Personalities* in 1985, Holloway recalled the pleasure he had felt, as a child of six beginning piano lessons, in altering the set pieces and ‘making them take a different direction’, a reminiscence he repeats in the autobiographical introduction to *Music’s Odyssey*, where he supplements it with further stories of his resistance to being told what to do, to rules and conventions. The hint of ‘Holloway or the highway’ (call it Emersonian self-reliance) in the tone and mindset of *Music’s Odyssey* imbues it with a certain fresh and invigorating energy. At the same time, its determination to do it all itself, its virtuosic one-man-bandism, creates an odd effect of impermeability, as if it were intent on inoculating itself against unwanted, perhaps seductive influences. It is pervaded by the presence of what is being kept out, what it has turned its back on, namely the scholarly, the academic, the unapologetically intellectual, above all the analytic. My guess is that Holloway associates these approaches with the flight of contemporary music into the university in the decades after the Second World War – the abandonment of the general public by composers in favour of an esoteric conversation among themselves. His aversion to this turn has as its corollary an unshakeable faith in the contract between composer and lay audience. *Music’s Odyssey* is his monument to this faith, and the reader for whom it is written is the listener for whom he composes, a figure of his imagination.

It​ is also a fund of ideas for new things to listen to. One of the many works that Holloway has discovered for me, Purcell’s anthem ‘O Lord God of Hosts’, a setting of a passage from Psalm 80, ends with the words ‘O let us live’ called out by each voice in turn, across the run of the music and in a broader pulse. The effect is intensely moving, especially when one sings it, because of its precarious joy, its being at once a plea to God, of uncertain outcome, and a hope for the self, lest one should end one’s life never having lived. Perhaps, above all, it moves us because it seems the cry of music itself. But music does other things too. Holloway’s fear for the life of music, for music as a call to life, that it might be snuffed out by intellection, makes him wary of its more abstract incarnations and predisposed to overblow the role of musical affect (*Affekt*, as he insists on writing it). The composer in him is drawn towards the intricate history of music as a craft. He is alert to the distinctions of what he calls ‘facture’, the way a piece of music is put together, and his preferences are swayed by the quality of invention he finds in a composer, the manner in which the music opens out new territory. But facture for him is always the servant of experience, our experience of music in time, one event succeeding another. He has little to say about form.

In *Art and Its Objects*, Wollheim proposed that it is ‘part of the spectator’s attitude to art … that he should make it the object of an ever increasing and deepening attention’, while in *The Invitation in Art*, Stokes wrote: ‘A poem, like a picture, properly appreciated, stands away from us as an object on its own.’ Thinking of music as object, as separate from us, as ‘over there’ rather than ‘in here’, is difficult. It takes years to learn to pay quiet attention to a picture or a poem, but at least they stay still. We scarcely know where music resides; we cannot fix it in place; it flows past us; to varying degrees it envelops us, carries us away, floods our mental space. It’s especially hard to disentangle ourselves from music’s emotional content. Great music moulds time, such that we can hold in our minds musical argument, narrative, architecture, despite the transience of the sounds these shapes are made of, and to some degree we grasp these acoustic patterns intuitively. But there is more to music than its performance in time. It can be read as a body of knowledge, played through and studied in score, removed from the hot medium of experience to the cool and dispassionate space of contemplation.

Music is both too difficult and too easy to access, and it seems that we have opted to stick with ease over difficulty. We don’t bother to teach ourselves the language of music because we don’t need to, and maybe we don’t want to, in case, in knowing more about it, in involving ourselves in its difficulties, we dispel its magic or, at the very least, lose the value it has for us as a place where we do not have to think. *Music’s Odyssey* accepts, indeed consecrates, this capitulation. For a composer who over a lifetime has devoted his creative energy to bringing music down from its ivory tower to connect once more with the public, the difficulty of music, of listening, of understanding, of maintaining the distance necessary for aesthetic contemplation, has had to be downplayed. There are moments in *Music’s Odyssey* where Holloway loses patience with his own descriptive idiom: ‘Many more words could be thus adduced … but they would be mere signs and ciphers’; ‘But isn’t all this a bore?! It takes longer to read than to hear’; ‘This music, like all music, speaks for itself’; ‘But all this is redundant to what music *does*, concrete and exact … a grammar of *Affekt* without needing words’; ‘adjectives fail.’ Could it even be, he asks, that the ‘vaguely romantic, inchoate, but deeply held’ responses of ‘average “lay” music-lovers’ are enough? Could it be that ‘the “ignorant” popular view is not vulgar: it is right’?

The frustration is understandable. In avoiding analysis for fear of losing most of his public, Holloway has tied his hands, hands which could have uncovered so much about the way the expressive character of music inheres in the notes. The best things in *Music’s Odyssey* yearn for the deeper engagement of Holloway’s musical mind, for the sort of extended analytic treatment he brought to bear on his early study of Wagner and Debussy. It might seem ungrateful to dwell on the things a book so packed with glancing insights might otherwise have accomplished; but the pleasure it gives for what it does is tinged with regret for what it does not do, or does not care to.
