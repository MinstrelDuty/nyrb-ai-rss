# 正文

According to​ Stephen Campbell, around 250 books about Leonardo were published at the time of the 500th anniversary of his death in 2019, of which about fifty were biographies. It is hard to imagine any other painter or sculptor, living or dead, ever attracting this kind of attention, but it is equally hard to attribute it to Leonardo’s artistic talent, especially as he is chiefly known for one work, the *Mona Lisa* (or two, if one considers the almost wholly repainted *Last Supper*). Leonardo is considered to be not just an artist, but a man with an astonishing range of interests and insights, someone for whom there is no historical parallel. Commercial Leonardo museums and exhibitions have recently opened in various Italian cities, including two each in Venice and Rome. These contain no original works of art, but give much attention to reconstructions of machines illustrated in his surviving notebooks.

The main problem facing anyone trying to write a biography of Leonardo, apart from the obvious one of finding something new and plausible to say, is the nature of the available evidence. In addition to a small number of paintings, this is of two kinds: his surviving notebooks, amounting to at least seven thousand pages, and the information provided by historical sources and early printed texts. Not much new has come to light for more than a century, but the main outline of his life has long been clear. The illegitimate son of a notary, Leonardo was born in 1452. He was trained as a painter in Florence and at the age of thirty was sent to Milan by Lorenzo de’ Medici, apparently because of his excellence as a musician, to present a silver lyre to the ruler of the city, Ludovico Sforza. There he remained, working under various patrons (including Ludovico himself), until the conquest of Milan by the French in 1500. While there, he painted *The Last Supper* on the wall of the refectory at Santa Maria delle Grazie and produced a full-size clay model for an equestrian statue in bronze of Ludovico’s predecessor, Francesco Sforza, which was later destroyed by the French. In search of work, he returned to Florence, though soon left to serve as a military engineer for Cesare Borgia. By 1503 Leonardo was back in Florence, where he remained for the next three years, during which he began a large mural of a battle scene for the town hall but abandoned it when he was invited back to Milan by the French governor. In 1513 he moved to Rome to work for the pope; in 1516 he accepted an invitation from Francis I and travelled to Amboise, where he died three years later.

Several short accounts of Leonardo’s life were published over the next couple of decades, of which the most interesting was by the historian and physician Paolo Giovio, who had probably met him. Giovio described Leonardo as handsome, affable, brilliant and generous as well as an excellent musician and singer. After mentioning Leonardo’s work on optics, light, shadow and anatomy, and his obsession with pictorial technique (despite leaving his works unfinished), Giovio added that his talent for arranging theatrical performances made him ‘particularly pleasing to all the rulers of his time’. An example of his prowess, mentioned in several early sources, was a mechanical lion constructed to honour Francis I. The lion was able to walk towards the king, open its chest and reveal a bouquet of lilies.

Leonardo was one of the first European artists to become well known in his lifetime, but most of those who had heard of him in the three decades after his death seem to have had little idea of what he had done. In this respect he was unlike, say, Michelangelo or Raphael, whose fame was based on specific works of art, particularly the frescoes in the Sistine Chapel and the Vatican Stanze. The work most often associated with Leonardo was *The Last Supper*, but Michelangelo Biondo, a physician living in Rome who published a short book on painting in 1549, thought it was by Mantegna. Biondo mentioned Leonardo as an outstanding artist, but all he really knew about him was that he had written a book on anatomy. A few months later a fuller account of Leonardo appeared in the first edition of Vasari’s *Lives.* This is more about Leonardo’s personality, described in broadly similar terms to those used by Giovio, than about his paintings, very few of which Vasari had seen. The text includes a long and generalised description of *The Last Supper*. The picture had already been deteriorating during Leonardo’s lifetime but when Vasari finally saw it in 1556 he called it a *macchia abbagliata*, an indistinct blur. Several early copies provide the best evidence of its original appearance.

How far we can rely on Vasari’s account of Leonardo is debatable, but it dominated perceptions of him for the next two centuries. During that time the only major development was the publication, in 1651, of a *Treatise on Painting* based on Leonardo’s unpublished notes. The text, which is dull and doesn’t always make sense, was prepared by scholars, not artists. At one point there is a discussion derived from Leonardo’s manuscript on how to depict a man operating a spinning top (*calmone*) which in the published text became a cannon (*cannone*). Aspiring painters were encouraged to read it, but its impact seems to have been slight. Poussin, who provided illustrations for the first edition, dismissed the treatise: ‘All that is of value in that book could be written on one sheet of paper in large letters.’

Modern views of Leonardo really begin in the early 19th century. A crucial factor was the opening of the Louvre, which for the first time allowed the public to see the *Mona Lisa*. Together with various other paintings by Leonardo which had been in the French royal collection for centuries, the *Mona Lisa* was put on display in 1804, after Napoleon had kept it in his bedroom for four years. Over the following century it became the most famous picture in the world. Most of the paintings now attributed to Leonardo, apart from those in the Louvre, became well known only later in the 19th and 20th centuries. Ideas of what his pictures actually looked like were vague before the late 18th century and many of those which were then most highly admired could only be identified with great difficulty, if at all, since they tended to be in private hands.

Leonardo’s great fame as an artist really began with the increasing availability of high-quality reproductions in the 20th century, along with the growth of mass tourism. Campbell writes of ‘Da Vinci Worlds’ to characterise the way Leonardo has become a commodity. The most extreme example was the purchase in 2017 by a member of the Saudi royal family of the problematic *Salvator Mundi* for $450 million – the most expensive work of art ever sold. Although Campbell makes a distinction between the world of art-historical scholarship and the myth surrounding the figure of Leonardo today, he perhaps exaggerates the difference. Whatever else it may involve, art history is about providing attributions for works of art, an activity largely invented in the late 19th century with the emergence of connoisseurs, who claimed to possess a gift for identifying authorship. This activity had traditionally been done by professional artists, but since many of the works that were now in demand were produced during the early Italian Renaissance (and for centuries hadn’t been much regarded), the new connoisseurs could claim a scholarly expertise that most artists did not possess. Money, of course, was an essential element, since the connoisseurs were usually involved, directly or otherwise, in the art market, and some were anxious to exclude potential competitors.

Art historians are now more discreet about this kind of activity, but there is still prestige involved in attaching a well-known name to a previously disregarded work – much less to the opposite, and this is true whether the work in question is in a museum or on the market. An important aspect of connoisseurship is that those who claim to be good at it are often reluctant to change their minds, even when faced with clear evidence, say from historical documents. Few of the most convinced connoisseurs have direct experience of drawing, painting or carving, and are often not much interested in those who do. Thus no one seems to have thought it worth asking leading contemporary artists about the two most contentious modern attributions to Leonardo: *Salvator Mundi* and the *Bella Principessa*, a profile portrait on vellum of a young woman in Renaissance dress.

Art historians are also responsible for promoting the idea that Leonardo was a singular genius, a pioneer with interests spanning anatomy, the natural world and machines, in addition to painting and sculpture, and that his thoughts have a special value and significance. This is derived from the notebooks, which have no parallel in surviving documents from the period, and were presumably preserved largely because of the drawings they contain. We can’t say how unusual many of his ideas were in the world of the artists or craftsmen to which he belonged. But since his ideas on painting seem to have had little influence beyond the artists of his immediate circle, the main justification for studying them seems to be that he was exceptional for his time. The extent of the scholarly activity is astonishing. In Florence’s main art history library there are about 35 shelves of books devoted purely to Leonardo, and this doesn’t include the vast production of articles about him in academic journals. The study of Leonardo has become a self-sustaining industry.

Campbell argues that the image of Leonardo today is ahistorical, but points out that the notion of him as a solitary genius doesn’t fit with the evidence in the notebooks, which indicates that he regularly employed assistants and collaborators. Nor should we necessarily believe that Leonardo held no religious beliefs, or that he was a vegetarian. (Not that such claims are particularly surprising. Anyone who is familiar with Vasari’s *Lives* will know that Italian Renaissance artists were often supposed to have been unconventional in character or behaviour.) One way in which the prevailing myth of Leonardo needs to be challenged is to follow Giovio by placing more emphasis on his appeal to rulers. Powerful people are often attracted by those who can charm and amuse them. This was something at which Leonardo was unusually adept, and we can be confident that it wasn’t his study of anatomy or optics or the composition of pigments that made him appealing, so much as his musical gifts, humour and inventiveness, as demonstrated by his mechanical lion.

Leonardo often carried out his investigations in far greater depth than he could possibly have needed as a painter. He wasn’t the first Florentine artist to study human anatomy, but none before or since seem to have done so with such intensity. His closest predecessor in the study of perspective was Brunelleschi, who had created a painted panel with a view of the Baptistery in Florence, which appeared to be a perfect illusion when seen through a small hole and reflected in a mirror. It was an impressive display of geometric skill, but of limited relevance to the practice of painting. The brain adjusts for binocular vision and variable viewpoints, so painters don’t need to apply the rules of perspective in such a rigorous way. Similarly, Leonardo’s extraordinarily detailed investigations of optics, including shadows and modelling, often don’t take into account the way pictures are experienced, nor do they reflect a wider change that was taking place in painting during his lifetime, with the emergence of a more sketchy type of brushwork. He certainly wouldn’t have been able to explain why we consider paintings by, say, Velázquez or Manet so convincing as representations of real people, often in real settings, or why painters can produce impressive and compelling works in very different styles. There is not one correct way of painting, yet this was what Leonardo implied in his notes. It was his extraordinary skill at drawing, especially in his less finished and more rapid sketches, that seems to have been particularly influential on other artists.

Campbell’s last chapter, on the works of Leonardo’s immediate followers and slightly later imitators, underlines the limitations of his ideas about painting as transmitted to his pupils, because their pictures are almost without exception dull and lifeless. Campbell’s general thesis, that we should think of Leonardo less as a unique genius and more as a product of his times, is convincing, but it is unlikely to be welcomed. Most people, including many art historians, prefer an attractive myth to prosaic reality.
