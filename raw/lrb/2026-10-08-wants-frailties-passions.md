# 正文

‘No period has writers who play more difficult games with fact and factuality than the Augustan,’ Barbara Everett wrote in 1985. ‘This helps to make Pope the most interesting and problematic of subjects for biography.’ It also helps to make him an interesting and problematic choice for editors, since any Collected Works involves a narrative of authorial progression and may therefore be construed as a biography of sorts. When the editor of such a collection is also its author, the other pertinent genre becomes autobiography. Pope, the most anxiously self-curating of writers, recognised as much when he issued what might have struck readers as a rather premature gathering of his own compositions in 1717. He was not yet thirty years old, but already a seasoned toyer with the facts. Recalling his early years and possible motives for choosing ‘the dangerous fate of authors’, he presents himself as a casual, accidental poet – someone to whom the preservation and editing of his own works would not come naturally. ‘I confess,’ he says (in what is anything but a confessional spirit), ‘it was want of consideration that made me an author; I writ because it amused me.’ A darker note emerges at the end of the preface: ‘In this office of collecting my pieces, I am altogether uncertain whether to look upon my self as a man building a monument, or burying the dead?’ Those two activities are not mutually exclusive: burying the dead might well be succeeded by erecting a monument to them and Pope liked to imagine that he was engaged in both activities in *The Dunciad*, the poem that occupied and disturbed the latter part of his life from its first emergence in 1728 until its last revision in 1743, a year before his death. He had notionally sought in his final mock-epic satirical attack on bad writing to ‘rid’ himself ‘of those insects’, as he described the swarm of inferior authors incessantly and (as he saw it) impertinently rushing into print in the early decades of the 18th century. But he needed the insects at least as much as they needed him.

The works that Pope selected for his 1717 collection ranged from early experiments in pastoral – traditionally the genre on which aspiring writers cut their teeth – to hitherto unpublished efforts in translation and elegy. One way of understanding this miscellaneous volume, the key foundational text in Henry Power’s edition, which seeks, as he explains, ‘to give a sense of Pope’s development as a writer’ – and to attest to what Howard Erskine-Hill called ‘a life of such concentrated diversity’ – is as an early anthology of his attitudes to women. Crucially, it predates the spectacularly nasty fallout from his quarrel with the aristocratic Lady Mary Wortley Montagu, an episode that saw them trading venomous insults (she was accused of being dirty, malicious and lewd; he was wretched, hateful, lower-class and deformed) and coloured later poems such as ‘The Characters of Women’ (1735). Its opening lines attribute to a female speaker the damning claim that ‘Most Women have no Characters at all’; by the late 1720s, the number of exceptions that Pope was prepared to admit to that rule was vanishingly small. His interest in women had shrunk to such an extent that – despite the fact that Dulness is represented as a dozy queen and a goddess – female authors are not even seen as worthy of sustained attack in *The Dunciad*. The 1717 *Works* take a very different view of the literary potential of women. This volume includes the full and final version of *The Rape of the Lock* as well as ‘Verses to the Memory of an Unfortunate Lady’, two poetic epistles ‘To a Young Lady’ and (as the concluding work) ‘Eloisa to Abelard’. None of these is in any conventional sense a love poem; that is, even when the speaker can be assumed to be male, he does not write to or about a female subject as the direct object of his own attraction or devotion. *The Rape of the Lock* is an elevated ‘heroi-comical’ celebration of female beauty and social combat; the ‘Verses to the Memory of an Unfortunate Lady’ are an imaginative exercise in tender and vengeful regret for the suicide of a young woman; the epistles ‘To a Young Lady’ are lightly sociable and charmingly immediate rather than longing, demanding or despairing addresses; ‘Eloisa to Abelard’, although passionately incarnating the conflict between religion and desire, is in the voice of a 12th-century female sufferer rather than an enraptured or censorious male onlooker. Taken together, these compositions demonstrate an extraordinary imaginative and emotional commitment, not always sympathetic, to enter into the minds and hearts of women.

What did Pope find when he got there? In *The Rape of the Lock*, Belinda’s mind is a vague jumble, her heart mysteriously penetrated by an unknown lover. She is so resplendently gorgeous that almost nobody can survive unscathed the experience of looking at her. Pope’s treatment of the poem’s central crime – which was founded on a real incident, the theft of a lock of the heroine’s hair – is delicate, funny and constantly surprising. By translating the realm of aristocratic female experience into and out of the idiom and conventions of Homeric epic, he makes the characters seem at once larger than life and less than human. Belinda, arming herself with cosmetics and jewels at her dressing table, really does seem about to enter a realm that is as fraught with danger and opportunities for glory as the world of Achilles or Odysseus. The vulnerabilities and transience of female beauty are contrasted with the poem’s capacity to outlast and commemorate them; at the end, the lock shoots into the sky, a radiant trail, and Belinda herself is addressed. She may not have found the conclusion entirely reassuring:

> When, after millions slain, yourself shall die;  
> When those fair suns shall set, as set they must,  
> And all those tresses shall be laid in dust;  
> This lock, the Muse shall consecrate to fame,  
> And ’midst the stars inscribe Belinda’s name!

The speaker, asserting his power over his subject, promises to confer on the heroine an immortality that she seems otherwise incapable of achieving, whether through religious devotion (she has several Bibles but prefers to read love letters; the glittering cross she wears is intended to make other people adore her rather than to indicate obedience to God) or by submitting to marriage and motherhood (Belinda shows very little interest in either of these things). *The Rape of the Lock* is coyly suggestive of the physical realities of womanhood; inhabiting a milieu that is beguilingly superficial, Belinda wishes, despairingly, that the Baron had taken hairs from somewhere – anywhere – ‘less in Sight’ than her head. In a later rendition of Horace, Pope refers to one such place as the ‘hoary Shrine’, phrasing which, as Power explains in a typically excellent note, is considerably more decorous than the original: ‘*cunnus albus*, which translates literally as “white cunt” – though in Horace’s poem *albus* probably means “clothed in white” (so, the “cunt of a married woman”)’. Pope was keen enough on his image of female ‘Fore-buttocks to the navel bare’ to use it not only in this poem (‘The Second Satire of the First Book of Horace’) but also in *The Dunciad*, where it is applied to the writer Eliza Haywood. It seems in both cases to have been intended as an amusing reference to exposed cleavage rather than genitalia.

Pope dextrously conjures a world of facts and experiences in which the rules of engagement are complex and exacting. Here, alliances are repeatedly tested and may suddenly dissolve, and one false move can result in expulsion from the charmed circle:

> With varying vanities, from ev’ry part,  
> They shift the moving Toyshop of their heart;  
> Where Wigs with Wigs, with Sword-knots Sword-knots strive,  
> Beaus banish Beaus, and Coaches Coaches drive.

This was a hyper-real, brilliantly accelerated version of Pope’s everyday social routine: one involving game-playing, combat and strenuous effort, as tightly bound as the closed heroic couplets in which he rehearsed and accentuated the qualities of what he observed around him, or as the rigid corset without which he was unable to stand or sit up straight. His family had interpreted the physical symptoms which began to manifest themselves in his teenage years as the consequence of too much reading and writing. They were, in fact, indications of Pott’s disease, or tuberculosis of the bone, which Pope probably contracted as a baby from his wet nurse’s milk. The condition arrested his growth and provoked fevers, eye inflammations, debilitating headaches and disorders of the lungs and heart. It rendered him, in the words of one biographer, ‘a dwarf and cripple’. It also left him increasingly dependent on the ministrations of women, as Samuel Johnson (another victim of his wet nurse) recorded in his *Life of Pope* in 1781, drawing on the testimony of ‘a female domestick’ who had known Pope ‘perhaps after the middle of life’:

> He was then so weak as to stand in perpetual need of female attendance; extremely sensible of cold, so that he wore a kind of fur doublet, under a shirt of very coarse warm linen with fine sleeves. When he rose, he was invested in bodice made of stiff canvass, being scarce able to hold himself erect till they were laced, and he then put on a flannel waistcoat. One side was contracted. His legs were so slender that he enlarged their bulk with three pair of stockings, which were drawn on and off by the maid; for he was not able to dress or undress himself, and neither went to bed nor rose without help. His weakness made it very difficult for him to be clean.

If these were primarily the afflictions of his later years, Pope had to contend from the start with another severe disadvantage: his Catholicism. The Penal Laws remained in force throughout his lifetime, imposing restrictions on property ownership and excluding Catholics from public office, voting, a university education and most professions. In such a context, as the only child of late parents who were far from wealthy, poetry may not have seemed a vocation so much as the only way out. The denial of full property rights to Catholics helps to explain why Pope lavished so much attention and affection on the villa and gardens he built at Twickenham (on a plot of leased land), in which he lived from 1719 until his death. He deployed the striking adjective ‘home-felt’ more than once in his work; it sounds as if it was made for him, but it wasn’t his coinage (its first recorded occurrence is in 1637 in Milton’s *Comus*). Pope places the word in this verse paragraph from *An Essay on Man* (1733-34), his unfinished inquiry into human knowledge, the foundations of society and the possibility of happiness:

> Heav’n, forming each on other to depend,  
> A master, or a servant, or a friend,  
> Bids each on other for assistance call,  
> Till one man’s weakness grows the strength of all.  
> Wants, frailties, passions, closer still ally  
> The common int’rest, or endear the tye:  
> To *these* we owe true friendship, love sincere,  
> Each home-felt joy that life inherits here:  
> Yet from the same we learn, in its decline,  
> Those joys, those loves, those int’rests to resign.  
> Taught half by reason, half by mere decay,  
> To welcome death, and calmly pass away.

The passage strives to represent individual debility, including the physical kind with which Pope was forcibly acquainted, as a collective force for good: our reliance on one another is beneficial to human society. Yet there is something unconvincing about the way the argument is packaged here. It doesn’t take much to imagine that human ‘wants, frailties, passions’ might do something other than bind us more closely together, even in the prime of life. And the process by which our existence is imagined to be calmly resigned, and death welcomed, seems at once sketchily described and too swiftly or easily achieved to be plausible. Johnson, ever fearful of death and suspecting the poem’s complacency, dismissed it: ‘Never were penury of knowledge and vulgarity of sentiment so happily disguised.’

The theory might seem hard to swallow, but in practical terms the interdependence of master, servant and friend is vividly and immediately brought to life in the dynamic opening lines of a slightly later poem, *An Epistle to Dr Arbuthnot* (1735): ‘Shut, shut the door, good *John*! fatigu’d I said,/Tye up the knocker, say I’m sick, I’m dead.’ Although the title informs us that Pope’s close friend Dr John Arbuthnot is the addressee, and although the poem was published in order to commemorate that friendship in the last months of Arbuthnot’s life, it is quite another John – Pope’s gardener – who is the recipient of the speaker’s most urgent directives. The *Epistle* displays for Arbuthnot’s inspection a treasured and embattled domesticity, as Pope fends off droves of amateur writers who court his favour, patronage and cash; with good John barring his door against invaders, he shores up his allegiances, teases and condemns his critics and defends the pursuit of satire (a pursuit about which Arbuthnot had warned him). If the speaker’s boasts of his own humility, purity of motive and filial devotion are difficult to stomach – and sit oddly alongside the intermittent lashings out at his enemies – the avowals of friendship which bind the poem together ring true.

Another form of human co-operation can be observed throughout Pope’s translations and imitations of classical texts, works that formed a large part not only of his training but of his poetic character and cultural allegiance. Power, whose *Homer-Haunted: The Many Afterlives of an Ancient Poet* also appeared this year, includes in his edition a generous selection of these intimately responsive and allusive works, and is amply qualified both to annotate them and to suggest their broader significance.[\*](/the-paper/v48/n18/footnotes/wants-frailties-passions-footnote-1) He follows the example of the 1717 *Works* by including some of Pope’s short, freestanding translations from Greek and Latin, alongside the better-known early poems, in order to stress the perpetual interdependence of the two kinds of composition – even the ‘modern’ works are saturated in ancient references and images – and to show the poet in training for the translation of the *Iliad* (1715-20) that made his name and fortune.

For an editor, much of the interest and many of the problems associated with Pope derive from the fact that he revised and amplified his works, producing multiple versions of what is notionally the same composition and presenting obvious difficulties for an edition that seeks, as Power’s does, to present his writings chronologically. Which text is the best to include and at what point in the edition? *The Rape of the Lock* was first published anonymously in 1712 in a miscellaneous collection of works by various hands. Two years later it appeared as a separate publication under Pope’s own name, having expanded from its original two cantos into five. For its inclusion in the 1717 *Works* Pope made further changes and the major addition of Clarissa’s brilliant but ineffectual speech to Belinda, recommending common sense and good humour and modelled on the warrior Sarpedon’s speech to Glaucus in the *Iliad*. No right-minded editor would prefer the first version of the poem to the last, wonderful performance. But Pope was still describing it in 1717, on the title page, as ‘*Written in the Year 1712*’. As he explains in his ‘Note on the Texts and Their Ordering’, Power has generally chosen to present Pope’s compositions ‘in the order of their first appearance’. This would seem to favour the inclusion of the 1712 text of the poem, or the retention of the year 1712 as the date when it first made its mark on the world. However, Power understandably considers *The Rape of the Lock* to have been ‘substantially reshaped’ after its initial publication, to the extent that it became a new text, and argues that it would therefore ‘not make sense to present the five-canto version … as a work of 1712’.

The resulting placement of the poem, which was revised both in 1714 and 1717, is not entirely satisfactory. It appears just after ‘Part of the Thirteenth Book of Homer’s *Odysses*’ (1713) and before a selection of passages from the *Iliad* dating from 1715 to 1720 – so, presumably, it is meant to be situated in an imagined 1714, when the five-canto version was first published, but without the speech from Clarissa which is included here. After the *Iliad* group comes ‘Preface to the Works of 1717’, the same preface that introduces the final text of *The Rape of the Lock*, but in this edition it is found ninety pages after that poem. (The two-canto, 1712 text of *The Rape of the Lock* is the only one of Pope’s works to be included in this edition as an appendix; it could legitimately have occupied a 1712 slot in the contents and main text, but must have been thought to be of only supplementary interest.) Such are the inherently maddening forms of compromise and accommodation with which an editor is contractually occupied.

Pope himself loved the strenuous game of editing, not only in the sense of compiling and introducing his own works but also as exemplified in his practice of absorbing and impersonating the hostile forms of criticism that routinely met his publications. By pre-emptively striving to make a poem’s reception part of its emergence into the world, he aspired to control the life cycle of a work and indeed the business of authorship. His mock-learned alter ego, Martinus Scriblerus, pre-dates *The Dunciad* and was a collaborative invention, dating from around 1714 and involving contributions from Jonathan Swift, John Gay, Arbuthnot and others. They contrived the persona in order to ridicule the follies and abuses of scholarship: Scriblerus is pedantic and myopic and, as *The Dunciad* expanded from three books to four, the apparatus occupied by his commentary – introductory essays, reviews and advertisements, as well as gigantic ‘remarks’ in footnotes that frequently occupy more space on the page than the lines they are notionally explaining – became ever larger. The modern reader is likely to find such material both exhausting and bewildering, and some of it has been judiciously omitted from this edition – as has the entirety of the 1728 *Dunciad* … *in Three Books* (other than its title page). But, as Power makes clear, the prominence of the explanatory content is central to the argument Pope is making about the fatal decline of literature and culture. It helps us to understand what the poem is about. Assuming the appearance of a classical text, accompanied by lavish mock-scholarly annotations, *The Dunciad* not only describes but enacts ‘the death of epic’ itself:

> Lo! thy dread Empire, chaos! is restor’d;  
> Light dies before thy uncreating word:  
> Thy hand, great Anarch! lets the curtain fall;  
> And Universal Darkness buries All.

Pope liked to cast himself as a beacon of good sense and as the regulator of social, moral and literary impropriety. He could also be slippery, impish, cruel and deceptive. Hybrids and mixtures always fascinated him: people and creatures whom he liked to sum up as ‘equivocal’ and who therefore seemed to affront the clear distinctions between high and low, male and female, good and bad, to which his poetry was officially committed. His works are full of the voices and characters of other people, friends and enemies, the living and the dead, and his writings are intensely committed to ideas of companionship and community. They are also intensely involved with psychologically astute visions of solitude and abandonment. Around a third of the works included in this edition might be classified as letters of some kind, either because they are directly addressed or dedicated to someone or because they possess the formal title of an ‘epistle’. But some of these seemingly outward-facing works might be better classed as missives from the author to himself. Power includes two versions of a letter written by Pope to the dying Arbuthnot in 1734 – one of them was sent; the other (an enlarged and more formal text) was printed – and suggests that the differences between those versions are ‘instructive’. It is not that one is necessarily more sincere than the other, but the published text devotes a great deal more room to Pope’s defence of his own character as a writer and specifically as a satirist who targets real, living people, arguing that to avoid attacking individuals deprives literature of its limited capacity to change the world:

> The only sign by which I found my writings ever did any good, or had any weight, has been that they rais’d the anger of bad men. And my greatest comfort, and encouragement to proceed, has been to see, that those who have no shame, and no fear, of any thing else, have appear’d touch’d by my Satires.

To be ‘touch’d’ in this way, as many of the targets of *The Dunciad* undoubtedly were – they were either deterred or prevented from publishing, and therefore, in some cases, could no longer earn a living – may well be counted an accomplishment. But if we are to take seriously Pope’s persistent claim that moral virtue mattered far more to him than his reputation as a poet, then it is the unpublished text of the letter to Arbuthnot, the one its recipient actually read, with its simple emphasis on sending good wishes to the doctor’s family and the hope that they received the venison Pope had requested for them, that ought to weigh more heavily. It is a touching document in an altogether different sense – a home-felt declaration of love.
