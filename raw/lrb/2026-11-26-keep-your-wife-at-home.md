In his​ *Journey to Armenia* (1933), Osip Mandelstam takes a side-excursion into French painting. He reveres Cézanne (‘Good old grandfather! A demon for work. The best acorn in the forests of France’); seems to admire Van Gogh (‘I never saw such barking colours’); and relishes Pissarro’s depiction of ‘raspberry-grey boulevards’ with their ‘little boxes of hansom cabs, their fishing pole whips pitched on their shoulders and the shreds of splashed brain on the kiosks and chestnut trees’. The only major painter he dislikes is Matisse: ‘A rich man’s artist … Enough already of this carpet chess and odalisques! Persian whimsies of a Parisian painter!’ There are passing dabs of praise for Monet and Signac; also for Renoir, of whom he writes: ‘Looking at Renoir’s water you feel blisters on your palm as though you’d been rowing.’

This is a most peculiar compliment. Rivers and lakes, like much else in Renoir, are serene: in the background to *Luncheon of the Boating Party* or *Oarsmen at Chatou*, or beneath the two behatted ladies in *The Skiff*. There is just enough wind to fill a yacht’s sails, and to give a gentle swirl to the summer’s water. But Renoir’s name doesn’t make you think of chafing oarsmanship (the Impressionist of blisters and aching hands is Caillebotte, whose work Mandelstam is unlikely to have seen). We associate Renoir rather with sun-drenched languor, with women’s smiles and women’s bodies. He was a celebrant of life, pleasure and peacefulness. He loved Corot and particularly loathed Millet: ‘His sentimental peasants made me think of actors dressed up to look like peasants … [His] *Angelus* has done more to set people against religion than all the speeches of the Commune put together.’ Though he used working-class female models, it was for their bodies and availability, not their social background. In the sumptuous catalogue to the National Gallery’s show, *Renoir and Love*, only one painting makes you pause, if briefly, over the social class of its participants: *Mussel-Fishers at Berneval* (1879). A female mussel-fisher, with two baskets on her back, turns to look at the three mites – one with a couple of baskets of his own – who accompany her. But one glance at their good clothes, decent shoes and charming smiles is enough to make us suspect that these are middle-class children sent out to play at being mussel-fishers. (Except that it turns out to be a little more complicated than this: the three children were in fact the offspring of a local fisherman, so it seems a little dressing-up box gentrification must have been applied to them.)

There is nothing wrong with this: Renoir played to his strengths and interests and, also, his commissions. He knew what he wanted to do, and what he didn’t. He painted close to home, both physically and psychologically: ‘Give me an apple tree in a suburban garden. I haven’t the slightest need of Niagara Falls.’ As for mountains, God created them to be ‘a background, as in Giorgione’. But it does mean that of all the major Impressionists Renoir is perhaps the one we take most for granted and who has least shifted in public and critical standing. Cézanne remains Olympian; Monet has grown greater and more complicated; Caillebotte has surged into view; Mary Cassatt and Berthe Morisot have been foregrounded; while Bazille has poked his head above water again. But Renoir’s reputation, like those of Pissarro, the theorist of the group, and Sisley, has remained, if not exactly stuck, at least settled, which leaves him open to be patronised. His greatest paintings are safely in big museums; there are few likely surprises at auction; no biographical revelations are expected; occasionally his paintings are stolen (most recently, from his last house and studio in Cagnes), which brings his name into the papers. Perhaps his watercolours and pastels are under-known. The only new Renoir story – and about his subjects, not Renoir himself – is the anguished one told by Edmund de Waal in *Letters to Camondo* (2021) and now in greater detail by Catherine Ostler in *The Renoir Girls*.[\*](/the-paper/v48/n21/footnotes/keep-your-wife-at-home-footnote-1) In 1881 Renoir painted *Pink and Blue*, a double portrait of the two youngest daughters of Louis and Louise Cahen d’Anvers, who belonged to the Parisian Jewish gratin. Alice and Elisabeth are shown in their party finery. But the girls’ mother took an immediate dislike to the painting; she banished it first to a maid’s room and then, in 1909, sold it to the Bernheims. Further, she and her husband made Renoir wait for his money, which infuriated him into antisemitic disgruntlement. ‘I am washing my hands of the Jews,’ he wrote to a friend (though he didn’t). And by a brute irony, Elisabeth, the little girl in blue, so redolent of the Belle Époque, found herself 63 years later – then 69 and in fragile health – being picked up by the Gestapo and interned at Drancy; she died either at, or on the way to, Auschwitz. *Pink and Blue* is now in the São Paulo Museum of Art.

In 1919, the dealer René Gimpel asked his colleague Paul Durand-Ruel if Monet was well off. ‘Much less so than Renoir,’ came the reply, ‘who alone is really wealthy, and who in addition has an important collection of his own works.’ But if Renoir was the richest Impressionist, he didn’t behave like it – Monet, with his private chef, furs and expensive motor cars, performed more richly. Renoir was the most prolific artist of the group: he is estimated to have painted about six thousand works, four thousand of which were sold almost at once. This is far more than anyone else in his immediate circle: Monet painted two thousand or so, Pissarro about fifteen hundred, Sisley about a thousand, Morisot just over four hundred. He was much less destructive of his own work than Monet; though also more careless of it, lighting the fire with pencil sketches, leaving behind paintings at places where he had worked, giving them away to servants and offering them in lieu of payment for meals and lodging.

In an oeuvre of such a size, repetition is inevitable, and nowhere more so than in Renoir’s depiction of women. Despite his period views on them, he was deeply uxorious, almost certainly faithful to his wife, and ‘crushed’ when she died. But her presence, and her looks, dominate his female portraits. Jean Renoir gives us an idea of the physical proportions his father considered ideal in a face:

> The eyes should be halfway between the top of the head and the tip of the chin. If the upper half of the head was too big, in his view it was the sign of an enlarged brain – ‘the brain of a megalomaniac, or, more simply, of an intellectual; not to mention water on the brain’. If the top half of a face was too small, it signified good, honest stupidity. If the lower part was too pronounced, it meant stubbornness … He liked small noses better than large ones, and he made no secret of his preference for wide mouths, with full, but not thick, lips; small teeth; light complexions; and blonde hair.

When he met Aline Charigot, ‘the proportions of her features and body accorded with the canons Renoir had formulated.’ So he had been painting her even before she began sitting for him. And while this look does not infect his portraits of upper-crust women, it is everywhere when he paints those in his circle: there is even a portrait of his son Pierre with exactly those particularities (though admittedly with black hair). Jean wrote that his father ‘liked women who had a tendency to grow fat’. Which Aline duly and faithfully did. And therefore the women he portrayed did the same until they became almost parodic: women as pneumatic as Michelin men, with puffed rolls of fat ascending their torsos at regular intervals. Not all were convinced by this development. Cassatt deplored what she crisply referred to as ‘enormously fat red women with very small heads’.

Renoir was much loved, and much praised, right from the beginning. Fantin-Latour, then at the height of his fame, visited the Atelier Gleyre and made no secret of his admiration for the young student, ‘whose virtuosity harks back to the Italian Renaissance’. While decades later, Cézanne, in a letter of July 1902, wrote: ‘I despise all living painters, except Monet and Renoir.’ And despite coming from the poorest background of all the Impressionists, Renoir seems to have got on well with his clients from the higher social spheres, who clearly valued his depictions of them. But he didn’t please everyone. Jacques-Émile Blanche, when a promising young painter, wanted to take lessons from one of the many great artists he knew socially. Manet would have been perfect, had he not just died; Degas, a good friend of his father’s, was his next choice, but Degas didn’t take the young man seriously as a painter, thinking him no more than a gifted amateur. So his final option was Renoir, who had previously painted panels for the Blanche family house in Dieppe. However, Jacques-Émile’s fiercely protective, not to say gorgonesque, mother refused to countenance it. She had judged Renoir scruffy, witless, uncouth and vulgar, writing to her husband in July 1881: ‘He is so crazy in his painting, in his conversation, without any education, no doubt very good but hating anything healthy in life.’ This of one of the most joyful, life-celebrating painters of his age, then at the height of his powers.

In his turn, Renoir could be an equally sharp disliker and disapprover. He hated: bread cut in slices (as opposed to torn); chandelier covers; faux-Renaissance furniture; spa towns with bandstands; the productions of Meissen, Sèvres and Gobelins; the trompe-l’oeil earthenware of Bernard Palissy (a dislike shared more than a century later by Anita Brookner); the ‘evil influence of Romanticism’; public displays of affection (‘It won’t last long – they’re waving it about too much’); wine connoisseurs; Beethoven (who was ‘indecent’ for telling us about himself); tourists; intellectuals; Wagner, both the man and his ‘boring’ music (they had met in Palermo, when the composer had got on his nerves, and ‘rubbed [him up] the wrong way’ by having opinions about painting); Guimard’s Paris Métro entrances; all ‘literary’ painting; local colour (‘always an invention of tourists’); and the very label of Impressionism. He disliked the colour yellow, thinking of it as ‘an enfeebled red’ (the National Gallery show confirms this, offering only some pallid straw hats, a pair of beige gloves and some ochre wainscoting). He abhorred all modern architects, especially Viollet-le-Duc, who had wrecked both Notre Dame and Rouen Cathedral, and ‘vandals’ such as Haussmann. Of recent buildings, he consistently loathed the Opéra Garnier (‘To think that the Germans missed it with their Big Berthas!’). Though generally polite and well-mannered, he could also be delightfully caustic. The art dealer Ambroise Vollard once wrote a monograph on the painter, and insisted on reading the full text aloud to him. Vollard complained afterwards that Renoir didn’t seem to be giving it his proper attention: he had ‘listened only absent-mindedly’. But in fact he was on full alert, subsequently commenting: ‘Not bad, Vollard’s book on Vollard.’

The title of the National Gallery show, *Renoir and Love*, is nicely, and perhaps deliberately, provocative. We might expect Renoir and Pleasure, Renoir and Sensuality, Renoir and Beauty, even Renoir and Lunch. But Renoir and Love? From the age of thirteen to eighteen, he worked in a porcelain factory, painting designs. According to Jean: ‘During those five years, Renoir was to learn the essentials of life: art and love. As regards the second item, I should perhaps have said more appropriately, “women”.’ And what were Renoir’s thoughts on this subject? ‘Love is made up of a great many things and I’m not clever enough to explain them.’ ‘I loved women even before I learned to walk.’ ‘I can’t stand having anybody around me but women.’ ‘I like women best when they don’t know how to read’ – a remark which, with quaint filial loyalty, Jean parses as ‘another example of his revolt against accepted values’.

But, while admiring them, he was, of course, aware of women’s faults: their slavish obsession with fashion, their love of ‘narrow shoes and high heels’ and their inability to leave their hair alone. Although he adored certain of their appurtenances – Suzanne Valadon called him ‘a hat nymphomaniac’ – he didn’t like educated women (‘I can’t see myself getting into bed with a lawyer’); indeed, he thought that what women gained by education, they lost in other fields: ‘I am afraid that the generations to come won’t know how to make love well.’ He valued the physical exertion involved in tasks such as floor-scrubbing, fire-laying and doing the washing, which moved women’s bellies in ways that were absolutely necessary; the want of such exercise would produce ‘girls incapable of enjoying sexual intercourse to the full’. Women who went out to work were repugnant to him: ‘If you ever marry, keep your wife at home. Her real business is to look after you and your children, if you have any.’ And he was against the bottle-feeding of children because it produced men ‘lacking all the gentler feelings’. Another dictum: ‘Don’t trust anybody who doesn’t get excited at the sight of a pretty breast.’

If this makes him sound like a typical French lecher, we must also take into account that he was generally very proper – he even addressed his models as ‘vous’ – extremely shy about his feelings and afraid of being ‘sentimental’. He strongly approved of Cézanne’s daughter-in-law Renée, who was full of the joys of living, and added by way of explication: ‘She even makes mistakes in spelling, and in my opinion that’s essential in a woman.’ Not one of these remarks is quoted in the catalogue to *Renoir and Love*, perhaps because they make him sound too pungently a man of his time. However, the curators do cite his response to his wife when she expressed her jealousy of the naked models bedizening his studio: ‘I paint them just as if I were painting carrots.’ Whether his wife – let alone his models – felt any better on hearing this is unresolved. And it also provokes a further question: did Renoir ever paint a carrot?

His pictures show many aspects of contemporary life – the merry camaraderie of cafés, dance halls, restaurants and opera boxes, the flirting and the dancing, with much drinking and smoking, by women as well as men – but not of modern (industrial, mechanical) life. There are no motor cars, no factory chimneys belching in the distance, no noisy threats to this green, pleasant and largely tranquil world. ‘I am of the 18th century,’ Renoir said to Gimpel, citing Watteau, Fragonard and Hubert Robert as his ancestors. But whereas Watteau’s characters were embarking for Cythera, Renoir’s took the boat to Guernsey. He is a painter of dappled sunlight on flesh and fabric, some of the dapplings as large as pancakes. There are numerous small, well-behaved dogs (even a poodle with a wooden leg, in his early *Mother Antony’s Tavern*). And there’s no getting away from that double-edged adjective ‘charm’. His pictures charm, the people within them charm, and the children he painted charm the most – and don’t they know it (see the little poppet with her hoop at the front of *The Umbrellas*). There is not a satirical, let alone cynical, brushstroke in his entire oeuvre. But at the same time, do they steer clear of that single-edged and Renoir-feared adjective ‘sentimental’? They may be truthful, but are they not also emotionally manipulative? And if not at the time, then later? Such art can, over subsequent generations, begin to provoke a retrospective sentimentality. Here is a world in which – if it truly existed and we had an entry ticket – we might have been happy to live ourselves. Ostler quotes Colin Bailey, director of the Morgan Library: ‘No other artist of the 19th century bequeathed such an enduring and endearing image of elegance, comfort and prosperity – one that, as Proust noted, was not to be found in the canvases of any of the more fashionable artists of the Third Republic.’ Did Renoir think that this was his ambition? Quite possibly yes. ‘For me, a painting must be something amiable, joyful and pretty, yes pretty!’ he said. ‘There are enough annoying things in life for us not to make more of them.’

There is no bad weather in Renoir. Even in *The Umbrellas* there is not a damp hair or pavement to be seen (the sky even has patches of blue), and the umbrellas might as well be parasols. Compare Caillebotte’s *Paris Street; Rainy Day,* where the stretches of wet cobblestones dully gleam (in Paul Theroux’s phrase) like ‘glazed loaftops’. Renoir also shied away from depicting poverty. And, therefore, he disliked the work of Jean-François Raffaëlli: ‘But you ought to like him,’ a friend said. ‘He painted the poor.’ To which Renoir replied: ‘That is precisely why I have my doubts about him. In painting there are no poor.’ Which could mean: the act of painting the poor raises them above their poverty and ascribed class (as in the case of the mussel-fisher’s children); or it could mean that depicting the poor in their outward appearance is a misdepiction, since they have the same qualities and originality as other people, but lack only money. So the remark could be evasive and self-serving, or a powerful defence of the poor. Renoir profoundly disagreed with Zola’s vision of *les bas-fonds*. Where the writer saw only misery, corruption and brutishness, the painter saw ordinary people struggling to get along, and to get by. Artistic vision depends as much on temperament as on scientific observation. One of the period’s most famous images is Degas’s *The Absinthe Drinker* (1875-76), which shows a slumped woman in a café, with her glass of ‘the green fairy’ in front of her, and a doleful expression as vacant as her addict’s head. Degas’s model was the actress Ellen Andrée. But three years later she was to be the model for Renoir’s *After the Luncheon* (1879), where she becomes a radiant young beauty with beringed fingers, a spectacular hat and classy company, about to raise a tiny glass of digestif to her smiling lips.

Though Renoir is the portrayer of affectionate conviviality, the participants in many of his pictures, whether dancing or courting or lunching, often aren’t looking at one another directly. In the quiet, intimate *Conversation* (1879), a young woman sits with her back to us, her joined hands making a kind of sluice to carry her argument towards her scholarly listener. He is paying attention to what she is saying, while looking slightly past the top of her head. (This is the opposite of indifference: some people concentrate better on what is being said when not making eye contact.) And on a larger scale, consider the *Luncheon of the Boating Party,* which features ‘artists, models, actresses, poets, millionaires and boatmen’. J.K. Huysmans, reviewing the picture in the Seventh Impressionist Exhibition of 1882, made a joyous category mistake, assuming that the picture showed only boatmen and prostitutes. And so he complained that the painting ‘doesn’t smell strong enough; his whores are chic and merry, but they don’t give off the odour of a Parisian whore; these are springtime whores freshly disembarked from London.’

The fourteen figures shown are at the bill-paying stage of a meal at Maison Fournaise, a restaurant on the Seine, and are enjoying a complex series of interactions in couples and threesomes. Renoir found it so difficult that he said it would be his ‘last big painting’ (it wasn’t). However, while it looks like a depiction of busy, indeed extreme, sociability, this is only true of the mid and background. The three largest figures, in the foreground, are all very deliberately *not* making contact with one another. On the left, a beefy boatman looks leftwards, over the heads of the lunchers, as if longing to get back to his boat. Next to him, a young woman (Renoir’s future wife, Aline) is nose-to-nose with her hairy dog Kiki. On the right, another fellow, dressed as a boatman, sits astride a back-to-front chair, a cigarette between thumb and forefinger, gazing outwards, looking bored. Is he merely relaxing with nicotine? Is he tired of the pretty girl whose main emotional interest seems to be her dog? Is he itching to get back out on the water and gain some blisters? Or – wait a minute – is he, perhaps, gazing homoerotically at his fellow *canotier?* He has apparently been identified ‘since the 1890s’ (from an anonymous scribble) as Caillebotte, even though he doesn’t look especially like the self-portraits and photographs we have of the artist. Who recently, as in the 2024-25 d’Orsay show *Caillebotte: Peindre les hommes*, has been lightly outed as gay or gay-ish.

There’s no actual evidence in either the Renoir or Caillebotte archives, and the catalogue is more than cautious: ‘Whether or not the model actually was Caillebotte, the fact that the sitter has been identified as him since the 1890s is significant.’ But sexual retrofitting is à la mode, and this is what it looks like if you tap your nose knowingly. If we decide that the bored chap in the boating outfit is gazing at Monsieur Beefy (roughly – the trajectory of his eye seems to me lower) rather than into thin air, that sort of settles it, doesn’t it? Which leaves us only with the question of why Renoir, in the midst of this massively complicated picture, should decide to insert a sly coded reference to Caillebotte’s possible homoeroticism. Similarly, of *The Skiff*, which shows two women in a rowing boat on tranquil waters, the catalogue wonders: ‘Is this an allusion to lesbianism?’ Why might it be so? Well, because Maupassant evoked lesbianism ‘in his novel *La Femme de Paul*’. Which is in fact a 17-page short story, first published six years after the picture was painted. And don’t forget, Aline was fond of rowing (though not, as far as we know, of lesbianism).

The Impressionists, like any new artistic group that defies traditional art education and traditional expectations, were naturally attacked and mocked: by critics, snobs, Salon-going know-somethings and newspaper cartoonists; by painters of other schools and conservative aesthetes like Edmond de Goncourt, whose love of the 18th century didn’t soften him in any way towards Renoir and his ilk. Goncourt records in his journal a discussion with the smoky Symbolist Eugène Carrière about ‘Pissarro and others’. Carrière’s verdict: ‘It’s target-practice art … it’s painting you have to view from fifty paces.’ In 1890, Goncourt spent an afternoon with the industrialist and art collector Camille Groult, who specialised in English art, in particular

> those paintings which were fundamental to the entire French painting school of 1830 … Oh! Constable, the great, the greatest master! … and among these pictures, a Turner: an ethereally blueish lake, its contours undefined, and a distant lake beneath a sudden electric burst of sunlight, at the end of some wild landscape. Good Lord! How this makes one despise the originality of Monet and those other so-called ‘originals’.

But Monet would repeatedly deny that Turner had the slightest influence on him. In 1900, painting London from the sixth floor of the Savoy Hotel, he told the London correspondent of *Le Figaro* that Turner’s work was ‘antipathetic to him because of the exuberant romanticism of his fantasy’.

*Renoir and Love* contains as good a selection of Renoir’s works, though only 45 of them, as possessiveness and lending protocols permit (only two of the three large dancing couples, and, alas, no *Luncheon of the Boating Party*). But the dating of them is significant. The earliest is *Mother Antony’s Tavern* of 1866; the latest *Les Grandes Baigneuses* of 1884-87*.* So, just his first twenty years or so. Renoir began to make serious money from 1890, as the art market became a money market; this was also the point by which he had mostly stopped depicting men. Thereafter, it was children and women until he died in 1919, still producing, despite his failing health. His flower paintings were much prized, and America (where Durand-Ruel opened a gallery in 1888) was always kind to him. It also contained one inveterate supporter: Albert Barnes of Philadelphia. Renoir was Barnes’s favourite artist and his collection contained far more works by him (180 in total) than by any other artist: what Barnes’s biographer Blake Gopnik drily describes as ‘an array of topless Renoirs’. After Renoir’s death, Barnes descended on Cagnes like some ambulance-chasing aesthete. He claimed (though he was a great exaggerator) to have been shown eight hundred paintings, but his offer to buy all or any of them was rebuffed by Renoir’s children.

Chronologically, *Renoir and Love* ends with *Les Grandes Baigneuses*, the strangest of paintings. It shows five nude figures, all female. On the left, two adults pose on a bank; on the right, two children play in the water. Joining the two halves, an older girl threatens to splash the two women, one of whom starts to pull a wrap around her, while the other responds with an almost theatrical fright-gesture. Jean Renoir wrote that his father was ‘one minute an impenitent Impressionist’ and the next ‘an intransigent classicist’, and this painting could be seen as an attempt to combine the two: classical stasis on the left and Impressionist movement on the right. But the problem lies with the two adult women, who are in a condition not so much of classical stasis as of freeze-frame paralysis; especially the immediate splashee, who is less classical than preposterous. She was based on a figure in a 17th-century lead frieze in Versailles, but bizarrely the lead figure looks the livelier, the painted one the more leaden. Perhaps the problem was a self-conscious awareness of challenging the old masters: with this picture in mind, Renoir wrote to his friend Paul Bérard: ‘I still believe that I am capable of knocking Raphael off his pedestal.’ His dealer, Durand-Ruel, hated it, while Pissarro’s sympathetic but damning verdict was: ‘I appreciate how much effort Renoir has put into this, and it’s all well and good to want to move forward, but he has limited his concerns to the line and his figures are detached from each other, without any consideration for their relationship.’ And it’s impossible, if a trifle unfair, to forget that within ten years Cézanne would be starting on his own, far more radical realisations of *Les Grandes Baigneuses*, ‘moving forward’ tremendously.
