# 正文

Missouri Williams’s​ novels are vicious, lurid and raucously nihilistic. Her first book, *The Doloriad* (2022), is set outside a ruined Prague, where an incestuous clan – the only known survivors of an unspecified apocalyptic event – scramble in the dirt. At first four people remained: the Matriarch, her brother, the brother’s wife and a legless schoolmaster. The Matriarch convinced her brother (and somehow his childless wife) that the siblings should procreate and repopulate the earth. By the time the novel begins, she presides over a small empire. There are her children with her brother, and the children her children had together. (The brother’s wife has had the good sense to die.) Meanwhile, the schoolmaster secretly builds a mountainous nest of garbage in which he incubates the moths he hopes will eventually devour him.

A teenager called Agathe, whose perspective we follow for much of the novel, scrutinises her siblings, who are tilling scorched fields:

> They were flocked by their own children, who resembled not [her brother and father] Jan but his younger siblings, many of them, like Dolores and Agathe, lacking some limb or quality, they were born without hands, feet, legs, or the upper portion of their lips, and were prone to strange fits and visions, moody and withdrawn … these were weak hopeless copies, burning with borrowed light.

Everything in this world is caustic and unnatural. The sun is dangerously bright; time does not seem linear. Agathe suffers from seizures and her experience of events is somnambulic. The Matriarch, whose hold over the colony is slipping, longs to encounter another band of survivors, anyone who might feel ‘the same pain, the empty city, the poisoned river, the unbearable whiteness of the new chemical sky’. She orders Dolores – one of the colony’s most impaired members, who can neither speak nor walk – to be dragged in a wheelbarrow to the centre of a nearby forest and abandoned as a sacrifice.

Rather than progressing, the narrative decays and dissolves into a morass of sibling rape, murder and mutiny. Looking at a pregnant sister, Agathe feels like ‘kicking the big, drooping body onto its side and stamping on the inflated belly until it burst’. New life holds no promise. *The Doloriad*’s title carries a suggestion of a jeremiad – a vicious condemnation of a fallen society. But Jeremiah means ‘God raises up’ and a jeremiad retains something of that promise of elevation. Dolores, by contrast, means pain or sorrow.

*The Vivisectors*, Williams’s second novel, has a similar narratorial disposition. Once again we are in a European city. This ‘crumbling necropolis’ is home to a prestigious university in the style of Oxford or Cambridge, and its inhabitants fall into two social groups: the academics (esoterics who hold themselves at a remove from the material concerns of their mouldering world) and the gardeners (a band of macho anti-intellectuals whose job it is to beat back the vegetation that threatens to envelop the city).

Williams’s protagonist is again called Agathe, which means ‘intrinsically good’, though the Agathe of *The Vivisectors* is anything but. In the opening scene her elderly uncle tells her that her mother has ‘finally’ attempted suicide. This leaves her unmoved. Something has disfigured her family, some unspeakable violence that took place in her childhood. There’s a suggestion that Agathe herself may have been responsible. Now in her twenties, she rarely speaks. At night, she grinds her teeth to stubs. When her uncle tries to offer her advice, she meets him with indifference:

> He was trying to help me without understanding how dangerous it is to help a person like me. Even after all these years, I don’t think he suspected how many awful things I had to say, all the hatreds I had stored up … At the centre of my soul there was a big dead rock that wanted nothing to do with anything else, and when I thought of my name or the qualities that supposedly made me *me*, this was all I saw – a great big lump of stone sitting in the shadows.

Agathe feels neither pleasure nor pain. She walks so much that her socks become soaked in blood. She enjoys antagonising the incompetent urban design professor for whom she works. ‘Nobody has ever been able to see what I really am,’ she tells us, ‘although at times they have sensed it.’

Like *The Doloriad*, with its dream sequences, soliloquies and unfathomable plot developments, *The Vivisectors* is near impossible to summarise. Agathe’s family drama provides one narrative thread; the battle between the academics and the gardeners offers another. The book has been described as a campus novel, and the closest thing it has to a central storyline concerns a departmental dispute over identity politics. Agathe’s boss is obsessed by a scandal involving Adam, a graduate student from her home city, who gets into an argument about race with a young professor called Jacob. Adam is descended from a ‘persecuted people who had suffered cruelly at the hands of various nations’. Jacob is descended from ‘foreigners’ who ‘five hundred years ago’ had been slaves in Adam’s city. Adam’s people are still despised in some quarters as ‘slippery and untrustworthy’, ‘too rich and too powerful’. Jacob’s people face ongoing oppression. In the course of the disagreement, each says something the other interprets as a dog whistle. The situation escalates, academics take sides and sexual politics enter the debate. Facing expulsion from the university, Adam insists he’s the real victim: having slept with most of his female classmates, he is now being persecuted by pissy, jilted women and jealous, wimpy male colleagues.

*The Vivisectors* presents a bleak view of academic institutions. Its scholars are fraudulent and vain: ‘Their activity was a sham. All they did was generate piles of useless papers that they used to justify the continued existence of their departments.’ The university is little more than a breeding ground for a vapid politics of victimisation. Agathe’s boss takes Adam’s side in the argument, declaring the problem to be ‘highly strung’ young people who want ‘nothing more than to cut people down for the slightest error, the smallest slip’. The professor tasks Agathe with befriending Adam and feeding her information about him. Though Agathe initially loathes him, they begin to draw closer. This gives Agathe the opportunity to reflect at length on the virtue-signalling rife in the university. Yet such passages read less like a character’s inner monologue than like authorial rants. Reviewing *The Doloriad* in the *New York Times*, J. Robert Lennon claimed that Williams’s vague and abstracted third-person narration made the book’s ‘moral centre impossible to identify’. But Williams doesn’t seem interested in novels with a moral centre. *The Vivisectors* is governed by a different principle – reprobate formalism, perhaps.

Midway through the book, Agathe’s father confesses to having lost ‘faith in words to convey any kind of meaning’. Elsewhere, another character derides contemporary fiction as ‘nothing but stacks of metaphor, badly chosen metaphors’. Williams appears to be taunting us. After all, *The Vivisectors* is not just a contemporary novel, but one overburdened with metaphors. From the outset she invites us to treat the university and the garden as symbols: ‘The university watched the wildness growing at the heart of the city with fascination. It was a symbol, the academics agreed, of a much greater entropy. Something was underway, a process that could not be stopped.’ Here the heavy-handedness of the conceit makes it clear that Williams is mocking the academics’ eagerness to find symbolic meaning in everything. At other times the reader is invited to engage seriously with the novel’s symbolism and its esoteric set-pieces. As the authorial attitude towards the garden fluctuates, so too does what it signifies. Sometimes it seems to stand for the ‘wildness’ that humanity must ‘tame’ for survival; sometimes it stands for the natural forces of the universe working to stamp out the abomination of human bullshit. There’s the Freudian reading: nature as id, nature as a manifestation of the viciousness that has overtaken Agathe.

At one point there’s a druidic discussion between seven gardeners, each advancing a theory of his practice. ‘A great gardener is an artist of eternal murder,’ one of them says. The most convincing monologue comes from 1st Gardener:

> Everything about the garden exceeds the attempt to describe it … The garden cannot be summarised. The garden cannot be reduced. The only way to apprehend the garden is to stand inside it, and even then something of the garden continues to escape comprehension. The garden is always excessive. It can never be seen in its entirety. It cannot be captured in speech. But despite this you must describe this garden. You must describe it in a fallen language, in a language full of holes, with words that are imprecise and ugly. There is no other way. And so what passes into history is an image of the garden. An imperfect image of a perfect garden.

Christian ideas and images abound: gardens, floods, fallen nature. In *The Doloriad* the children watch reruns of a television show about Thomas Aquinas. Williams has talked in interviews about her interest in Augustine and has cited Étienne Gilson’s *Reason and Revelation in the Middle Ages* (1938) as the most important influence on her first novel. She isn’t trying to advance a Christian worldview; rather, she mines Christianity for metaphors to be used, mocked, inverted, warped. This allegorical mode doesn’t guide readers towards any coherent theology, but rather creates a sense of interpretative chaos.

Williams has a fine ear for prose rhythm. Her sentences come out in a rush, and her similes are deliciously odd: a woman is described as ‘overflowing with emptiness, like a glass full of foam’. *The Doloriad* has been compared to the work of Cormac McCarthy (for its brutal existentialism), to Ottessa Moshfegh (for its abrasiveness) and to Clarice Lispector (for its fragmented streams of consciousness). But Williams’s style can be exhausting. In both novels, the density of allusion, polemic and parody makes the text difficult to parse. Narrative avenues open up and then lead nowhere. There are many aphorisms (‘form is everything; content is meaningless’) and monologues (on the ‘battle for our lives’ against screens; on affirmative action for women in academia). There’s also an extended parable about a prince climbing a tower to rescue a princess.

The manic, histrionic quality is intentional: it expresses a vision of humanity defined by hollowness and degeneracy. ‘When I think about the world around me,’ Agathe says, ‘I see an arrangement of walls, each enclosing and excluding different planes of emptiness, fields where nothing grows and villages devoid of inhabitants. *This* is the true state of things.’ A question emerges: can Williams write a novel about nihilism – one that enacts nihilism – and leave the reader with something meaningful? *The Vivisectors* uses the devices by which fiction generates meaning – allegory, metaphor, existential inquiry – yet it frustrates every attempt to make sense of them. The result is grim, energetic, exasperating: a knot of fertile, hyper-literate ideas that strangle one another. It’s as if the story were written by the God who decides in Genesis 6 that humanity was a failed experiment and that it’s time to wash the world clean.
