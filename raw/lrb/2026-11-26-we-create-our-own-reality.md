# 正文

Attitudes​ to the enterprise called ‘critical theory’ are by now more or less set, and most of them are negative. The right loathes ‘Cultural Marxism’ in particular, by which it means the Frankfurt School above all. According to this view, despite the collapse of Communism, its ideology lives on in the writings of Adorno, Benjamin, Marcuse and others, which continue to poison young minds with resentment over the role of class in culture, not to mention contempt for God, country and capitalism. The memoir-manifestos of arch-conservatives such as Andrew Breitbart are punctuated with traumatic encounters, often in college days, with German theory of this sort – or rather a caricature of it, for these pundits didn’t actually do the reading. (Mark Levin, whose talk show is called *Life, Liberty & Levin*, rails against ‘the so-called Franklin School’.) Critical theory is projected as a ridiculous bogeyman but one with great powers of persuasion.

The left response is, weirdly, symmetrical to the right’s, for its bad object is ‘French theory’, especially structuralism and poststructuralism, which are decried as ahistorical, anti-materialist and anti-dialectical – in a word, anti-Marxist. Structuralism, in its concern with ‘the order of things’, is taken to conform to the bureaucratic administration of postwar capitalism, and its poststructuralist successor, high on ‘floating signifiers’, is said to reflect the flashy surfaces of the society of spectacle. While some on the right cast critical theory in primal scenes of political revulsion, others on the left prosecute it with the rancour of apostates. As we know from cases of zealous anti-Freudianism, haters often begin as lovers, and stories of disillusionment are intrinsically dramatic. Such confessions sound as if they must be truthful.

Somewhere between right and left is the liberal relation to theory, which is well represented by *The Frenchmen: Or, My Life in Theory*, in which Emily Eakin, an editor at the *New York Times Book Review*, looks back on her experience studying literature at Harvard in the late 1980s. Her view of theory assumes aspects of the others; Eakin too writes of her ‘college theory trauma’. But it is less extreme; she remembers her ‘multi-year breakdown’ almost fondly, as an intellectual love affair that was intense while it lasted but good to leave behind. Her book is fun to read, but part of the pleasure is the reassurance it offers. Lucky us! We survived that heady rhetoric of ‘the death of the author’ and those paranoid visions of dominant ‘structures’ – all that concern about language, the unconscious, ideological state apparatuses and power. Common sense has prevailed, and the individual can carry on much as before.

Eakin has done the reading, and she provides elegant expositions of her Frenchmen: Jean-Paul Sartre, Claude Lévi-Strauss, Jacques Lacan, Louis Althusser, Michel Foucault, Roland Barthes, Gilles Deleuze and Félix Guattari, Jacques Derrida and Paul de Man (who was Belgian, but close enough). Even with 48 mini-chapters she finds little or no room for Guy Debord, Jean-François Lyotard, Jean Baudrillard, Alain Badiou or Jacques Rancière, probably because they don’t fit her focus on structuralists and poststructuralists. Only a few women are included – Simone de Beauvoir, Hélène Cixous, Luce Irigaray and Julia Kristeva – and these mostly in passing. Evidently for Eakin, feminism, like Marxism, didn’t play a significant role in French theory. But then her book is titled *The Frenchmen*.

By the time Eakin entered Harvard, French theory had found a home in the university, while it circulated more widely in the form of such clichés as ‘There is nothing outside the text.’ Eakin accepts reductions of this sort when she is feeling dismissive of theory and refuses them when she is engaged. Throughout the book she demonstrates how different from one another these thinkers were, and how mutually antagonistic; one senses the close confines of intellectual life in postwar Paris. As the foremost public intellectual as well as the Marxist standard bearer, Sartre was the primary figure to displace. Lévi-Strauss did the most damage in *La Pensée sauvage* (1962), with his structuralist critique of the historicist Sartre, but the challenge also came from fellow Marxists such as Althusser, who pitted his antihumanist rereading of Marx against the humanist version advanced by Sartre. Foucault writes somewhere that scholarship was born of monks quarrelling over manuscripts, but there was nothing monkish about these thinkers, and the stakes never seemed picayune: they laid into one another in public forums with the grandest of proclamations. The passion of those debates drew sometimes on sibling rivalry – Sartre and Lévi-Strauss were separated by just three years – and sometimes on Oedipal frustration: Derrida was 36 when he deconstructed Lévi-Strauss in 1966, the first salvo of poststructuralism.

As Eakin narrates these intellectual agons, she also examines the personalities of her subjects, which she believes affect the writings directly. Of Foucault she writes, ‘the contradictions in his work make more sense when viewed in the context of his private life,’ a claim she supports with one of his own comments: ‘My books have always been my personal problems with madness, with prisons, with sexuality.’ Eakin takes this approach with others as well, and the effect is to humanise even the most severe of these anti-humanists. At times, though, she allows racy biography to override intellectual history. Foucault militated against power at every turn, but did his political positions necessarily stem from his marginality as a gay man? Derrida stressed the undecidability of meaning, but was his fascination with aporia symptomatic of his depressive anxiety? Althusser stressed our interpellation by social institutions, but did this focus reflect his manic sense of ‘individual self-erasure’? Such connections can be drawn, but it is difficult not to render them as causal, and a basic lesson of critical theory is to be sceptical of determinations of this sort.

Sometimes the life not only explains the work but almost undoes it, and here Eakin is hardly to blame, for some of her protagonists were very bad actors. (They wrote as though they had no bodies, she complains, but they didn’t live that way.) Among the many misdeeds recounted in *The Frenchmen* are two notorious crimes. In a psychotic episode in 1980, Althusser strangled his wife, Hélène Rytmann; and in 1987, four years after his death, de Man was exposed as the author of antisemitic texts written for a collaborationist publication in occupied Belgium during the Second World War. De Man was branded a ‘total fraud’ (a headline in the *New Republic*), and so too was the deconstructive method of reading he had developed in parallel with Derrida. In the schadenfreude that followed, deconstruction became synonymous with obfuscation, and undecidability with irresponsibility, or worse. ‘The “de Man scandal”,’ Eakin concludes, ‘was widely seen as the affair that finally did theory in.’ The last nail in the coffin was the *New York Times* obituary of Derrida, who died in October 2004, a posthumous hatchet job if ever there was one. As an editor at the *Times* when the obituary appeared, Eakin made no objection to the piece – ‘a confession’, she admits now, ‘that sounds like an excuse’.

Eakin sometimes lumps her Frenchmen together as structuralists (even Georges Bataille is called ‘a proto-structuralist’) when, strictly speaking, the label applies only to Lévi-Strauss, Althusser and Lacan; Foucault rejected the approach, Deleuze and Guattari ignored it, and Barthes, Derrida and de Man moved beyond it. For Eakin, the structuralist sees language as a stable system that can be used to analyse other systems, whether myths, ideologies, dreams, fashion or food. This is fair enough, yet in her view to adopt this method is to walk straight into ‘the prison-house of language’ (as Fredric Jameson called it). In a debate about the nature of madness, Derrida once accused Foucault of ‘structuralist totalitarianism’. Eakin agrees: ‘In the Foucauldian universe, as in the structuralist one, humans appear to have no agency.’ And she casts this aspersion on other lines within critical theory as well: ‘Lacanian psychoanalysis was, like Marxism, another holistic “total knowledge” philosophy.’ The magical invocation of ‘agency’ and the implication that ‘total’ philosophies are potentially totalitarian are among the many clues that her account is a liberal one. For Eakin, poststructuralism was no improvement: where structuralism was too systematic, poststructuralism was too ‘destabilising’, and its claims ‘disintegrated on contact with the damning particulars of individual motives and actions’. Part of the appeal of French theory for ‘intellectually insecure American students like me’, she says, was that it ‘swept away doubt, offering in its place the certitudes of an all-encompassing system’. My experience was almost the exact opposite, but then I didn’t first encounter theory in the academy.

Like any cultural practice, French theory emerged in a particular context, and some of its concepts didn’t travel or translate well. Its critique of ‘the subject’ had real force within a Cartesian tradition, less so in others. Ditto its critique of representation, which makes most sense in a culture steeped in discursive analysis. ‘Decentring’ is a crucial operation in poststructuralism, but it takes a centre as dominant as Paris (culturally, politically, administratively, academically) to make it count; in an already decentred environment such as the US it doesn’t have the same purchase. And only a society that cares deeply about the figure of the author will be alarmed by reports of its death.

So why did French theory flourish in the US? For many this success is just another species of failure. Certainly Foucault was very influential at Berkeley, as were Derrida and de Man at Yale, while others impressed at Columbia, NYU, Johns Hopkins and elsewhere, and they inspired significant shifts in teaching and research. Today, however, that assimilation is seen as simple recuperation or academicisation. Foucault, in his retort to Derrida, dismissed deconstruction as a ‘little pedagogy’ of the text, and for a time it did become a semi-rote protocol of reading in some departments on some campuses, one form of ‘the technobureaucratic labour of the new professional-managerial class’, as John Guillory put it. What’s more, coming to America did lead French theory to stray from its political projects; long ago Régis Debray joked that left intellectuals thought they had discovered China in Paris, only to land in California. Yet these two views are partial at best; Guillory is too severe, and Debray too snarky.

French theory was also time-sensitive. Consider the vagaries of demystification and deconstruction over the years. In his pre-structuralist period, Barthes practised a brilliant form of demystification, most influentially in the short texts on mass culture in *Mythologies* (1957), but as early as 1970, in his preface to that volume, he suggested that it had to be exceeded. Ideology critique could degenerate into a posture of cynical reason that almost any discontented student could mimic. Deconstruction was rendered banal in another way, as the seductive term was bandied about without much concern for the method. It was corrupted utterly when Steve Bannon declared the ‘deconstruction of the administrative state’ to be the goal of the first Trump White House. But then it isn’t as if power had trembled before either type of inquiry. As Karl Rove told the *New York Times* during the Iraq War, ‘we’re an empire now, and when we act, we create our own reality. And while you’re studying that reality … we’ll act again, creating other new realities.’ It is difficult to demystify a regime that flaunts its own contradictions, let alone to shame leaders who are shameless. Eakin points to another recent reversal of critical theory, a ‘Foucault realignment’ whereby the right uses his concept of ‘biopower’ to contest governmental actions such as Covid lockdowns and vaccine mandates. (No less a theorist than Giorgio Agamben is on board here.) However, this too is only part of the story. As Eakin notes, resistance to a social order that depends on surveillance and incarceration, not to mention gender normativity, draws deeply on Foucault to this day.

For Eakin, as for many others, the age of French theory is well past. In this account not only are its protagonists long gone – several had died by the mid-1980s – but its mission was accomplished years ago. Here she agrees with a comment of Stanley Fish from 1994: ‘Deconstruction is dead in the same way that Freudianism is dead. It is everywhere.’ Yet this everywhere can feel a lot like nowhere. Eakin wonders, for instance, about its relevance in contemporary debates about identity politics, given that theory is hardly affirmative of subjecthood. She also sees its focus on ideological state apparatuses as outdated: ‘To a citizen of the 21st century, with its freewheeling digital culture, multibillion-dollar social media companies and relatively weak federal governments, the idea of a state ideology conditioning the thoughts and behaviour of its citizens sounds faintly ridiculous.’ I take her point, but then a renewed critique of the subject might be just what current campus disputes over identity need, and ideological state apparatuses can assume many different forms: if the Church was the dominant form in medieval Europe, today it may be social media companies. Social media operates through personalisation, which is a central effect of contemporary power. In *The Frenchmen*, Eakin participates in this personalisation twice over, first by profiling her subjects as personalities and second through her account of her own ‘life in theory’. The valences of theory shift and history continues to deliver its ruses. AI might confront us with a ‘death of the author’ more actual than the one imagined by Barthes. And in the form of ‘large language models’, language may well be a dominant structure.

If French theory is long dead, why do its opponents still need it as a foil? Here again there is a rough symmetry between the positions on left and right. As far as the left is concerned, such theory, especially in its poststructuralist iteration, suits the neoliberal university just fine, while for the right it has produced a politicisation of ‘tenured radicals’ that isn’t Marxist so much as nihilist. Critical theorists can’t win; in this small battle within the greater war against the academy they still get it from both sides.
