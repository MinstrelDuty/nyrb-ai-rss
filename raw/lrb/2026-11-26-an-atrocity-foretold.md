# 正文

On​ 24 October last year, the fighters of the Rapid Support Forces launched their final assault on the city of El Fasher in North Darfur, which they had besieged for more than five hundred days. El Fasher was the last government redoubt in Darfur, but the Sudanese army had crumbled. Over the next three days, RSF fighters moved from house to house shooting people; they stormed hospitals, gunning down patients, medical staff and the wounded; they killed families who had sought shelter at the university and were caught on film executing a wounded man lying among other casualties in the Faculty of Medical Laboratory Sciences. At the Saudi Maternity Hospital, fighters searched the wards over several days, killing patients and staff; more than 460 people were killed there, their bodies later burned. Thousands who tried to leave were stopped at checkpoints or on the earth berm the RSF had built around the city and asked to disclose their ethnic identity. Many were killed at the roadside. Women and girls were raped, sometimes next to the bodies of their family members. One person who survived the attack said that of 63 people who fled together, only seven reached the neighbouring town of Tawila alive. Al-Fateh Abdullah Idris, known as Abu Lulu, the RSF’s brigadier general, boasted that he had killed two thousand people before losing count.

The UN’s Human Rights Office documented the killing of more than six thousand people in those few days. One of the RSF’s own commanders, unnamed by journalists, put it at seven thousand. Those numbers would make the toll roughly commensurate with the Bosnian Serb massacre in Srebrenica in 1995, a crime for which eighteen men were convicted of genocide by the International Tribunal for the Former Yugoslavia. A scathing report on the Dutch UN peacekeepers, whose commander was pictured sharing a drink with Bosnian Serb commanders after the garrison’s capitulation, led to the fall of Willem Kok’s government in The Hague.

But the real toll in El Fasher may be far higher than in Srebrenica. The Yale Humanitarian Research Lab, which monitored the siege and its aftermath, used satellite images to identify probable human remains, mass graves and evidence of immolation. Its best estimate for the death toll is sixty thousand. That number doesn’t include those who died trying to find safety or who perished of exhaustion and hunger in the overcrowded sanctuary of Tawila, the nearest point of refuge. Seldom has a massacre been forecast with such precision to so many world forums, international organisations and their member states, including the UK. On 23 June, in written testimony to the House of Commons International Development Committee, Nathaniel Raymond, executive director of the Yale HRL, stressed ‘the timeliness, granularity, accuracy and actionability of the intelligence HRL and others provided to Foreign, Commonwealth and Development Office personnel’ as events unfolded. It was ‘more than sufficient’, he went on, ‘to support the development of robust policy options that could have prevented the El Fasher massacre’.

The Sudanese way of war has been thoroughly documented over the last forty years. In the 1980s, the Sudan People’s Liberation Army took control of most of the southern countryside; in response, the bankrupt government in Khartoum licensed proxy militias to devastate the region. In 2003, the same policy was directed at Darfur, Sudan’s westernmost region, with the notorious militia known as the Janjaweed in the vanguard. It seemed to me, [as I wrote in the *LRB* of 5 August 2004](/the-paper/v26/n15/alex-de-waal/counter-insurgency-on-the-cheap), that a security cabal in government was perpetrating ‘genocide by force of habit’. Ten years later, after South Sudan’s secession, Janjaweed fighters were marshalled into a more formal paramilitary, the RSF. Initially controlled by the intelligence service, it was soon legalised and put under the army’s command.

In October 2021, the army and the RSF worked together to topple the civilian government in Khartoum, arresting the prime minister and several cabinet members. The chief of the army, General Abdel Fattah al-Burhan, and the commander of the RSF, Mohamed Hamdan Dagalo, known as Hemedti, took control of Sudan’s Transitional Sovereign Council. The current hostilities began eighteen months later, when Hemedti decided he could no longer share power with the army and attempted a coup of his own. In April 2023, he besieged the bunkers under the military headquarters in Khartoum, where al-Burhan and senior officers were holding out. Al-Burhan managed to escape in August, though thirty of his bodyguards were killed, and the army set up an alternative administration in Port Sudan, only returning to Khartoum this year.

The RSF consists of a core of well-trained, well-equipped mobile forces under Hemedti’s direct command, beside a larger congeries of militias. Many of these have their own priorities: pillage, vendetta, land grabs. Weeks after the present war began, RSF militia attacked Darfur’s Masalit communities (settled farming populations near the border with Chad), killing around ten thousand people. On 14 June 2023, the governor of West Darfur, Khamis Abakar, himself a Masalit, went on television to say that civilians were being killed in huge numbers. He called it genocide. A few hours later, RSF fighters arrived at his office, dismembered him, and circulated a video of his remains. The fate of the Masalit surprised no one. Before long, the RSF had occupied most of Darfur, home to about ten million people. Defending El Fasher alongside Sudanese army units garrisoned in the city were former rebels who had joined the government three years earlier but still had de facto independent units. Drawn mainly from the Zaghawa ethnic group, these were the RSF’s fiercest rivals. They feared that if the city fell, they – as well as hundreds of thousands of Zaghawa residents and others from non-Arab groups – would be put to the sword.

Less than a month after the fighting began, the Biden administration and Saudi Arabia summoned the two sides for talks in Jeddah. It was a precipitate but half-hearted step. Saudi Arabia wanted an end to the fighting, with the compliant government in Khartoum remaining in charge. But the United Arab Emirates, Saudi Arabia’s neighbour and rival on the shores of the Red Sea, had thrown its weight behind Hemedti and the RSF. It was an open secret that the UAE, which benefits from the influx of Sudanese gold, was providing the RSF with arms and mercenaries. The Yale HRL identified AH-4 howitzers, supplied by the UAE, bombarding El Fasher – a finding later corroborated by leaked US intelligence assessments. It also monitored planes chartered by the Emiratis that were delivering advanced Chinese-made drones at Nyala airport, about a hundred miles south of El Fasher, after it came under RSF control. Investigators used phone location data to track Colombian mercenaries moving from Bogotá to Darfur by way of a UAE base in Somalia. The Colombians operated combat drones and trained RSF units. Conflict Insights Group identified a brigade nicknamed the Desert Wolves, tied to a company with links to senior Emirati officials, including the national security adviser, Sheikh Tahnoon bin Zayed Al Nahyan.

The UAE denies all this, even in high-level, private conversations. The best chance to engage with Abu Dhabi would have been at the peace talks in Jeddah, but Washington didn’t make the approach. Antony Blinken, the secretary of state, compounded the error by assigning the ongoing crisis in Sudan to Molly Phee, assistant secretary of state for African Affairs, who had refused to take any serious action against al-Burhan and Hemedti after they ousted the civilian government. It was clear even then that Abu Dhabi and Riyadh were the first stations on the road to de-escalation. That would have required direct, personal contact with Mohamed bin Zayed Al Nahyan, president of the UAE. Only Biden could have made the call, with Blinken putting his shoulder to the wheel to build momentum, but it didn’t happen. On taking office as secretary of state in 2025, Marco Rubio dusted off a neglected blueprint and put together a ‘Quad’ of negotiating parties – the US, Saudi Arabia, the UAE and Egypt – but by then it was too late. Tensions between Riyadh and Abu Dhabi were running high. The UAE had paid off a number of neighbouring states – notably Chad – to facilitate its arms supplies. In turn, a group of states including Egypt, Saudi Arabia, Qatar, Turkey and Iran had come to the rescue of the Sudanese army with arms shipments of their own.

The UN has invested huge resources in Sudan: three peacekeeping missions since 2005 plus another in South Sudan which remains in place. The earlier Operation Lifeline Sudan, established in 1989, brought aid to civilians in rebel-controlled areas. It was in Sudan that ‘humanitarian principles’ – the rubric for relief agencies working in war zones – were first codified. The Security Council referred the Darfur war to the International Criminal Court in 2005 and holds twice-yearly sessions to review progress in accountability for international crimes. When the RSF turned on the Sudanese army three years ago, the UN could have made a simple pitch: every regional neighbour has legitimate interests in Sudan, but none is served by its destruction.

The UN secretary-general, António Guterres, seemed strangely indifferent. He failed to take the rudimentary step of reconvening the Sudan Consultative Forum, a grouping of interested governments, to share data and analysis. And he overrode the advice of his Sudan specialists in deciding to recognise al-Burhan’s regime as the legitimate government, though he was under no pressure to do so. If a government doesn’t control its capital, its claim to be recognised as sovereign is slender. The UN’s Office of the Legal Counsel could have deferred recognition, which would have allowed officials to set up an independent aid mechanism – bypassing the death-by-bureaucracy that Sudanese governments have perfected as a means of strangling aid efforts.

In refusing to recognise al-Burhan’s government, Guterres would have been following the example of the African Union, which suspended Sudan’s membership after the 2021 coup, saying it would only reinstate it when the country was restored to civilian rule. Withholding recognition would have been in line with Guterres’s stated preference for allowing African institutions to decide on African conflicts. Neither the US nor the EU had recognised al-Burhan. But, apparently without serious debate, Guterres invited al-Burhan to New York to address the General Assembly in September 2023. Guterres then reiterated that the African Union, which he had just overruled, should come up with a solution in Sudan. At the time, the head of the African Union was Moussa Faki, a former prime minister of Chad, a country receiving Emirati largesse; he was in no position to play the impartial peacemaker.

Sudan’s generals have long felt that the UN presence in their country ties their hands. They have accused Operation Lifeline Sudan of feeding rebel armies and aid workers of championing the southern cause. Recently, a Sudanese army general told me that ‘once we let the humanitarian nose into the tent, the whole camel followed’: in his view, it was the supply of aid to the rebels that had allowed South Sudan to declare independence. He wasn’t going to make the same mistake with the RSF. The UN-recognised government of Sudan has no presence within hundreds of miles of Sudan’s western border with Chad, yet when World Food Programme lorries arrive at a line in the sand, they have to stop and wait for the Humanitarian Aid Commissioner in Port Sudan – a security officer a thousand miles away – to issue a permit. A trickle of aid goes in, whenever the commissioner decides, but al-Burhan won’t allow UN aid workers to set up operations in areas controlled by the RSF.

Pioneering methods have been used to track unfolding humanitarian crises in Sudan over the past two decades. In the mid-2000s, the atrocities in Darfur were concentrated in remote areas without telecommunications or on-the-ground monitors. USAID published satellite images of burned villages to map the devastation. The metrics used for food insecurity – the Integrated Food Security Phase Classification (IPC) system – were developed in the Horn of Africa with advice from nutritionists. The IPC reports on populations at risk of food crisis and famine, not who caused it or how. But it was clear as soon as the RSF encircled El Fasher and the vast camps on its outskirts for displaced residents – Zamzam to the south, Abu Shouk to the north – that hunger would follow: close to a million people depended on rations provided by the World Food Programme. As soon as those convoys ceased, Médecins Sans Frontières recorded an alarming increase in child malnutrition, which only worsened as destitute people fleeing the RSF swelled the city’s population. The IPC’s ever cautious Famine Review Committee waited six months before determining that the crisis amounted to Phase 5: ‘famine’ conditions. By early 2025, Zamzam had been starving for twelve months. As the siege tightened, the city’s residents starved too. People were reduced to eating animal feed and leaves. Mothers fed their children once a day, giving them hot water to fill their stomachs. UN officials warned of deepening starvation in August. Satellite images showed expanding graveyards. The city went dark: not only had fuel run out but people had learned that fires could be spotted by RSF drones. ‘You couldn’t even light a cigarette,’ one resident said.

Twenty years ago, USAID images from Darfur helped to make the case for genocide. During the run-up to South Sudan’s independence, George Clooney conceived the Satellite Sentinel Project, which carries out near real-time surveillance from the skies. It’s also possible to monitor the social media feeds and WhatsApp groups of RSF commanders and to locate them as they record and circulate clips of their atrocities. In December 2024, the *New York Times* published a heavily redacted compilation of these scenes. Today, the best satellite-imagery tracker of human rights violation is Yale HRL. After the Masalit massacres in 2023, Nathaniel Raymond described Zamzam and Abu Shouk as potential ‘kill boxes’. His team released nearly seventy reports during the eighteen-month siege of El Fasher, detailing the shelling and drone strikes, the burning of neighbourhoods and the tightening of the RSF’s cordon. As the RSF closed in, he kept sounding the alarm.

Raymond’s appearance this year before the parliamentary committee was an inventory of warnings gone unheeded. It also served as a reminder of Britain’s influential role in its former imperial territory. The Sudanese diaspora in the UK, including many professionals and advocates for democracy and human rights, has long-standing connections with British diplomats, aid workers and scholars who know the country well. At the UN Security Council, the UK has been the penholder for Sudan, drafting statements and resolutions and leading decisions. During Andrew Mitchell’s tenure as international development secretary, between 2010 and 2012, British strategy on peace and human rights still carried weight at the UN. But in 2020, Boris Johnson merged the Department for International Development with the Foreign and Commonwealth Office, bringing aid spending in line with Britain’s trade strategy.

Mitchell returned to government as minister of state for development and Africa in 2022 under Rishi Sunak. Shortly before his arrival, a parliamentary report titled ‘From Srebrenica to a Safer Tomorrow’ prompted the FCDO to set up a ‘conflict and atrocity prevention hub’ to issue guidelines on the way the government should respond to the warning signs of genocide. If Mitchell was encouraged by this initiative, he soon found his efforts for Sudan and the Middle East stymied by ministers advancing their trade portfolios. In February 2025, Keir Starmer appointed Jenny Chapman as minister of state for international development. She, too, found it hard to leverage her objections to the UAE’s meddling in Sudan. She used the atrocity prevention hub to fund Sudanese groups working for human rights and peace, but the big decisions were made in Downing Street.

Foreign Office staff in London repeatedly issued atrocity alerts about El Fasher, but the prime minister’s office and the foreign secretary, David Lammy, seem to have felt they were crying wolf. Raymond was advised that he could only ‘warn once’, when the city was about to fall. On 26 September 2025, a month before it did, he told a UK official in New York that it was ‘five minutes to midnight’ and provided a detailed, off-the-record briefing to back up Yale HRL’s most recent publication. The information was fed to the FCDO, which passed it up the line, but by now Labour had decided on an ‘Africa reset’: effectively, a return to Johnson’s vision of 2020. Instead of seeing itself primarily as an aid donor and conflict mediator, Chapman’s department was to pursue its agenda as a ‘partner’ in investment and business. ‘The time is now,’ Raymond wrote, ‘for any public rebuke or public statement of potential consequences for UAE’s continued support to RSF on the current humanitarian situation in El Fasher and the highly likely mass killing scenario post-fall of El Fasher.’ Such a statement would have required Starmer’s approval. It didn’t happen.

Another opportunity had been missed five months earlier, in April 2025. As RSF forces rampaged through Zamzam, Lammy hosted a high-level conference on Sudan. Part of its purpose was to get the UAE to agree to a negotiated settlement. Lammy chose to say nothing about the attack on Zamzam. The outcome was a platitudinous statement from the co-chairs – Britain, France, Germany, the EU and the African Union – that included no reference at all to protecting civilians. The UAE had dug in its heels, taking refuge behind the imminent announcement of Rubio’s Quad, which would in principle make the British effort redundant. This was an advertisement of how diminished Britain had become. On 12 September, the Quad issued a Joint Statement on Restoring Peace and Security in Sudan. It had taken months of wrangling to get the Saudis and Emiratis to agree to even this generic statement. And – unlike similar declarations in earlier wars – Sudan’s warring parties had not agreed to it. The conditions for a negotiated solution were not in place.

This litany​ of missed opportunities is all the more painful given that the UAE is sensitive about its reputation. When the American rapper Macklemore cancelled an appearance in the UAE in August 2024, saying he would not perform until it stopped ‘arming and funding the RSF’, it caused more consternation in Abu Dhabi than all of Britain’s quiet entreaties. And it was clear that if the international community spoke up, the UAE would listen. In June 2024, UN Security Council Resolution 2736 demanded a ceasefire in Sudan without preconditions and the lifting of the El Fasher siege, with safe passage out of the city for civilians. It didn’t mention the UAE by name, but called for UN member states to refrain from interference. This was the only time since the outbreak of the war that such a rebuke, mild and indirect though it was, had been issued. On the day the resolution was adopted, satellite images showed the RSF’s assault vehicles halting their advance. A source with knowledge of RSF internal communications told Raymond that Abu Dhabi had ordered Hemedti to pause the offensive while it weighed up whether the resolution would carry real consequences. ‘We could see from space, 450 miles above the Earth’s surface, these vehicles grind to a halt at the moment we were receiving that message,’ Raymond said. The pause lasted for a few days, as the UAE tested the waters. The UK was in the middle of a general election campaign. Biden was toothless – his calamitous debate with Donald Trump took place two weeks later. Blinken said nothing. After a week of calm the RSF resumed its attacks. There was no outcry.

The dance of missteps continued in Washington after Trump returned to office. In November 2025, three weeks after the fall of El Fasher, Saudi Arabia’s crown prince, Mohammed bin Salman, still a steadfast supporter of al-Burhan, raised the issue of Sudan at the White House. An astute politician would have realised that a private phone conversation with Mohamed bin Zayed was needed, offering the two Gulf royals an opportunity to back away without losing face. Instead, Trump announced that he would help ‘bring an immediate halt to what is taking place in Sudan’, at the request of his ‘very good friend’ MBS, killing the prospect of Emirati compromise.

Almost a year later we are no further on, but there is unfinished business for the British government. Sarah Champion, chair of the parliamentary committee that heard Raymond’s testimony, wrote to Chapman afterwards: ‘This evidence was profoundly shocking and, if accurate, would be some of the most concerning accounts I have heard of Foreign Office failure to take seriously its commitments to atrocity prevention.’ She asked for a written response detailing the way the government proposed to investigate the case. Chapman had not responded by the time she resigned her post, after Andy Burnham took office in July. But the FCDO still has a case to answer. Britain is a party to the UN Convention on the Prevention and Punishment of the Crime of Genocide. Note the term ‘prevention’: Britain had every chance, but a solemn obligation was not fulfilled. The UAE is also a signatory to the Genocide Convention, but its nominee for the post of UN secretary-general, Lana Nusseibeh, steadfastly refuses to answer questions on this topic. The killing and starvation in Sudan continue. Abu Lulu, briefly removed from his command after bragging about his tally of kills, is reported to be back on the frontline.
