# 正文

When​ Francis Ford Coppola won the Razzie Award for Worst Director for his film *Megalopolis* last year, he responded with a long Instagram caption beneath a zoomed-in photo of his face.

> I am thrilled to accept the Razzie Award … at a time when so few have the courage to go against the prevailing trends of contemporary moviemaking! In this wreck of a world today, where ART is given scores as if it were professional wrestling, I chose to NOT follow the gutless rules laid down by an industry so terrified of risk that despite the enormous pool of young talent at its disposal, may not create pictures that will be relevant and alive fifty years from now.

Coppola’s attempt at a tongue-in-cheek embrace of the award wasn’t unusual in the canon of celebrity Razzie acceptances. Brian Helgeland, who wrote the Kevin Costner post-apocalyptic Western *The Postman*, kept his Razzie next to his Oscar to remind him of the ‘quixotic nature of Hollywood’. Since the birth of the American film industry, as Tim Robey’s *Box Office Poison* demonstrates, directors have invoked Hollywood’s risk-aversion, their own misunderstood genius and their defiance of commercialism to explain the failure of their movies. But Coppola’s protestations also point up the oddness of the Razzies. Why is there an award for bad movies? The film industry, unlike that of any other art form, is just as in love with its failures as it is with its successes. For every *Lawrence of Arabia* there is an *Ishtar*, for every *Apocalypse No*w a *Heaven’s Gate*. And each of these pillars – the hits and the flops – supports its own mini-industry of criticism, documentaries, podcasts, online discourse and awards.

It’s not enjoyable to look at a crap painting (though Steven Spielberg and George Lucas, two of the biggest Hollywood hitmakers of all time, each own a large collection of Norman Rockwells), and most people, even if they like bad books or music, don’t like them because they’re bad but because they think they’re good. Why, then, are some bad movies so much fun? One reason is that a bad film can be enjoyed collectively. This has been the idea behind a million student movie nights as well as the programming at independent cinemas such as the Prince Charles in London, which invites the audience to talk along and throw plastic spoons at the screen during screenings of Tommy Wiseau’s low-budget cult film *The Room*. Another reason (auteurs such as Wiseau notwithstanding) is that a film, unlike a book or painting or even an album, isn’t usually the product of a single mind or one person’s efforts. The guilt that creeps over us after a couple of hours spent pointing and laughing at someone’s magnum opus is mollified after ten minutes of credits. Bad-movie connoisseurs have a genuine affection for the dross they mock: as well as *The Room*, there are many agreed-on classics of the form, including the low-budget *ET* rip-off *Mac and Me* and *Piranha 3D*, a blood and sex-charged reworking of *Jaws*.

There are important differences between a bad film and a flop. Robey focuses on the flop, though the vast majority of the 26 films he writes about are very bad. A ‘flop’, narrowly defined (as it has been since the turn of the 20th century), is any film whose budget exceeds its take at the box office. The variety of reasons that this happens turns, in Robey’s hands, into a history of the last hundred years of Hollywood. Films that owe their existence to crass calculations by studio executives, cynical marketing ploys, clichés, dying formats or gimmicks are unlikely to become cult classics. At the same time, the stuff of a cult classic – overambitious material and bizarre formal choices, hammy performances, a moonshot taken and missed – seldom makes for a healthy take on opening weekend, so there is some overlap. Into the first category, in Robey’s account, we can put such deservedly forgotten works as *Land of the Pharaohs* (1955), *Gigli* (2003) and *Catwoman* (2004), while in the second we find ‘the medium’s weirdos, outcasts, misfits, freaks’: *Freaks* (1932), *Nothing but Trouble* (1991) or Terry Gilliam’s *The Adventures of Baron Munchausen* (1988). There are also a handful of films in the book that have been re-evaluated since they first limped onto screens and are now generally considered works of art, including Orson Welles’s *The Magnificent Ambersons* and Charlie Kaufman’s *Synecdoche, New York*. Robey makes no excessive claims for the importance of the flop, but his analysis does illuminate the many different kinds of afterlife that even the most ill-conceived Hollywood productions can have, hinting that there might be a formula by which films live or die.

Robey says in his introduction that one criterion he used to choose his 26 examples was that the production had to ‘have been epic or crackers, a comedy of errors, or in some way freshly entertaining’. Reading about the kaleidoscope of failures and basic mistakes that attended each production, some of them life-threatening many times over, one wonders how any film makes it to the cinema at all. During the filming of D.W. Griffith’s silent epic *Intolerance* (which he made after protests against *The Birth of a Nation* in 1916 to prove that white Christians were just as put-upon as the Black people he had incited his audience to lynch), a battle scene with elephants injured sixty cast and crew members. On William Friedkin’s *Sorcerer* (1977), ‘almost half the crew had to be hospitalised or sent home with amoebic dysentery, gangrene or malaria.’ David Lynch’s adaptation of *Dune* was especially deadly: Jürgen Prochnow, as Duke Leto Atreides, had to dive out of the way of an exploding lightbulb, and when retaking the same shot ‘he suffered first and second-degree burns when the green gas billowing from a device attached to his cheek overheated.’ The extras playing the desert-dwelling Fremen, many of them ‘homeless Mexicans, found on the street and paid in shoes’, had to wear rubber suits in the baking heat.

It’s not just people that have suffered on set. Animal cruelty was par for the course in the production of both good and bad films until at least the 1950s, and the tagline ‘no animals were harmed in the making of this film’ didn’t appear in end credits until 1972. Robey discusses *Doctor Dolittle*, starring Rex Harrison, a hideous confection which made up for its lack of plot or charm by dazzling the audience with a menagerie of frightened and occasionally pugnacious animals. This caused some trouble offscreen. ‘A restless squirrel had to be sedated to co-operate during a brief scene – but when trainers used a fountain pen to drip-feed it with gin, all they managed to get were a few moments of this featured player swaying on a railing before it lost consciousness. Meanwhile, a bored fawn needed her stomach pumped for eating a quart of paint during a scene break.’ (The ‘worst-behaved cast member’ on the set, Robey notes, was probably Harrison, who insisted on talk-singing all his songs live and threw antisemitic slurs at his co-star Anthony Newley.)

A film Robey leaves out, probably because it’s already well known as an example of the tortures men and beasts have gone through in the cause of sub-par cinema, is *Roar* (1981), a showcase for Tippi Hedren and her husband Noel Marshall’s collection of exotic animals. Set on a Tanzanian nature reserve, it was filmed in California with an almost stylish disregard for health and safety: at least seventeen animals died, and some seventy cast and crew members were injured by the scantily trained lions, tigers and leopards. Both Marshall and Hedren were bitten multiple times; Hedren’s teenage daughter, Melanie Griffith, was mauled and needed reconstructive facial surgery; the cinematographer, Jan de Bont, was scalped by a lion called Cherries and had 220 stitches. (De Bont went on to direct *Speed* and *Twister*, two of the most lucrative action movies of the 1990s; having learned the ropes on the set of *Roar*, he allowed *Twister*’s two lead actors to be temporarily blinded by floodlights and pushed a camera assistant into a ditch.) After eleven years in production, *Roar* made a domestic gross of $110,000 against a budget of $17 million.

Of course, no producer wants sixty crew members to go to hospital, just as no studio wants to finance a film that brings in zilch. Robey points out that the money-grabbing instinct is sometimes counterproductive: ‘before their downfall’, films that flopped were ‘often dreamed up erroneously as sure things, by some backstage calculus derived from everything box office is meant to teach us’. His decision to order the chapters chronologically gives a sense of the fads that came and went in Hollywood over the course of the century. In the silent era, audiences favoured grand historical epics and weepies, which led to such fiascos as *Queen Kelly*, in which Gloria Swanson plays a convent schoolgirl seduced and disgraced by a Teutonic prince. The film was scuttled by the ‘defiant overspending’ of the director, Erich von Stroheim, who ‘fondled and dawdled over the tiniest minutiae’, in Swanson’s words, but failed to notice that the talkies had arrived. Swanson footed the bill, and it never got a commercial release in the US.

The roadshow pictures of the 1950s toured cities across the US for a limited number of weeks, each screening staged as a theatrical event with an intermission and high ticket prices. Executives hoped that the spectacle would lure audiences away from their new TVs, but by 1967, when *Doctor Dolittle* was released as a roadshow, movie theatre design had begun to move from the gilded scrollwork and velvet of the first half of the century to the plain banks of seats that are still common today, and the bloated grandeur of the performances had begun to outstrip their modest surroundings. As the genre ossified, roadshow films became overlong and weighed down with bombast; with a few exceptions, such as *Last Tango in Paris* (1972), the last five or so years of roadshows produced little of lasting value – *Darling Lili*? *Funny Lady*?

The 1990s were the era of the swashbuckling action movie, but as well as *The Mask of Zorro* and *The Mummy* there were *Cutthroat Island* and *The 13th Warrior* (at a loss of around $133 million, possibly the biggest flop in movie history). It was also the decade in which producers started seriously pushing franchises; the first two *Home Alone* films grossed $836 million against a collective budget of $46 million. Robey singles out *Speed 2: Cruise Control*, which was based on a dream de Bont had about ‘a cruise liner crashing into a tropical island’, and *Babe: Pig in the City* as examples of the quickly hardening wisdom that any success must yield a sequel, ‘however moronic’. The failure of those two films didn’t cure Hollywood’s addiction to reducing creative works of all kinds to ‘IP’; of the ten highest-grossing films in 2025, five were sequels, four were remakes or adaptations of existing ‘content’ and one, *F1*, was based on a true story.

Conflict​ between studio producers and filmmakers flares up again and again in Robey’s account. Some of the more interesting films he discusses were victims of executive meddling. *The Magnificent Ambersons* was ‘infamously recut and substantially reshot’ while Welles was away in Brazil, and the negatives of his original cut were incinerated the following year. Robey pins this catastrophe, which turned Welles’s cynical elegy for the dying days of the horse-and-carriage Midwest into a schmaltzy cautionary tale about the dangers of pride, on a particularly bleak test screening in Pomona, California, just after the US entered the Second World War. The final cut contains several intriguing scenes and a few dazzling performances (Agnes Moorehead as a middle-aged spinster confronting her altered circumstances, bathed in shadow and slumped against a boiler wailing: ‘It’s not hot, it’s cold!’), but it hangs together oddly and ends with a whimper.

At the other end of the taste spectrum, Dan Aykroyd was making the horror-comedy *Nothing but Trouble* in Hollywood at the same time that his studio, Warner Bros, was trying to deal with the out-of-control production of Brian de Palma’s *Bonfire of the Vanities* on the East Coast. When they finally saw the rushes, they were horrified. Aykroyd had had a string of hits in the 1980s – *The Blues Brothers*, *Ghostbusters*, *Dragnet* – but this time he had gone too far. The plot concerns a pair of yuppies (Chevy Chase and Demi Moore) who get lost somewhere off the New Jersey Turnpike and end up imprisoned in the small mining town of Valkenvania by an insane judge with a penis-shaped nose and his family of deformed psychopaths. Aykroyd and John Candy play most of the parts, dolled up in lumpy prosthetics, and the film outdoes itself again and again in its efforts to achieve the greatest possible affront to taste and dignity. The low point is reached when Aykroyd appears as one of two adult-sized, oiled-up babies in nappies who live on a junk heap. Despite its parade of grotesquerie, though, there’s something brutally compelling about it, and Robey makes the arresting case that it wasn’t the film itself, but Warner Bros’ forcing Aykroyd to shorten and sanitise it to ensure the film got a PG-13 rating, that caused its ruin. Aykroyd has never directed another movie.

It’s fun to dissect Hollywood’s worst failures, but despite film studios’ increasing hostility to risk, it will never be possible to come up with a blueprint for success. Some of the mishaps Robey recounts have beset films that became masterpieces, and even the greatest filmmakers have a limited view of their own work while making it. *Sunset Boulevard*’s original opening, set among a crowd of talking corpses in a morgue, was rewritten at the behest of the studio after test audiences couldn’t tell if it was meant to be funny. (The change was providential; the film became a supreme classic as well as a triumphant comeback for *Queen Kelly*’s star, Gloria Swanson. Von Stroheim appears as Norma Desmond’s ex-husband and butler, and a clip of *Queen Kelly* is played on screen.) *Apocalypse Now* was delayed for two years while the production underwent a series of calamities that would put any of Robey’s picks in the shade. The main part had to be recast; a typhoon wrecked the set and delayed the production by eight weeks; the entire payroll was stolen; Martin Sheen had a heart attack; Marlon Brando showed up to work fat and having learned none of his lines. There was hookworm, a tortured buffalo and an attempt to use real human corpses as props. Coppola finished the film by taking out a loan, putting up his car, his house and the profits from *The Godfather* as collateral. (Fifty years later, he paid for the production of *Megalopolis* out of his own pocket.) Eleanor Coppola’s making-of-the-movie documentary, *Hearts of Darkness*, contains a secret recording of her husband during the filming: ‘My greatest fear is to make a really shitty, embarrassing, pompous film on an important subject, and I am doing it … from the most sincere depths of my heart, the film will not be good.’ You never can tell.
