# 正文

In​ 1990, Noël Annan, then in his early seventies, published *Our Age*, a study – part memoir, part history – of those members of his generation who, as he put it, ‘make their times significant and form opinion’. His contingent wasn’t an ‘intelligentsia’ in some imagined enragé French sense; its members, almost all of them men produced by the public schools and Oxbridge, had not rejected their culture, institutions and class. They had their weaknesses, Annan admitted. Humanists rather than scientists, determined to wear their learning lightly, they had been too complacent about Britain’s international standing and too slow to grasp the economic dysfunction and cultural antagonisms that enabled Thatcherism. But for thirty years, a ‘golden age’ that ran from 1945 until 1975, working hand in glove with politicians, civil servants and broadcasters, the intellectual aristocracy of ‘our age’ had made Britain more sophisticated, cosmopolitan and tolerant.

Annan perfectly embodied his caste: in the intelligence service during the war, a colonel at 29, provost of King’s College, Cambridge at 39, a life peer at 48, and a sought-after committee man and admired writer and commentator throughout. Understandably, he features as exhibit A in Colin Kidd’s new book on postwar British intellectuals. But Kidd hasn’t just used Annan as an exemplar; he echoes Annan’s periodisation, framework, definitions and, to a degree, his argument. Like Annan, Kidd elides ‘British’ with ‘Oxbridge’, ‘intellectuals’ with ‘dons’. Like Annan, he credits war service with making those dons less an ‘independent adversarial intelligentsia’ than an ‘estate of the realm’, their senior common rooms replicating the ‘easy all-male camaraderie’ of the ‘officers’ mess’. Like Annan, he locates the heyday of these dons in the three decades from 1945 to 1975 and their distinctiveness in an assured and stylish performativity (he uses the term ‘sprezzatura’). The decades that followed were a diminuendo, a ‘twilight’, and just much less fun, with an ‘academic vocation’ becoming just a ‘professional job’, women and Americans entering en masse, and many bright young Oxbridge products deserting for the higher salaries and lighter teaching loads of California or the Ivy League.

One could contest a great deal of this. Did the yeast of a few exceptional individuals really leaven the whole lump? Did Hugh Trevor-Roper and Peter Laslett better incarnate the ‘spirit of the age’ than the less ‘establishment’ A.J.P. Taylor or E.P. Thompson? Annan contrasted the reformist commitments of ‘our age’ with the homosexuality and communism of the Cambridge spies, but weren’t Gilbert Murray and Arnold Toynbee more representative interwar figures and just as ‘incorporated’ as their postwar counterparts? Since conscription was universal during the Second World War, did war service really make Oxford and Cambridge dons so much more ‘worldly’ than those at London or Manchester or Leeds? Might one look, instead, to the hundreds of refugee scientists and scholars who revitalised one field and university after another? Then there’s the question of how to weigh all that sparkling high-table conversation against the exclusion of women and any too-gauche parvenus and too-Jewish Jews – the academic women cordoned off in their high-minded colleges, the hapless wives left to eat ‘cold shape’ at home, the parvenus dispatched to the redbricks and the too-Jewish Jews to America. Given my regrettable sex and my debt to those émigré scholars, I can’t help but ask these questions.

I don’t want to harp on such selectivity, however, because this book is revelatory, although as much for the unresolved tension at its heart as for its explicit claims. Kidd admires his subjects and tries to see their world through their eyes, but he worries about that choice, ‘acutely conscious’ that the book’s ‘register’ – ‘a balanced mixture of due appreciation, respect for one’s forebears, gentle ribbing and hints of satire’ – might ‘on occasions appear collusive or overly reverential’. It does, but not consistently. Instead, it seems unresolved, its paean to a golden age that offered a ‘fortunate generation an amplitude of life, a public stage, a robust self-assurance, and a verve, elegance and grace both in mode of life and mode of expression’ interrupted by Lawrence Stone remarking that the ‘malice and hatred’ of a medieval village was matched only by an Oxford common room or Eugen Weber saying that this culture left dons ‘psychologically warped and stunted’. And once Kidd begins to examine his dons ‘as intellectuals first and foremost’, with serious scholarly interests to defend, the dissonance worsens. Between two opening chapters introducing his characters and two closing ones detailing their demise, he inserts five about dons at work – evaluating scholarship, hiring colleagues, feuding over new methods and disciplines, defending old institutions or building new ones. In those chapters, the costs of a culture we thought he was celebrating are revealed.

One chapter tackles the tricky issue of the place of religion in a sceptical age. Annan wrote luminously about how that quintessential Victorian, Leslie Stephen, lost his faith (and thus his Cambridge job) without abandoning the strenuous ‘Victorian values’ such belief was supposed to sustain. Annan was, like his subject, a Cambridge unbeliever, but his own apostasy caused no trouble at all. Partly this was because religious tests were long gone, but it was also because these postwar agnostics and atheists cared more about ‘tone’ than ‘elements of belief’ and comfortably fell in with an Anglican service or Latin grace. Only Catholics rejected this easy accommodation, with the Somerville philosopher Elizabeth Anscombe mounting a fierce campaign against the proposal to give Harry Truman an honorary degree, since to do so – given his decision to drop the atomic bomb on helpless civilians – would be to condone murder. Anscombe’s philosophy was as vigorous as her doctrine; by substituting word games for moral inquiry, the ‘ordinary language philosophy’ propounded at Oxford, she charged, excused mere ‘conformity with the world’. But Isaiah Berlin, as Kidd tellingly recounts, was offended less by Anscombe’s convictions than by her manners – her slovenliness, her extremism and perhaps her fleshy, in-your-face femaleness, unstylishly pushing out one baby after another.

Not that all dons lacked a sense of social duty. Another chapter recovers the many efforts by the polymath historian Laslett to reform university structures and expand their reach. Neither Oxbridge nor the redbricks much welcomed his proposal to end all undergraduate teaching at the former and all postgraduate degrees at the latter, but his efforts to expand extension courses, bring dons into broadcasting and (especially) build the Open University were more successful. Social openness was, however, more easily exported than internalised. Oxbridge welcomed the worldly and charming Berlin but not Samuel Finer (too ostentatiously Jewish) or Harold Perkin (too obviously working class). When bright young men from modest backgrounds gained entry, they were expected to remake their manners and accents forthwith. ‘The nagging *social* demands of Oxbridge life did contribute, massively, to the deformation of the academic persona,’ Kidd admits, dwelling especially on the case of J.H. Plumb, son of a skilled worker, who showed up to take the Cambridge entrance examination in 1929 dressed – in dark suit and bowler hat – like a college porter; although he reportedly came top in the exam, he was not given a place. Returning as a don, Plumb took ‘assimilation to the point of parody’, binning his Leicester accent and collecting English silver, Sèvres porcelain and fancy cars in ‘hypertrophied emulation of the presumed values of the establishment’. This is a sad and unsettling portrait, but would Plumb’s stash of silver seem less objectionable if he had inherited it?

An academic culture that enforces conformity to this degree seems stony soil for scholarly innovation, and in three chapters on dons’ engagement with anthropology, sociology and ‘America’, Kidd shows that it was. Edmund Leach’s brilliant work put Cambridge in the running in anthropology (though Oxbridge anthropologists were also decoyed into fruitless if amusing feuds with Claude Lévi-Strauss), but the real challenge lay in figuring out how to deal with the discipline of sociology. Sociology embodied everything Oxbridge most disliked: it was new; it employed strange methods and ‘barbarous’ language; it was very much an American product. Dons had come to value America as a source of well-funded sabbaticals and lecturing gigs, but the democratic practices of an American campus sometimes shocked them: the historian Michael Howard was appalled to be told to take a tray and line up with the undergraduates at dinner in his Stanford residence hall. Now the Americans were at the gate.

The faculties at both Cambridge and Oxford fussed and prevaricated. Cambridge cleverly arranged a visiting stint for Talcott Parsons, who – as ‘an elephantine academician, utterly lacking in the sprezzatura and lightness of touch which might charm Cambridge senior common rooms’ – confirmed every prejudice. (This might seem harsh, but I had to read his translations of Max Weber at the age of eighteen, and I think it entirely justified: Parsons could not write a lucid sentence.) When Cambridge finally appointed a professor of sociology, in 1969, they gave the job to an anthropologist; when Oxford introduced a degree in human sciences that same year it was in the face of much opposition, including from Trevor-Roper, writing anonymously and in the voice of a 17th-century monk. Kidd finds this witty rather than – as I would have done – enraging, but he acknowledges that behind the resistance lurked the fact that sociology’s subject is social institutions, practices and codes. Small wonder that dons wanted to keep the sociologists away from the perfect research subject of their own collective life.

These chapters show dons valuing conformity over risk, the known over the unfamiliar, style over substance and general conversation above all; talking about one’s ‘field’ at high table was, Kidd tells us, very bad form. If your concern is for scholarship not style, this might seem a bit troubling – but the scholarly world is large and leaky, so this policing of the borders didn’t matter very much. Oxford and Cambridge remained strong in many fields, and if they shunned sociology, it thrived at the new universities and the American behemoths of Wisconsin, Michigan and Chicago. If political philosophy wasn’t a main focus at Oxford, Habermas was busy writing at Frankfurt and Rawls at Harvard. This was an age of university expansion. H.J. Dyos built up urban studies at Leicester and Asa Briggs at Leeds; E.P. Thompson inspired young social historians at Warwick; Birmingham set up its Centre for Contemporary Cultural Studies, where Stuart Hall taught us to look at our social relations and practices more critically.

Because he keeps his eyes trained on Oxbridge, Kidd doesn’t pay any of this much mind. Instead, his last two chapters recount a sad story of squabbling, embitterment and decline. Student protests and sharp criticism from Labour modernisers put both universities on the defensive in the late 1960s and early 1970s, with All Souls especially condemned as snobby and fossilised, and if some dons defended the students – Leach, Raymond Williams, for a time John Vincent – others more than lived up to their critics’ jaundiced views. Kidd describes John Sparrow, warden of All Souls, as ‘an indefatigable public antagonist of student protest … immature, childishly selfish, with a juvenile streak of cruelty and lacking any reliable sense of proportion’, and Trevor-Roper as a conservative with ‘a bent for mischief-making’. Thatcher, coming into power a few years later, pushed ‘disillusioned Whig-liberal grandees’ to the right too, and Kidd is pretty unsparing about this. Berlin’s liberalism had always been so ‘minimalist’ as to be ‘almost indistinguishable from a genial acceptance of the world as it was’, while Annan, accustomed to ‘the gilded comforts, pleasures and high living associated with the more patrician dons’, became ever more reactionary and Plumb a ‘royalist toady’. By the century’s end the dons were ‘cowed and despondent’.

Iam not so sure.​ I can see that this coterie was now defensive and irritable, but to understand change, one must compare like with like – that is, say, the work and ‘style’ of the regius professor in 1955 with that of the regius professor in 1985 – rather than take the (now resentful) views of the earlier inhabitant as authoritative. By focusing on one cohort and avoiding comparison across generations and the wider world, Kidd conflates the ordinary process of generational and intellectual change with the demise of a whole caste. Academic life is full of churn. The world changes; younger scholars are seized by new questions and, as they hare down new paths, challenge their elders’ work or leave them behind. Those elders might read that as the decline of ‘standards’ but it really isn’t; it’s just the decline of their own preoccupations and authority. Oxford didn’t let standards slip by appointing J.H. Elliott, R.J.W. Evans and Lyndal Roper to the regius chair in history held in the ‘golden age’ by Trevor-Roper, or Cambridge by appointing Quentin Skinner, Richard J. Evans and Christopher Clark (all distinctly uncowed characters) to the chair held by Herbert Butterfield. The massive expansion of universities in the 1990s and 2000s did change the ancient universities, but mostly by integrating them into the wider scholarly world. Today one encounters all types and accents at Oxford and Cambridge and can study all kinds of subject, which is all to the good.

These universities remain culturally distinctive, though, in ways that often strongly mark those who pass through them. Oxbridge colleges still ease dons’ lives with all sorts of amenities and marks of specialness: ancient buildings, fellows’ gardens, senior common rooms, staff who cook their meals and guard entry to their quads, even to patches of grass – as I learned as a visiting fellow at Trinity College, Cambridge – on which only fellows can walk. Undergraduates learn the language and rituals too: to wear gowns for more than just graduation, to keep out of the wrong gardens, to join dining societies and to compete for historic prizes – including the plum of seven years with no obligations at All Souls, the Oxford college that has no undergraduates but can, wonderfully, pour out Puligny-Montrachet at a dinner for fifty. Kidd swam in these waters young, as an undergraduate at Cambridge from 1982 until 1985 and a prize fellow at All Souls from 1987 until 1994 – that is, well into the period he defines as ‘twilight’ but with the ‘golden age’ recent enough for him to hear a lot about it. He acknowledges this, crediting ‘conversations with some of the protagonists and their colleagues … from the 1980s onwards’ for some of his information, but his personal connection probably also contributes to the tension between the book’s pungent character sketches and more deferential frame. It’s hard to meld an academic study and a tribute act.

To a degree, I sympathise. True, the United States doesn’t have anything quite like Oxford or Cambridge, but Harvard – with its comely residential ‘houses’ strung along the Charles River, its yards and quads, its festivities and clubs, its many lucrative endowments and prizes, its Society of Fellows modelled on All Souls – comes close. I went there at eighteen and will never cease to be grateful, not because it gave me a good education (I could have got that at many universities), but because it saved me, plucking me out of Midwestern Lutheranism, paying for the whole of my undergraduate and postgraduate education, and giving me (at one point literally) a key to one of the best libraries in the world. When I left more than two decades later, after fifteen years on the faculty and a few in administration, I missed so much about it: its heterodox but harmonious architecture, the Kabuki theatre of its faculty governance, the many doors it opened, the wealth it oozed and the fact that, when things didn’t work, the person you called didn’t drily say, ‘Welcome to Columbia,’ but: ‘I can take care of that for you, Professor Pedersen.’ I never regretted leaving though, not just because I found Columbia’s edgy students and argumentative faculty suited me, but still more because, in New York, no one cares that you’re an Ivy League professor; you’re just another resident of a crumbling and glorious city. This is a great check on self-regard, and I found it unimaginably freeing.

I was left, though, with a healthy respect for Harvard’s cultural power and, when I got to know them better, for that of Oxford, Cambridge, Yale, Princeton and a few other such institutions. If thirteen out of nineteen postwar prime ministers did their undergraduate degrees at Oxford, eight of nine US Supreme Court justices got their law degrees from Harvard or Yale, and this is not because it wasn’t possible to get a decent legal education anywhere else. Of course, the confidence and style Kidd anatomises underwrote that hegemony, but so did some structural factors. Oxford, Cambridge, Harvard and Yale are all in smallish cities with populations under 200,000, so that the gown dominates the town – unlike, say, UCL, the University of Chicago, UCLA, NYU or Columbia, all in cities where they’re just one institution among many and not the most important. Wealth matters, too, not just the on-display wealth of the institutions themselves, but the wealth that underwrote the ‘elegance’ of donnish life. The relative decline in academic salaries has eroded such status and style, Kidd points out, but it’s worth noting that some of his main figures no more lived off their salaries than do today’s academic entrepreneurs, with their podcasts and substacks. The *Oxford Dictionary of National Biography* records ‘wealth at death’ of between one and two million pounds for Berlin, Trevor-Roper and Plumb (no figures are given for Annan), all of whom made money from publishing and broadcasting, though Berlin also had family money, and Annan and Trevor-Roper both married well. This was quite a sum two or more decades ago, and five to ten times the amount left by A.J.P. Taylor or Maurice Cowling, both much less establishment figures, if in opposing political camps. A sociologist would look to such data points to begin to explain the elective affinity between some intellectuals, some universities and the cultural authority to which a changing establishment looked – but then, as Kidd shows, Oxford and Cambridge never really warmed to sociology.
