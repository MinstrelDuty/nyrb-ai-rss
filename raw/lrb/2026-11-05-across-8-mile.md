# 正文

What happened​ to the veterans of Occupy Wall Street? In the case of Bree Hietala, she moved to Detroit with her husband, bought cheap derelict houses left behind in the city’s decline and abandonment, fixed them up and rented them out. They planted an urban farm on empty lots where houses had once been. Hietala started a flower business with a friend. She had two children. She began watching Tucker Carlson.

‘The people he’s bringing on his platform and the interviews he’s having are the exact people I want to be hearing,’ she told me one warm Detroit evening in early autumn. ‘Truly. Like Norm Finkelstein. He’s just one of many.’ She was referring to an interview that Carlson, the former Fox News presenter and supporter-turned-goad of Donald Trump, had just put up online. Finkelstein is a longtime critic of the use of the Holocaust to deflect accountability for Israel’s atrocities.

At one time it would have been safe to call Carlson ‘conservative’ and ‘right-wing’, and in many ways the second label still holds, but his current positions cut across traditional categories. He calls Trump a warmonger for attacking Iran and criticises US support for Israel. He portrays President Zelensky of Ukraine as a tyrant in league with a cruel West to harm a benign Russia. He has defended the Holocaust denier Darryl Cooper, who on Carlson’s show described Winston Churchill as the chief villain of the Second World War. Carlson recently claimed that the ‘legacy’ population of Britain watch GB News, yearn to end immigration and are about to be locked up if they refuse to fight against Russia – much, he said sadly, as the British state once locked up Oswald Mosley for seeking peace with Hitler. ‘I don’t agree with what Tucker Carlson is saying,’ Hietala assured me, slowing down as she chose her words. ‘But I do find [he] is platforming conversations that are not OK in … the two-party system.’

It was election season in the US, a few weeks before the mid-terms, which will decide control of Congress until 2028, and consequently how far it may be possible to rein Trump in for the remainder of his final term. Detroit is the largest city in Michigan, a swing state. To win the Senate, the Democrats need to hold on to the one Michigan seat (out of two) that is up for grabs, and have chosen a new candidate, Abdul El-Sayed, a moderate leftist in European terms, a ‘communist loser who hates Jews and Israel’ according to Trump, ‘very, very evil’, per J.D. Vance.

Regardless of the outcome of the mid-terms in Michigan, the Democratic primary in August between El-Sayed and the party establishment’s choice, Haley Stevens, left a bruise. Reporting on the contest tended to describe El-Sayed as a ‘progressive’ and Stevens as a ‘centrist’ or ‘moderate’, terms that belong to the traditional divide between social democracy and liberal capitalism. But little media attention was paid to El-Sayed’s most obviously social democratic policy, ‘Medicare for all’. The national media, at least, was more interested in the backing he’d had from prominent democratic socialists, and in both candidates’ positions on Israel. In the end, what had seemed to be Stevens’s great advantage, the fantastic amount of money spent on her behalf, unprecedented in a Democratic primary ($62 million, half of it from America’s biggest pro-Israel lobbying group, Aipac), was turned against her by El-Sayed. He won, weathering the Islamophobic bile from Republicans (El-Sayed is a Muslim, born in the US to Egyptian immigrants) and the thinly evidenced attack ads from Stevens’s backers claiming that he ‘disrespects women’.

It was a narrow victory, eked out with big results in college towns like Ann Arbor and among students and younger graduates in places like Grand Rapids and Kalamazoo. Democrats in Detroit, with its high proportion of low-income families and its predominantly Black population, preferred Stevens by almost two to one, while some wealthier white Democrats in neighbouring cities backed El-Sayed. Among the young urban farmers of Detroit’s thinned-out city streets I found overwhelming support for El-Sayed, powered by anger over Gaza and disgust at the Democratic establishment.

I arrived late to Hietala’s equinox party. It was about 10 p.m. when the Uber dropped me off at the former church she and her husband had bought in the inner-city district of Poletown. It was a white, boxy little building that Hietala had made welcoming with old furniture, flowers and a patina of candlelight. The church was a remnant of a street once lined with shops and houses, but the lots where they had stood were now overgrown with unmown grass, shrubs, weeds and trees. A few weeks earlier, Hietala had seen an elderly Black woman standing in the street wearing the robes of a newly minted PhD. She wanted her picture taken on the corner where she grew up, and pointed to the grassed-over spaces where her childhood world had been: ‘hardware store, one cent candy store, club, bakery’.

Hietala led me to a grove of tall Siberian elms behind the church. There was a bonfire, wine in thrift shop tumblers, groups of friends conversing in the darkness. There was none of the usual city illumination of streetlights and lit windows. The moon shone through the crowns of the trees. ‘You know, I’m a lifelong Democrat. My parents were Democrats. I am no longer. I would not say I’m part of that party,’ she said. ‘I am a person with no party. I think most people feel that way.’

‘But you’re still with Abdul?’

‘One million per cent. But he’s outside the Democrats. You know, the Democrats are fighting against Abdul … You have a lot of Trumpers that were Democrats, and then they got to a point, they’re like: “You know what? Essentially my family backstabbed me. I want to get back at them.” I think there’s a lot of Trump people that are just pissed at being led the wrong way. I have no anger toward Trump voters anymore.’

I asked about high-profile progressive Democrats like Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez – had the party, as Hietala put it, ‘broken her at the knees’?

She said it had. An older man with a waxed, full beard voiced an obscure myth. ‘I mean, she was trained by the CIA to begin with, so …’

A few blocks away, on Farnsworth Street, where two decades of urban farming, mutual support and seemingly principled acquisition of dirt-cheap properties has produced something like a stable community, I met Caroline Baron, a potter and public defender’s researcher, who shared part of an old white clapboard house with her partner. We drank tea on the front porch. Later, I wondered what the Democratic leadership would make of her. Would they be horrified that this young Jewish woman was so alienated from them that, as she told me, if El-Sayed hadn’t won the primary, she might not have bothered to vote? Or would they shrug and sigh and point out that El-Sayed was the Democratic candidate, she was actively supporting him and, one way or another, this meant the coalition was working?

Baron was disillusioned with her elders, both in the party and in her family. Her parents had disappointed her by their failure to be outraged by the slaughter in Gaza. ‘There are a lot of older Jewish folks in [their] neighbourhood like my parents, and they are really unable to get on board with voting for Abdul, and it is just kind of shocking to see [from] people who are staunch Democrats,’ she said. Her grandmother, a child of immigrants from Russia, was ‘raised by her communist aunts, and was a communist herself, and very anti-imperialist, and even at 102, she still refers to the region as Palestine, and it is disillusioning to me to see how my parents and other folks they know can be so hawkish. I actually have a memory of my father going to an estate sale and getting a set of matchboxes with the presidents on them, and burning all of the boxes that had George W. Bush’s face, and I understood him to be anti-imperialist and anti-war in this innate way. It’s been disillusioning to see how there’s this disconnect.’

El-Sayed has mocked his Republican opponent, Mike Rogers, for his fealty to Trump, but his campaign as a whole hasn’t pushed the idea of the president as a monster whose humbling would restore a happy American realm. Instead he has kept up his attacks, so effective against Stevens in the primary, on the torrent of special interest money flooding the political arena. Of his eleven-word campaign message, four are ‘money out of politics’. Effectively, he is accusing both parties of legalised corruption; at a time when the corruption of Trump and those around him is central to the mainstream liberal conviction that the president is uniquely wicked, bracketing big-dog centrist Democrats together with Trump is highly provocative – which appeals to the leftists of Poletown. There has been much agonising over the working-class Democrats in Michigan who switched to Trump in 2024, but for Baron, it’s not so much that these voters should move back to the Democrats as that the Democrats should move towards them. ‘What has distanced me from the Democrats is their inability to be a party that really stands up for working people, and I think that that is what a lot of people crave.’

Baron was one of the flock of volunteer canvassers who doorstepped for El-Sayed in the primary. The era of pens and clipboards is long gone. Organisers got her to download an app to her phone with the codes to unlock data on a particular area, mapping which doors to knock on, who was supposed to live there and any information about how and whether they’d voted in the past. As well as El-Sayed, she found that voters wanted to talk about another Democratic primary, for a Detroit seat in the House of Representatives, between a Democratic Socialists of America (DSA) candidate, Donavan McKinney, and the sitting Democrat, Shri Thanedar. McKinney’s attacks on Thanedar’s sources of funding – ‘Bankrolled by crypto. Bankrolled by Aipac’ – were sterner even than El-Sayed’s on Stevens. It worked: McKinney got the nomination.

I asked Baron about the paradox that before his win in 2024 Trump had been the candidate saying he wanted to spend American taxpayers’ money in America, rather than on foreign wars – El-Sayed’s position now. The difference, Baron said, was money: lobbies like Aipac had to be prevented from coming between the voters and their elected representatives. ‘America *is* Palestine,’ she said. What she meant wasn’t just that the US would continue to fail Palestinians until money was taken off the scales of American politics, but that there was a parallel between American internal state violence and the US-supported Israeli state’s violence. I thought, for a giddy moment, she was making a still more radical comparison, with the extraordinary story of Detroit, where, at the heart of what was not so long ago one of America’s most populous cities, there are now farms with tractors, livestock and crops.

All large cities​ have suburbs, but in America the concept has a particular meaning. Detroit’s suburbs aren’t subordinate to the city. They’re small cities in their own right (Americans use the word ‘city’ where British people might say ‘town’), with their own school districts, their own police forces, their own property taxes, their own often highly restrictive planning rules and their own powers to enable or block a pan-metropolitan public transport system. Perhaps for this reason, there isn’t one. Detroit is a big city of about 650,000 people, but it’s bordered to the north, east and west by more than a hundred suburb-cities and townships (to the south is the Detroit River and, on the far bank, Canada). Collectively, Detroit and the suburbs form a mega-city of between 3.8 and 4.4 million people, depending on how you measure it, fifty miles across. Sometimes this is called Metro Detroit. More often this shadowy whole is deconceptualised, unsubstantiated.

This is more than an administrative detail. People from these suburbs are unlikely to say ‘Detroit’ if asked where they live, though they might mention its proximity. Baron’s studio and kiln are only a short drive away from her house, in Hamtramck, which is completely surrounded by Detroit but is a separate city, all two square miles of it, with its own schools and police and bureaucracy. Neither Bree Hietala nor Mike Rogers or Abdul El-Sayed would claim to be from Detroit; Hietala grew up in the city of Northville, Rogers was born in the city of Livonia, while El-Sayed, born in Rochester Hills, attended high school in the wealthy city of Bloomfield Hills. These are all suburbs of Detroit.

The city-suburb gap is psychically sharpened by its 20th-century origins in decades of sometimes violent white hostility to Black people as they moved into previously all-white neighbourhoods, and the subsequent white retreat to spacious new government-backed family homes further out, where racist loan policies made it hard for Black households to follow. The long boundary between north Detroit and the suburbs, which runs in a straight line along 8 Mile Road, became a metonym for the racial divide, white people above the line, Black below it. Coleman Young, Detroit’s first Black mayor, said on his inauguration in 1974: ‘I issue open warnings now to all dope pushers, to all rip-off artists, to all muggers. It’s time to leave Detroit. Hit 8 Mile Road. And I don’t give a damn if they’re Black or white.’ This message was widely, and wildly, distorted by his opponents, framed as a decree sending criminals to the suburbs, even as a threat to white people to quit the city. There was an echo of this distortion in the movie *8 Mile* (2002), a fictionalised version of Eminem’s emergence on the Detroit scene as a white artist taking on his Black peers in rap battles, when one of his character’s rivals says to him: ‘You need to take that white-ass rap back across 8 Mile.’ As Chris Gilmer-Hill, a DSA member who is running as the Democratic candidate for a house seat in the Michigan state legislature, told me, ‘8 Mile historically was the segregation line between Detroit and the segregated suburbs. So you would generally have a large Black population south of 8 Mile, and then a much whiter population north of 8 Mile. That has started changing, especially in the last twenty years. The inner-ring suburbs along here are diversifying really quickly, but you still have that visible line.’

From 2011 until 2021, electoral districts for seats in the House of Representatives and the state legislature in Michigan were based on a grotesquely gerrymandered map drawn by the Republicans, who controlled the state house and senate and the governorship. Congressional District 14 on this map swayed out of downtown Detroit like a genie emerging from a lamp, a few miles wide and 45 miles long, now heading east, now west, now north. The Republicans had used a technique called ‘packing’, where the map-drawing party crams as many of its opponents’ likely voters as possible into a district they were bound to win in any case, removing them from more competitive districts. But in 2018, in a remarkable feat of grassroots organisation, a citizen group campaigning under the slogan ‘Voters should choose their politicians, not the other way around’ managed to get Michiganders to vote for a change to the state constitution, putting redistricting in the hands of an independent commission. The new map has more districts straddling 8 Mile, covering city and suburb.

Gilmer-Hill, a polymathic young Black Detroit native (he graduated from Harvard in 2022 having studied, among other things, neuroscience, Chinese, applied computation, Breton folklore and Middle Babylonian), helped the change along. The courts judged that the commission’s first map gave unjustifiable weight to voters’ race, so Gilmer-Hill sent in some tweaks, not expecting that the commission would embrace them wholesale. When I met him, in a hipster café called the Congregation, he was in the unusual position of looking forward to almost certain victory in a district he drew himself. ‘I’m so glad that they did still decide to cross 8 Mile. It’s historically a segregation boundary, and I think that’s not something that we want to just endlessly replicate, even in our fair maps.’

The journey​ from downtown Detroit to the northern suburbs must begin in the depeopled quiet of the centre itself. Skyscrapers huddle, some old and handsome, and there are bars, restaurants, branches of global fashion brands, an Apple store, but there’s no bustle, no street life, no discernible rush hour. It seemed like Sunday morning, or high pandemic days, any time of the day or week. One evening, a few minutes’ walk from my hotel, I was startled by the sight of a vast bank of floodlit faces, thousands upon thousands of people in steeply raked seats. The Detroit Tigers, the city’s Major League baseball team, were playing the Washington Nationals. After games, the streets around the stadium fill up with people and traffic for a time, before the quiet descends again. Hundreds of millions of dollars of public money were spent subsidising new stadiums in the middle of Detroit – one for baseball, one for football, one for ice hockey and basketball – in the belief that they’d bring footfall, jobs and housing back downtown, but much of the new development around the stadium is high-priced parking lots.

To get from downtown to the suburb-city of Warren, just north of 8 Mile Road, you have to go by car. The bus network is skeletal. Detroiters are proud of the restored Michigan Central Station, an architectural treasure from the days of municipal wealth which, after the last train left in 1988, had been abandoned to pigeons, rats and ruin-aesthetic photographers. The Beaux-Arts interior looks splendid now, with its cathedral-like waiting room shining under trompe l’oeil vaulted ceilings. But the station still has no trains. The dense network of trams that originally served it is long gone, too. The only active station in Detroit has three slow trains a day to Chicago – none to the closer big city of Toronto. There’s one tram, running up and down a three-mile track, and an elevated light railway performing a tiny loop around downtown.

Detroit didn’t invent the car, but it was where Henry Ford showed how to mass produce it cheaply, making possible the existence of suburbs from which people could commute privately, rather than by mass transit. A true-believer motorist visiting Detroit today might think that the metropolis – the elusive city of greater Detroit – had built just the right amount of road for everyone to drive fast everywhere all the time, jam-free. The swiftness comes partly from a 20th-century brutality in building highways and freeways through densely populated areas, but is mainly due to the emptying out of the city, its population down almost two-thirds from a peak of 1.85 million in the 1950 census to 640,000 in 2020.

Detroit pioneered the ‘urban depressed freeway’, where the road is sunk into a deep concrete-lined trench. This protects residential areas from noise and fumes, but makes Detroit invisible to those speeding through it. All you see are the sloping sides of the concrete gully and billboards with the giant faces and torsos of personal injury lawyers. You can imagine anything you like on the far side of the freeway ramparts: idyllic blocks of pristine clapboard houses, open countryside, a dystopian post-industrial hellscape. Or you can forget, as you travel between on-ramp and off-ramp, that there’s any place at all.

Warren lies in a wider suburban area, Macomb County, that was critical to the president’s victory in Michigan, one of the states that gave him victory over Kamala Harris in 2024.[\*](/the-paper/v48/n20/footnotes/across-8-mile-footnote-1) (Rogers ran for a Michigan senate seat in that year, too, and lost, underperforming Trump on the same ballot by 4.44 per cent.) Macomb County was where, forty years ago, the pollster Stan Greenberg talked to some of the white auto workers, mainly union members, who had shifted their support from the Democrats to Ronald Reagan. He coined the term ‘Reagan Democrats’ to describe them. These people, he found, took Democrats’ calls for social justice as a coded pledge to siphon off their earnings to African Americans. When Barack Obama won Macomb in 2008, Greenberg wrote an op-ed for the *New York Times* under the heading ‘Goodbye, Reagan Democrats’. Then Trump won Macomb in 2016, and again, by a bigger margin, in 2024.

Warren is diffuse, low-rise. It has no centre, no main street with stores and bars. It’s more racially diverse than it used to be. Some of the male urban farmers I met spoke of it as I imagine suburbanites once spoke of Detroit (and maybe still do), as a place to be avoided. In an election campaign crackling with anger against tech-lord arrogance – against data centres sucking up Michigan’s power supply and raising prices for everyone else, against the suddenly ubiquitous cameras of the private surveillance firm Flock – it upsets Detroit’s ecosocialist neo-rurals that Amazon delivers packages in Warren by drone.

In the evenings I sometimes visited an Irish bar there, a neat single-storey place in a parking lot by a six-lane highway. There was an unbroken ring of TVs above the bar, alternating between the Tigers and the Lions, the local football team, so everyone could watch both matches at the same time. While we watched and talked and drank beer the patrons would take scratch cards from little pails on the counter, a kind of gambling grazing. I got talking to a snack food executive up from Pennsylvania. He was a Trump voter. ‘He’s a bully,’ the man said. ‘But if you’re not a bully, you get bullied. He has America’s best interests at heart. He’s done what he said he’d do.’

I tried to remember something Trump had done that he said he’d do – something my neighbour at the bar might like. I’d already established that he didn’t care for Trump’s tariffs, particularly the tariffs on Canada, or the war with Iran. Trump had promised to end the war in Ukraine. The man’s business had been hit hard recently when the Russians crippled a ship carrying a cargo of Ukrainian sunflower oil. I thought he might have in mind Trump’s harsh measures against immigrants, but it turned out these had been bad for the snack industry too. The company kept having its delivery drivers snatched by ICE.

The man’s wife had died of cancer and he felt he was too old, in his forties, to start again and have the kids he’d always wanted. His ambition now was to go to New Zealand and kill an elk there with his hunting bow. When he talked about how he went about setting prices at a time of high inflation he looked worried and uncertain, in contrast to the easy confidence he’d shown when he was talking about Trump. The long and short of it was that the firm planned to jack up prices as high as it thought it could get away with, in the hope of providing a cushion for when things got really crazy. But still, he insisted, Trump had America’s best interests at heart.

Another night, I sat next to a man called Skip. He nursed two glasses, one containing vodka, the other water, and every few minutes he used a straw and the end of his finger to transfer a few drops of water into the vodka. He told me the bartender always made his drink too strong. ‘I like him,’ he said of Trump. ‘He has the best interests of the country at heart. The war in Iran has been going on since the 1970s. This didn’t just happen last September. America can wipe them off the map in a few minutes, but I wouldn’t want innocent people to get killed.’

Skip was in town from Grand Rapids. He worked in the auto industry, much of which, he said, had gone overseas. ‘When Clinton signed Nafta in the 1990s, a lot of that shit was gone,’ he said. ‘All these countries have been fucking the United States for years.’ El-Sayed and Elissa Slotkin, the sitting Democratic senator, who beat Rogers in 2024, have both criticised USMCA, the free trade agreement that replaced Nafta and which Trump has denounced as favouring Canada and Mexico. But was it necessary for Trump to belittle and threaten Canada, to demand it give up its independence? ‘That’s the diarrhoea of the mouth. He’s too blunt. You can think stuff like that but you shouldn’t say it. I still believe he has the best interests of the country at heart.’

Skip harboured a great rage towards Obama. He said, more than once, ‘He’s the one that started all this racism.’ As he said it, his whole body began to shake.

In another bar in the historically more Republican suburb of Sterling Heights, I met a cop who’d just retired from one of the police forces in the area. All his colleagues had given him scratch cards as leaving presents and he was going through them one by one while he drank a beer drawn by his wife, who worked behind the bar. Halfway through each card he’d stop and gaze at the scratched-out circles so intensely that I asked him if there was any skill involved. He netted $25 from the cards and didn’t care to talk about Trump.

‘Imagine you’re on holiday somewhere, and a stranger asks where you’re from,’ I said. ‘What would you say?’

‘A suburb outside Detroit.’

‘And all the terrible things that have happened to Detroit over the years?’

‘It didn’t affect us.’

The facile opposition of city and suburb elides a great suburban diversity not only of race and class but of ambience and landscape. The extreme neatness, ultra-low-rise architecture and featureless immensity of Warren and Sterling Heights is not for everyone. Ferndale, whose southern border is on 8 Mile Road, is built on a more walkable scale, and has a different vibe: small old two-storey houses on tree-lined roads, the front yards scruffy but cared for, with a profusion of Pride flags and the inky flame-shapes of black squirrels flickering across the sidewalks. You would feel this was natural Democrat territory even if the Abdul for Senate yard signs didn’t greatly outnumber the Rogers ones. But there are divides party preference doesn’t reach. El-Sayed and the DSA’s call for universal healthcare in America has resonated, but ultimately it is a call for financial solidarity, and other calls for financial solidarity that don’t sound so loudly say much about Detroit, about Michigan, about America and about the Democrats.

Many of Metro Detroit’s suburb-cities are far richer, proportionally, than Detroit itself. The US system of local taxation, where each city’s tax base is tightly tied to the value (reassessed every year) of residential and commercial property, creates a steep gradient of winners and losers. This is particularly true when it comes to funding for schools, handled by yet another layer of local government, the school district, and run by yet another layer of elected officials, the school board. Many suburbs have their own school districts; some stretch across multiple suburbs; Detroit itself is one big school district. But after 75 years of businesses quitting Detroit, falling property values, and white flight, then Black flight, its tax base has been hollowed out, and it struggles to fund its needs, schools first of all.

There is some redistribution. Michigan pays schools the same basic per-pupil rate across the state. But each school district funds buildings, maintenance and facilities from its own taxes. El-Sayed is a former official for Detroit and for Wayne County. In a TV debate with Stevens during the primary, he said: ‘I know what it’s like to walk into a public school in Detroit in January, and kids are bundled up because the boiler is broken. Trying to learn how to read. Talk about a literacy crisis. How do you learn how to read when you have to wear a coat in the middle of the day? … The way that we have funded local education, specifically school buildings, has too often ported the historical racism of the past. Right now, we fund school buildings based on property tax. Property tax is a function of property value. Property value is a function of who’s been able to live where, as a function of [racist lending practices] from the past. And when we say that that same process should be patterning the social mobility of the future through public schools, that’s a real problem.’

Michigan isn’t alone in the Midwest in having a very high number of school districts for an urbanised state: it’s a feature of Illinois and Ohio, too. But in 2025 a study found that Metro Detroit had the most unequal distribution of property tax in the country. More than a fifth of taxable property wealth would have to shift across suburb-city boundaries to equalise the tax base. Unsurprisingly, the wealthier suburbs don’t want to share. Residents want to keep their smarter schools, their spiffier parks and community centres and libraries and police stations and rubbish trucks. And despite El-Sayed’s words in that primary debate, Detroit-suburb financial solidarity is low on the Democratic agenda. Many of the suburban residents who do not want to share are Democrats, even avid El-Sayed supporters.

Eric Dueweke, a fifth-generation Detroiter and former urban planning lecturer at the University of Michigan in Ann Arbor, a charming college town forty miles west of Detroit, said that as there were Reagan Democrats, so there are Ann Arbor liberals. ‘Ann Arbor liberals are the people who are totally gung-ho about integration and sharing and voting for Abdul El-Sayed, but not in their backyard,’ he said. Dueweke has lived in the Morningside neighbourhood in east Detroit since 1990, through the city’s toughest times. His son lives to the north, in the lakeside suburb of St Clair Shores. ‘There’s three different school districts in St Clair Shores,’ Dueweke said. ‘My son lives in the most northern one, which has the most resources. The one that’s closest to Detroit has the least resources. They don’t want to merge because everybody’s territorial about their schools and their kids more than just about anything else, and you know they might vote for a Democrat, but they don’t want their schools being integrated.’ When he said ‘integrated’, Dueweke was referring to a district merger, rather than the end of some form of racial segregation. But in greater Detroit, the four aspects of integration – racial, fiscal, administrative and moral – are closely entwined. Matters may have improved since 2014, when Thomas Sugrue wrote a new introduction to his masterly book about Detroit, *The Origins of the Urban Crisis (*1996), but his words remain relevant:

> The balkanisation of major metropolitan areas has created separate and unequally funded school districts, duplicative services and bureaucratic inefficiencies. But the obstacles to regionalism are seemingly insuperable in Metro Detroit where generations of racial hostility have poisoned city-suburban relations. Suburban municipalities have no incentives to share tax revenue with the city; many view the city as a lost cause. Not surprisingly, Detroit’s black majority looks outward with distrust, fearful that regionalism would simply be another mechanism for suburban control.

One Sunday morning I went to church in Harper Woods, a suburb that takes a bite out of the north-east corner of Detroit. In the front row I met Daniel Baxter, who is both a senior election official for Detroit city and the custodian of a landmark of the 20th-century Black defence against white segregationist violence, the Ossian Sweet house. Sweet, a doctor, bought the house in East Village in 1925. His was the first Black family to move into the area. They were having a housewarming party when a mob of as many as two hundred white people attacked the house, hurling rocks and bottles at it and yelling at the Sweets to get out. Somebody in the house fired at the crowd, killing one man and injuring another. Everyone in the house was arrested and accused of murder; none of the mob was charged. Eventually, with Clarence Darrow, fresh from victory in the Scopes trial, defending the accused, all were acquitted, their right to self-defence upheld. In his testimony, explaining the fear the mob provoked, Sweet described how as a child in Florida he’d seen a white crowd burn a young Black man to death and take parts from his body as souvenirs.

Baxter learned about the history of the house as a child; his parents bought it in 1958 and he grew up there. During the 2020 elections, he told me, he was supervising the count of Detroit’s absentee ballots at a convention centre downtown. Trump falsely claimed victory overnight, before Detroit’s absentee ballots and mail-in votes from across the country – ballots that would eventually give Biden victory – had been counted. A mainly white crowd, the nascent election denial movement, gathered outside the Detroit centre, banging on the glass, demanding entry (both parties already had large numbers of monitors inside) and chanting ‘Stop the count!’ ‘When I was a little boy,’ Baxter said, ‘I used to look out in the intersection to try to imagine what that mob looked like. That was in 1975. It would be 45 years later where I’d have an opportunity to see it with my own eyes. The only benefit that I had in my season was the fact that the Detroit Police Department was on our side. In 1925, the police were part of the mob.’

Why​ is Detroit the way it is? Henry Ford’s assembly line began to roll in 1913 in Highland Park, a tiny city which, like Hamtramck, is surrounded by Detroit. The following year, Ford more than doubled his workers’ wages, and began to slash the price of his cars. It was the start of Detroit’s industrial boom. The city became the centre of world car production. The arms demands of the Second World War intensified its productive power. Jobs were abundant and relatively well paid. Business and municipality, flush with property taxes, lavished grand public buildings on the city. There was work not only for the descendants of the tens of thousands of immigrants who’d come to Detroit in the 19th century, after the British ceded the French-founded city to the US, but also for hundreds of thousands of new European arrivals, many of them Catholics from southern Europe. And although they faced extreme discrimination when it came to hiring, there was work, and greater freedom, for millions of Black migrants who had left behind apartheid conditions and a devastated agricultural economy in the South. The Great Migration can be framed as corresponding to a founding American ideal, the migrant leaving their oppressive homeland to find freedom in America – even if the oppressive homeland was, in this case, another part of America. Michigan Central Station, where most of the migrants arrived, is sometimes referred to as Detroit’s Ellis Island.

What happened next, as meticulously analysed by Sugrue, was a combination of deindustrialisation and white hostility to Blacks, who were seen as overstepping their proper bounds. Before the Second World War Detroit’s industrial might had yet to crest, but the consequences of the Great Migration, racialist local attitudes and the New Deal would play out for generations. Black people were circumscribed by racial restrictions and mob violence, such as the Ossian Sweet incident, to a narrow strip, roughly ten blocks wide and a few dozen long, stretching north from the Detroit River. The river end was known as Black Bottom, the northern end Paradise Valley. Housing there was neglected and overcrowded, and Blacks with money were eager to move somewhere better, but they were in competition with working-class whites, many of whom were themselves insecure recent arrivals clinging to their whiteness as an attribute of status. The New Deal aimed to help tackle the housing shortage with subsidised mortgages for homebuyers and by supporting public housing. But the agencies responsible for deciding on mortgage lending wrote into their criteria that Black borrowers, and areas with Black people in them, were to be considered a bad credit risk – so-called ‘redlining’. It was thus much easier for white people to buy new housing, some of the most attractive of which was in the suburbs.

Detroit’s construction of public housing, meanwhile, was slow, bitterly contested by whites and effectively segregated. In the 1940s, Black advocacy groups, union leaders and their white liberal allies argued with socially conservative whites over the concept of rights: civil rights for all versus the right of white people to keep their privileges and their separateness. The second view won out. In 1950, a new right-wing mayor, Albert Cobo, entered office with the conviction that while public subsidies for white homeowners were their just reward, subsidies for affordable rentals would be wasted because they would go to Black people. Soon he shut Detroit’s public housing programme down altogether.

As hard as it was to buy private housing, Black families kept trying, and many white neighbourhoods – established ones in the city and new ones in the suburbs – worked to keep them out. Until the practice was decreed unenforceable in 1948, it was usual for new homes to be subject to racial covenants. A white Detroiter showed me one of these, which covered several lots in the suburb of Grosse Pointe Woods. It dates from 1939. Clause (h) declares that ‘no persons of any race other than the Caucasian race shall use or occupy any building or lot, except … domestic servants of a different race domiciled with an owner or tenant.’ After such covenants were judged outside the law, homeowners, estate agents, developers and officials sympathetic to segregation – particularly in some of the new suburbs – found all sorts of ways to restrict Black ownership, from zoning to peer pressure on white sellers. As late as 1960 it was found that realtors in Grosse Pointe were operating a secret race-based points system that ranked buyers according to ‘degree of swarthiness’.

When these measures failed and some defiant person bought a house, they would be targeted by mobs from one of the nearly two hundred innocent-sounding ‘neighbourhood associations’ set up in Detroit between 1943 and 1965. Black incomers were seldom physically attacked, but they lived in a state of siege that could go on for weeks or months. The violence was well organised – ‘the outgrowth’, Sugrue writes, ‘of one of the largest grassroots movements in the city’s history’. New homes built for middle-class Black buyers were vandalised or burned down. Existing homes with new Black owners were torched or had their windows broken or the power cut. Crowds up to a thousand strong, made up of everyone from working men to matriarchs to children, would mill around outside the target house, pelting it with stones, bricks and bottles and yelling racial slurs. The police usually had a presence, but seldom intervened, and the media rarely mentioned what was going on. After dark, demonstrators ‘marred properties with paint, tore down fences, pulled off cornices, trampled gardens, poured salt and gasoline on lawns, slashed car tyres, broke car windshields, and in one case, used a truck and cables to tear down a porch’.

Throughout​ this period, the Great Migration continued. Between 1940 and 1970, the Black population of Detroit increased by half a million, even as the city’s total population fell. The pressure on Black housing was increased by the authorities’ destruction of Black Bottom and Paradise Valley to make way for the I-75 freeway and its spur, the I-375, into downtown Detroit, as well as the new Detroit Medical Centre. Thousands of Black renters were evicted at short notice with no plans to rehouse them. The bars, blues and jazz joints of Paradise Valley, which nurtured Bessie Smith, John Lee Hooker and Lamont Dozier, of the great Motown songwriting trio Holland-Dozier-Holland, were lost.

A sense of long-accumulated injustices boiled over in the reaction by Black Detroiters to a police raid on a Black shebeen, one of the watering holes the Black community had turned to after their regular bars were demolished, in 1967. This used to be described as a riot, but many in Detroit now refer to it as ‘the rebellion’. The government sent thousands of National Guardsmen, plus airborne troops and tanks, into the city. Buildings were burned; there was looting; 43 people were killed, most of them shot by the security forces. The tragedy has been used ever since to stigmatise Black Detroit, but the white power structures the rebellion challenged were already teetering under the pressure of demographic change and the burgeoning civil rights movement. In 1963, Martin Luther King had led a multiracial crowd of a quarter of a million on a march for racial equality, the Walk to Freedom, through Detroit. It was no longer possible for the city establishment to ignore the power of the Black vote, growing not just from Black in-migration but increasing white out-migration. The suburbs, desirable in themselves and affordable thanks to pro-white lending, were also easier to turn into redoubts against Black people who might want to follow. In the city itself, the flipside of white segregationist resistance was white panic. Estate agents known as blockbusters exploited white paranoia by staging stunts to make it look as if Black people were moving into a neighbourhood. They would then flood the area with messaging that white people needed to sell up immediately or risk being stranded in a mixed area. Many of them sold their homes cheap, leaving the blockbusters to sell the houses at inflated prices to Black buyers. In other areas, white resistance faltered in the face of Black doggedness and the changing political climate. The spate of cross-burnings in 1965, and the enthusiasm in white Detroit in 1972 for the segregationist Democratic presidential candidate, George Wallace of Alabama, were rearguard actions, but it was grass-roots segregationism, Sugrue argues, rather than a reaction against the Great Society, that spawned the Reagan Democrats, and their present-day heirs, in Macomb County.

In 1967, Cecilia Walker, then twelve years old, lost her father, shot in the face by a shopkeeper who took him for a looter during the rebellion. The following year, redlining began to be abolished. Four years after that, Walker became the first Black aide on the Detroit staff of Senator Phil Hart. White householders would call up and tell her that the senator needed to do something about the Black people moving into their area, using the oblique euphemisms of 20th-century white prejudice. They’d say, she told me, ‘This neighbourhood’s getting really cloudy.’ It didn’t occur to them that they might be speaking to a Black woman. Walker said she always passed the message on.

Meanwhile, Detroit was shedding industry and people. After peaking in 1950, the population fell rapidly, despite the Black incomers. Industrialists, including the bosses of the big three carmakers, Ford, General Motors and Chrysler, started moving production out of the city – to the suburbs, elsewhere in the Midwest, the desert states, or the southern states Blacks had fled. The goal was to make production cheaper by escaping the power of Detroit’s unions. At the same time, automation led to job losses. Between 1947 and 1963 the city lost 134,000 manufacturing jobs. The abandonment of buildings began. Initially it was the rusting hulks of derelict factories that weren’t worth demolishing; then the enterprises that served the factories and the shops that served the workers; then the houses. In the 1970s Detroit became a majority Black city, but the jobs Black people depended on were disappearing. As people and businesses deserted the city, its property tax base eroded. American cities have other ways of raising money, and transfers from state and federal government can be considerable, but over time, the loss of property taxes builds up into a hefty deficit. Essential services like schools, refuse collection, fire and police become harder to finance; in a crisis caused by capital abandoning the city, trying to plug the gap by raising taxes risks a fresh exodus.

The number of abandoned houses crept up. Scavengers would strip them, leaving a shell, or set them on fire. In the 1980s many houses were burned down on Devil’s Night, a local pranking tradition on the night before Halloween. Cynics suggested that owners were cashing in insurance policies and blaming Devil’s Night tearaways. Crack swept through the city. Basic services began to fray: streetlights failed; rubbish collection became infrequent. ‘I think anyone will tell you that the 1990s were the worst,’ said Stephen Henderson, founder of the news site Bridge Detroit. ‘The last of the old auto plants go away in the 1990s, I think we’re left with three by 2000, and then you know there’s a stagnation that kind of takes over … everything just kind of comes apart … I remember how stark and widespread the violence became. When I was a kid in the 1970s and 1980s, Detroit had this horrible reputation as this really violent place, but it never felt that way to those of us who lived here. I mean, there were things that you didn’t do and places you didn’t go, but it wasn’t as if the city felt violent. The 1990s changed that. If you go back and look at the murder rate, that’s a simplistic measure, but it does tell you something about the city. It goes up precipitously in the 1990s, and if you look at the abandonment rate in neighbourhoods, houses just given up, people just leaving – that goes through the roof in the 1990s.’

Henderson told me about one day when he had an early morning call about a school shooting. When he got to the school, a call came in about another shooting at another school. ‘And while we were standing there, I mean, it happened to the point where there had been six shootings at schools that morning. And I just remember thinking, “This is not the city I grew up in.”’

Around the turn of the century, the crime rate dropped, and the construction of two of the new stadiums, one for the Lions and one for the Tigers, gave a hint of optimism. But across the city, unscrupulous financial agents were at work. Black people whose parents and grandparents had been denied cheap government mortgages were now encouraged to take out home loans by people who had no incentive to judge their ability to pay back the loan. When the financial crisis hit, Detroit suffered disproportionately. Bank foreclosures were severe, but what really hurt was Wayne County’s tough enforcement of property taxes, often based on inaccurate assessments of post-crisis property values. One study estimates that the county treasurer foreclosed on a hundred thousand properties after the crisis, more than one in four of the properties in the city. A new wave of abandonments and dereliction followed. In 2013, the city went bankrupt. At that point, two-fifths of the streetlights in Detroit were broken.

Michigan brought in emergency managers to run the city. They dealt ruthlessly with Detroit’s obligations. In the decades of industrial and population decline the city, unable to give its staff decent pay rises, had offered early retirement instead. Many of those who had taken advantage of this, and were too young to be eligible for Medicare, had their medical coverage slashed and their pensions reduced. Cecilia Walker’s husband, who worked for the city, was told he had to give back $50,000 from his pension pot.

Detroit was left with acre after acre of empty lots – forty square miles’ worth – and thousands of derelict properties. The Detroit Land Bank Authority was set up to deal with them. A large number of buildings were demolished and others put up for auction. Empty lots can be had for a few hundred dollars. Many lots and derelict properties have been bought up by speculators. The biggest buyer is John Hantz, a wealth manager from Southfield, a Detroit-adjacent suburb-city. He bought 140 acres of land, where more than a thousand houses once stood, for an urban farming project he called Hantz Woodlands. Ostensibly, it’s a project to beautify a blighted area, but it’s hard not to notice that the deal gives Hantz control over much of East Village, a neighbourhood close to the riverfront and right next door to the district with some of Detroit’s grandest old mansions, Indian Village. The Ossian Sweet house is in the middle of East Village. Opposite the house is a clump of Hantz’s new trees, thin-trunked and standing in tight ranks, like Birnam Wood waiting for the order to move.

I asked Baxter, the custodian of the Ossian Sweet house, about El-Sayed’s call for national solidarity on healthcare, and whether a similar solidarity might ever bring Detroit and its suburbs together. Like most Black people I spoke to, he was sceptical and fatalistic: the division was just there, like the weather. ‘Those type of things and issues that have existed in the city of Detroit have been normalised to the point where it’s just something that we live with on a day-to-day basis … You know, people are more concerned about where they’re going to get their next dollar to feed their family and put food on the table.’ Henderson said something similar. ‘Most people who choose where they live, I think, give very little thought to the implications of those borders. They know what those borders are, and they know what they’re getting by choosing this place over that. They don’t think about what that means in terms of hierarchy or class.’

Since the bankruptcy, Detroit has stabilised. The population is no longer falling. The streetlights and rubbish collection are working again. The city feels safe. Regeneration is performing its mysterious work downtown and along the riverfront, even if it still lacks people to fill its structures, and pockets of gentrification, for better or worse, are spreading. Michigan has plans to fill in the stump of freeway that doomed Black Bottom, the I-375, and make a people-friendly boulevard. Black people can now, at least in theory, live wherever they can afford, and many of them have moved out to the suburbs, some to greater prosperity and better schools, others to wherever they can find a place. As in London, there is a suburbanisation of poverty.

Dueweke has observed the shift in race relations in his lifetime. As a white teenager in 1966 he walked past the house of the first Black family to move into his neighbourhood; somebody had hurled a can of green paint over the family’s brand-new car. Forty years later, the financial crisis hit the suburbs too. ‘A friend who lives in one of the very nice neighbourhoods in north-west Detroit always says that the biggest thing that ever happened to integration in Metro Detroit was the financial crisis, because when all of these suburban houses started getting foreclosed on in the inner-ring suburbs, and all the Black people, like my neighbour, moved to them, the people in the suburbs were like, well, we’d rather have Black people there than an empty house.’

Despite​ the historical focus on 8 Mile Road as the great divide between Detroit and the suburbs, the sharper socioeconomic boundary is in the east, between Detroit and the five independent suburbs of the Pointes, which face Lake St Clair: Grosse Pointe, and Grosse Pointe Park, Farms, Shores and Woods. The Pointes are among the wealthiest areas in Metro Detroit. Grosse Pointe Shores has the Grosse Pointe Yacht Club – ‘Ladies may wear appropriate hats’ – and the former mansion of Henry Ford’s son, Edsel, filled with architectural salvage from English manor houses.

To cross into the Pointes from the Detroit neighbourhoods of Morningside or Jefferson Chalmers is to pass jarringly from great stretches of grassed-over lots and slumped, hollow-eyed houses to a zone of intensely green, inch-clipped lawns and immaculately painted clapboard. The Pointes’ fenced-in public parks are only for residents, who need a card for access. Sporadic buses to and from Detroit are used almost exclusively by Black workers, but as I found when I tried to catch one, there are parts of the Pointes where the authorities don’t want to have anything so vulgar as a bus stop.

Thanks to the area’s wealth, the single school district covering the Pointes is lavishly endowed with facilities and staff. Only last year voters gave the school board the authority to borrow $120 million over the next twenty years, a huge sum for an area with only fifty thousand people. Unlike many other school districts in the suburbs, Grosse Pointe doesn’t take part in Michigan’s Schools of Choice programme, which lets students from one district study in another. But a law currently working its way through the state legislature would change this, forcing the Pointes to take children from Detroit or other suburbs. Earlier this year a posse of objectors, led by the billionaire activist Sean Cotton, went to the state capitol in Lansing to complain about this plan, which they framed as an attack on local rights. Cotton had already put hundreds of thousands of dollars into a Super PAC to help a MAGA-adjacent slate of candidates get elected alongside him to the supposedly non-partisan school board (his own term ends this year). One of his confrères, Peter Ochs, told legislators in Lansing that the new law ‘would allow for an influx of students who are frankly not prepared to take the instruction that has been set up’. Another, Terence Collins, evoked the racial conflicts of the civil rights era when he accused supporters of the new law of violating a Supreme Court decision from 1974 that stopped Detroit bussing students across school district boundaries. Grosse Pointe objectors were party to that case, too.

Despite all this, the electoral and demographic evidence suggests that the area leans to the left. Cotton’s vast inherited wealth couldn’t stop his faction losing control of the school board to liberals. In the 2024 presidential election, all but one of the Pointes’ electoral precincts – the super-rich Grosse Pointe Shores – went for Harris. In the recent primary, the Pointes broke for El-Sayed. This disposition does not, however, extend to enthusiasm for, or even consideration of, redistributional plans for education, such as merging the Pointes’ school district with Detroit’s. That’s not on any official agenda, but it doesn’t mean it doesn’t come up.

Graig Donnelly, a building project manager for non-profits who lives with his wife and two young children in Grosse Pointe Park, told me that ‘at a macro scale of what’s going on in my country, I think it’s easier for people to feel like they’re being liberal or progressive. But then, when it comes to their own backyard and resources for my school, where my kid is, or my private waterfront park that only residents have access to – all of a sudden, it changes, right? … I have had lots of otherwise progressive-sounding folks get mad at me because I want to talk about opening the parks to everyone. “Oh well, then there won’t be room for everyone.” I’m like, I mean, what are you talking about? And these are people who will, in the national conversation, be progressive about immigration. You know, “I can’t believe what ICE is doing.” Yes, but when we talk about not having people show their ID or their pass to get into our parks, and just letting anybody who comes come in, they all of a sudden want to be protectionist.’

On the other side of the border, as locals call the boundary between the Pointes and Detroit, the Democrats were bickering. After I left the city, big national names like Obama and Harris endorsed and campaigned with El-Sayed, but the fallout from the primary was still in the air, and although Rogers seemed to be running a dreadful campaign, reversing long-held positions and all but accusing El-Sayed to his face of being a terrorist, there was unease over the Democratic candidate’s failure to break through with Black voters in Detroit. El-Sayed was expected to bring back to the fold young voters and Arab Americans lost over Gaza in 2024, but most Black people I met said the party took their vote for granted. Democratic canvassers in the city weren’t trying to persuade undecided voters to choose El-Sayed; they were trying to persuade long-time Democrats to vote. At a meeting of Democrat supporters in the back room of a café in Morningside, an African American Muslim turned on an Arab American man, talking of anti-Black slurs from Arab Americans running gas stations and Chaldeans – Arab American Christians – running liquor stores. A Jewish woman said her friends wondered why El-Sayed hadn’t visited this or that synagogue; some of them wouldn’t have him, she was told. The organiser of the meeting made some cutting remarks about the city’s failure to care properly for elderly people in winter, offending the city council member for east Detroit, Latisha Johnson, who came in as she was speaking. An elderly Black woman said she worried about the number of Black people her age who told her ‘they were just going to sit this one out.’

I met Johnson later in her office downtown. One of eight children, she had a tough upbringing; she was the first in her family to go to college. Although hers is supposed to be a non-partisan role, she made it clear she backed El-Sayed, and criticised so-called progressives in the city who hadn’t been wholehearted in their support. She didn’t name names, but she may have been thinking of her fellow councillor Mary Waters, who had a sit-down meeting with Rogers soon after the primary.

Johnson was sceptical about the future of the utopian end of the urban farming movement. She wanted to encourage capitalist redevelopers if that would repopulate the city and rebuild the tax base: ‘Four years from now, when we look at the numbers from the 2030 census, I don’t want the conversation for District 4 to be that we have to expand the boundaries any further,’ she said. And to the farmers, with a little impatience: ‘You buy time, you do the farming because you can do that, you can eat off of it, but then when somebody comes to develop and development gets closer to you, you sell the land for $100,000 or whatever it is.’

I was in Morningside one afternoon when the first autumn rains were coming in. There had been a tornado a week or so earlier. I visited a Land Bank house on view with a starting price of $1000. It had huge holes in the floor and was disconnected from utilities, but fundamentally sound – indeed, a previous buyer had already tried and failed to restore it in time for the Land Bank to sign off on it (buyers have six months to do this), leaving a solid interior frame for plasterboard walls to be fixed to. It was watched over by a retired drugs squad cop known as Goose, who entertained me with an hour of stories about the crack days (‘I’ve seen rocks so pretty, cocaine rocks, and you know, it’s tempting’). One of the people who came to look at the house was Sharice, a 45-year-old Black IT worker from the suburbs who was looking to move back to the city, do up the house herself, get a cheap side lot to grow vegetables – side lots next to auctioned houses cost $100 – and live more cheaply. ‘With the increased cost of living right now, like I’ve experienced that or noticed it, how it’s trending high, and especially for groceries, and not that we’re getting better food, it’s kind of worse actually.’

At the other end of the same block, Will and his husband, Sean, both white men, invited me into their house, which they’re slowly restoring, for coffee. Garlic, beans and chillis from the side lot they’re cultivating sat in an old crate on the table; verbena, hyssop and chamomile were being dried in bowls. Two chunky dogs patrolled the kitchen. Both men have jobs; the smallholding, they said, was a hobby. In their five years in the neighbourhood, they’d made friends, but there were squatters in a nearby house, which was owned by an impenetrably layered legal structure. The police wouldn’t act. The house had no electricity or sanitation, and the squatters had a young child living with them.

Halfway between Will and Sean and the Land Bank house was the home of Demetrious Smith, a Black auto worker and paterfamilias who plays guitar in church on Sundays. I asked if he was going to vote and he said he would, though he wasn’t full of enthusiasm. ‘I think they all do whatever’s best for them,’ he said, meaning politicians. ‘I don’t think anybody really cares what happens to African Americans. You just gotta pick the poison which is gonna hurt you the least.’ He mentioned what he called ‘the transgender movement’. ‘We’re Christians. A lot of the stuff they’re trying to implement, I don’t want to be part of that.’ But he was optimistic about the city. ‘Detroit has done a total 360, kind of bringing it back. Right now, you wouldn’t recognise downtown from where it was.’

Johnson recalled the Black mayor Coleman Young pleading with Black people not to leave the city. ‘Someone just told me not long ago that folks who were around then, who left, they have an extreme amount of guilt, and I think it’s because they left, and it was not Black people who came in after them.’ She had little time for elaborate administrative schemes to unite the city and the suburbs. Her vision was more gradual. ‘I’m trying to get us to a point where you can’t tell the difference, you can’t tell which city you’re in … If we just keep our area clean and our lawn mowed.’

A mix of city money, fixer-uppers and gentrifiers has already changed Morningside. Here, in a state that once sentenced the MC5 manager and poet John Sinclair to ten years in prison for selling two joints, weed vendors have opened stores on the Detroit side of the border, hoping to attract aficionados from the Pointes, which have declined to take advantage of Michigan’s legalisation of marijuana. The wealth gap is still starkly visible, but these days there is something more hip about the Detroit side.

Along Alter Road, some streets that should connect majority Black Detroit to the majority white Pointes still have barriers that were put up in the 1980s following petitions from Pointes residents to close the routes on obscure grounds of redundancy or high traffic. They stand now as symbols of division, not just between rich and poor, Black and white, but between different modalities of social democracy. I spoke to Michele Hodges, the mayor of Grosse Pointe Park, about one such barrier, an actual brick wall running across Goethe Street. She told me she’d formed a group called Building Bridges with the mayor of Detroit to get it taken down. The group met a dozen times. And somehow they couldn’t do it. ‘This was a very daunting task to take on because of all the history, and the racism, the ’67 rebellion … and I brought in the people from the far right who were serving on the city council when the brick walls were built, and people on the far left, saying, “We’ve got to solve this problem.” … There were some people who were upset because I didn’t go out there and tear down the wall overnight with a bulldozer. I can’t practically do that, but let’s say I was willing to do that, I don’t think that’s the healthiest route. I think we need to see it as a journey.’
