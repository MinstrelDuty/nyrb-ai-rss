# 正文

Ifirst heard​ about what was being done to migrants and refugees in Libya in the autumn of 2015. I was reporting from the port of Augusta in eastern Sicily, where ships – European navy vessels, commercial tankers, fishing boats – would dock to discharge people rescued from the Mediterranean. I had been there several times before, but this year, a doctor treating refugees told me, the injuries were far more severe. Some were caused by the boats or by the crossing itself: dehydration and sunburn from days at sea, chemical burns from leaking engine oil. Others came from mistreatment in Libya: scabies and stomach problems; injuries consistent with beatings, torture and rape. Some people, the doctor told me, arrived with broken legs and feet – injuries consistent with high-impact landings. These patients had been thrown from buildings by their jailers to speed up ransom payments.

Later, I met people who could confirm these accounts themselves. But I remember that conversation on the quayside and the powerful urge I felt to write it down, to let people know what was happening. I wasn’t alone in doing so. That year was the peak of Europe’s so-called refugee crisis, and hundreds of journalists were reporting from Sicily, the Aegean, the Balkans. A few had malicious intent: I remember seeing a reporter from Russian state television wearing a medical mask as he did a piece to camera from Augusta, as if reporting on a plague. But many, I imagine, were feeling some version of what I felt. People were suffering, and by letting others know about it we were contributing somehow to alleviating that suffering.

What changed? A recent investigation by Forensis, a Berlin-based research group, provides fresh evidence that the suffering continues, only now with a patina of official legitimacy. A short film, *Escaping Libya’s Detention Industry*, charts a transition from smugglers’ compounds, through which migrants were trafficked by organised criminal networks for profit, to state-run detention centres that, in theory, form part of a humane system of immigration control. In practice, the new centres are much like the old ones – and in some cases, operated by the same people.

Forensis makes its case by comparing examples of the two types of prison, reconstructed via satellite imagery, architectural models, eyewitness accounts and video footage. The first example, a huge smugglers’ compound at Bani Walid, to the south-east of Tripoli, was established after Gaddafi’s overthrow in 2011. Smuggling – of people, weapons, fuel oil – increased as the Libyan state fell apart, to be replaced by a patchwork of local militias, many of which had links to gangsters. The compound in Bani Walid was built by the Diab crime family. Its most profitable activity involved detaining people who had crossed the Sahara, extorting money from them, pimping out their labour and passing them on to other smugglers, who would charge them yet more money for a boat journey towards Europe. Moussa Diab, the head of the family, allegedly spent his profits on lavish parties in Dubai.

The more recent examples – at Ain Zara and Al Mayah, on the outskirts of Tripoli – are run by Libya’s Government of National Unity. (The GNU has been at war with a rival government, led by the military commander Khalifa Haftar.) In areas the GNU controls, which include Tripoli and its hinterland, the old smugglers’ compounds have been shut down. (Bani Walid ceased operating in the late 2010s.) Instead, undocumented migrants are detained indefinitely in centres run by the Department for Combating Illegal Migration. These facilities have received enthusiastic support from European governments: before 2011, Gaddafi positioned the country as Europe’s border cop by restricting Mediterranean crossings, and European leaders have been keen to see Libya resume this role. The EU and Britain have provided funding for DCIM centres, while the EU collaborates with the GNU’s security forces to intercept boats at sea and return them to Libya. Yet as the Forensis film shows, the centres at both Ain Zara and Al Mayah were established by militias involved in trafficking. And just like Bani Walid, both continue to be sites of abuse. Survivors of each of the three centres describe dire living conditions, violence and extortion.

I still struggle to accept that exposing the abuse didn’t end it, even though I know that simply drawing attention to a problem is rarely enough. But as the world gets more violent, the gap between knowledge and action seems larger than ever. Forensis, which was founded in 2021 as an independent spin-off from the research organisation Forensic Architecture, is part of a constellation of groups looking for ways both to expose injustice and ensure that accountability follows. In part, they take their cue from the technology we now have at our fingertips. Everyday devices – not least smartphones – have drastically expanded our ability to record and monitor the world around us, while a range of specialist techniques exist to analyse information at scale or piece it together from fragments. Almost no event, whether fast-moving and chaotic or deliberately obscured, goes unrecorded – so long as you know where to look.

Getting access to Libya has long been extremely difficult for international journalists, human rights investigators and other information-gatherers, just as it has been potentially deadly for their Libyan colleagues to carry out this kind of work. Nevertheless, the ongoing abuse in smugglers’ compounds and ‘official’ detention centres alike has been repeatedly documented, often with a mix of traditional and innovative methods. In *My Fourth Time, We Drowned*, for instance, the journalist Sally Hayden managed to contact people being shunted around Libya’s migrant prisons and then collate their experiences via messages sent surreptitiously from phones that had been smuggled into the detention centres.

The difficulty is that the most powerful tools for sensing – and for sense-making – are not evenly distributed. State authorities, for instance, have far greater resources than either journalists or detainees. The EU’s border agency, Frontex, uses its billion-euro annual budget to run aerial surveillance operations in the Mediterranean that help the Libyan coastguard to intercept migrant boats. An investigation by Human Rights Watch in 2022 found that the agency conceals the extent of its activities, redacting documents released through freedom of information requests and denying investigators permission to visit its surveillance base in Warsaw. Frontex’s main activity – forcing migrants back to Libya in the full knowledge of what awaits them – is further concealed by the euphemisms and distortions of the politicians who built the system. In this world, the only villains are the traffickers. Many voters in Europe accept this mutual delusion, despite what evidence they might see to the contrary.

Forensis pursues what it hopes is a more effective challenge to this status quo than the traditional approaches. Building on the work of its parent organisation, which began as a research project at Goldsmiths, University of London in 2010, Forensis carries out what it calls ‘counter-investigations’. These bring together a wide range of documentary and imaging techniques to amass evidence in a way that not only builds a case, but asks the reader or viewer to question the way the tools work and in what ways they can be manipulated.

Forensic Architecture, which continues operating separately from Forensis (much of the former’s work now focuses on Gaza, as well as theoretical research), has won plaudits for this approach to human rights investigations. Some projects, such as the digital mapping of CIA drone strikes against the Taliban, or the use of machine-learning tools to trace police violence during the Hong Kong protests in 2019, apply innovative technology to a traditional form of human rights advocacy, focused on unpicking discrete events and identifying the likely perpetrators. Others take aim at historical or structural injustice: an ongoing project examines sites in Namibia related to the genocide of Ovaherero and Nama peoples by German troops in the 1900s, while investigations into police shootings in the US, UK, Greece, Palestine and Turkey have exposed cultural and ethnic bias in the actions of officers. In 2019, Forensic Architecture was invited to produce a film for the Whitney Biennial; its response was a film investigating the use of tear gas made by a company belonging to a member of the museum’s board.

As the name suggests, Forensic Architecture has its roots in a specific discipline: its founder, Eyal Weizman, is an architect who began his career working with human rights groups monitoring Israel’s construction of the West Bank separation wall. Advanced spatial imaging techniques are central to its work: in 2016, for instance, it used a 3D model to reconstruct the layout and cells of Saydnaya prison in Syria – at the time, the heart of the Assad regime’s machine for torturing and executing its opponents. The reconstruction was made using survivors’ recollections, but since prisoners were kept in the dark, participants drew on their memories of changes in temperature, moisture, light, vibrations and sound to create the model. Like many of Forensic Architecture’s projects, the investigation was carried out in collaboration with a more traditional human rights organisation – in this case, Amnesty International. But FA brought conceptual innovation as well as art-world glitz. I first came across the Saydnaya investigation at a Forensic Architecture exhibition at the ICA in 2018, the same year the organisation was nominated for the Turner Prize. I had mixed feelings about the exhibition: the subject matter was certainly important, but it sat awkwardly in the gallery setting. What wins out in such a context: the evidence or the aesthetics?

At their most successful, such interventions have played on their surroundings. At the opening of documenta 2017, which was attended by leading German politicians, Forensic Architecture re-enacted a notorious racist murder in Kassel. (The culprits belonged to a neo-Nazi group that had been allowed to carry out a string of murders under German state surveillance; FA’s research was later used in an official inquiry into the killings.) When that doesn’t happen, though, the risk is that such work stays stuck in the worlds of art and academia – and that discussion focuses on its creators.

To what other uses could these techniques be put? Open-source intelligence – the practice of analysing publicly available information about an event – is now done by most traditional media organisations, while many NGOs have established their own high-tech investigative units. These techniques aren’t inherently subversive: in October 2023, for instance, the Centre for Information Resilience – an investigative agency founded by two Foreign Office veterans – was contracted to provide regular updates to the British government on the situation in Gaza. Its information hasn’t stopped the government from repeatedly trying to wriggle out of its obligations under the Genocide Convention and other international human rights treaties, for instance by continuing to supply parts for lethal F-35 jets to Israel. (In April the Foreign Office withdrew funding for the specialist internal human rights unit that monitored Gaza, ending the CIR’s contract altogether.)

Forensis is an attempt to make a virtue of these shifts, by focusing on practical human rights investigations and building a network of collaborators who can extend their reach. Since Forensis’s own researchers couldn’t visit Libya themselves, they established relationships with two groups. The first comprised Libyans, identified via the legal network Lawyers for Justice in Libya, who helped Forensis to collect on-the-ground footage, supplemented by testimony from locals who could describe the way the detention centres had developed over time. A striking sequence in *Escaping Libya’s Detention Industry* features the Diab compound at Bani Walid, visible only in footage snatched by a phone camera placed on the dashboard of a passing car. More obvious filming is impossible. Using the information compiled from the Libyan sources, the researchers at Forensis traced the development of the trafficking industry through changes in Bani Walid’s built environment. At first the traffickers held people in abandoned industrial buildings. Later, as profits grew, they constructed purpose-built sites whose layout and function closely resemble today’s government-run detention centres.

The second group comprised survivors of the prisons. Computer models of the compounds at Bani Walid, Ain Zara and Al Mayah were created with the help of former inmates now living in Europe. The tools for creating these models include QGIS, a mapping program that allows buildings to be given precise geographical locations; Blender, modelling software used to design interiors; and Unreal, a video game engine that allows the user to move through a 3D environment.

Unreal is often used for first-person shooters – the militaristic adventures that generate billions for the gaming industry each year. Here, however, it’s harnessed for what Forensis calls ‘situated testimony’: an interviewing technique developed in collaboration with psychiatrists to help survivors recover and situate traumatic memories. This serves several purposes. It helps to break down the traditional hierarchy between researcher and subject. Survivors lead the sessions, with the researchers on hand mainly to provide guidance on how to use the technology. But it’s also a way of questioning the standards by which evidence is judged. The many forms of ‘hard’ evidence – of data, documents, images – now available to us have diminished the value of individual testimony. An investigation like *Escaping Libya’s Detention Industry* attempts to restore its prominence by marrying it with technological innovation.

Towards the end of the film we are shown a map of the Mediterranean, on which are plotted the many sea journeys taken by two survivors of the government-run detention centres. The map, a computer-generated image that resembles the kind of material handed out at a government press briefing, is a mess of zigzags showing how each attempt to reach Europe was foiled by EU surveillance and the Libyan coastguard, returning the passengers to prison in Libya. (Martin, one of the two survivors, only succeeded on his ninth attempt.) This functions as a kind of commentary on the slick effusions of data and graphics pumped out by an organisation like Frontex and the lies they conceal. The journeys on this map have been plotted by Forensis researchers and the survivors working together, trying to visualise the latter’s necessarily partial memories. We believe them because of the film’s patient accumulation of detail.

This painstaking, collaborative approach also feeds into a wider campaign for legal accountability. Lawyers for Justice in Libya, which helped to gather information for the Forensis investigation, has submitted a criminal complaint about mistreatment in Bani Walid and elsewhere to Dutch prosecutors, who are investigating crimes committed in Libya. In January 2026, an Eritrean national known as ‘Walid’ was sentenced to twenty years in prison in the Netherlands for the torture of migrants in Libya, while the trial of another alleged trafficker is ongoing. LFJL is trying to encourage prosecutors to treat such crimes as systemic, rather than focus on isolated cases.

One of the persistent problems in discussing the situation for refugees in Libya is that attention is too easily focused on the immediate perpetrators of violence: traffickers such as the Diab family and the militias who now sport DCIM uniforms. In May, the former Libyan militia leader Khaled Mohamed Ali El Hishri will face trial at the International Criminal Court on seventeen counts of crimes against humanity and war crimes for the alleged torture, rape and other severe mistreatment of detainees in a militia-run prison outside Tripoli. ‘This case should mark only the beginning,’ according to a recent communiqué from LFJL and other organisations that urges the ICC ‘to pursue those most responsible – whether Libyan or European actors – who perpetrate and enable these systematic crimes’. But it’s harder to capture the way a decision taken thousands of miles away contributes to the problem; for that, it’s necessary to draw attention to larger patterns and processes. One benefit of the work of Forensis and like-minded researchers is that they provide the rest of us with a template to copy.
