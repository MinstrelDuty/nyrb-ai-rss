# 正文

On​ 16 June, a yacht flying the Red Ensign and a Russian frigate called *Admiral Grigorovich* approached each other in the English Channel, around twenty nautical miles south of the Isle of Wight and thus outside the United Kingdom’s territorial waters. As the distance between the two vessels lessened to about a quarter of a mile, the warship blasted its horn five times, repeated the quintuple blasts a minute later and then fired several rifle shots. The retired British couple on the yacht changed course and sailed away under power. Keir Starmer, still prime minister, was quick to condemn Russia, but a spokesperson for the Ministry of Defence was more circumspect, attributing the warning shots to the frigate’s attempt to prevent a collision. (The yacht’s owners groused to the *Times* and the *Telegraph* about the MoD’s attitude and denied that they had done anything wrong.) As a warship, *Admiral Grigorovich* has sovereign immunity and, like any vessel, it can sail freely through maritime areas not designated as a littoral country’s territorial waters.

The encounter occurred two days after Royal Marine commandos roped down at night onto the deck of a merchant tanker, *Smyrtos*, where it sat at anchor off Weymouth, seizing the vessel along with the 100,000 tonnes of Russian crude oil it was carrying and arresting its captain for sanctions-breaking. Although the ship had set off under a Cameroonian flag, it had lost this registration en route, after European pressure forced Cameroon to deregister 39 Russian oil tankers. The UK claims that Russia has a ‘shadow fleet’ of more than seven hundred ships, which carries 75 per cent of its sanctioned petroleum around the globe – the real numbers are hard to ascertain. Since the start of the year, European navies have seized twelve Russian vessels, including eight flying the Cameroonian flag. ‘Another bad day for Putin’, Starmer posted on TikTok after the seizure of *Smyrtos*, and Volodymyr Zelensky expressed his gratitude to Britain. Operations against Russia have not ceased under Andy Burnham. At the end of August, navy helicopters and warships tailed the Russian destroyer *Admiral Levchenko*, which was escorting a tanker and a vehicle-carrier – both flagged to Russia – through the North Sea and the Channel. The navy boasted that it had ‘forced’ Russia into sending ‘costly military resources’ to the area. They were referring to the Russian frigate *Neutrashimy*, which only a few weeks earlier had conducted a live-fire exercise forty nautical miles south of Plymouth.

In April, British naval vessels and aircraft tracked three Russian submarines in the North Atlantic, again outside UK territorial waters. John Healey, the then defence secretary, claimed that the submarines had been surveying underwater pipelines and telecommunications cables. HMS *St Albans* dropped buoys at intervals to let one of the submarines know it was watching. After a time, the submarines, two of them from Russia’s Main Directorate of Deep Sea Research (known as GUGI), returned to their base in Olenya Guba, near Murmansk in the Arctic Circle. Starmer once again celebrated: ‘I am determined to protect the British people from paying the price for Putin’s aggression in their household bills.’ The government claimed that there had been a 30 per cent increase over the last two years in the activities of Russian vessels in what it vaguely defined as ‘UK waters’, deliberately blurring the distinction between the UK’s territorial waters (twelve nautical miles from the shore) and its exclusive economic zone (two hundred nautical miles), presumably to exaggerate the Russian threat.

In 2015, Trevor Paglen put on an exhibition of photographs and films of surveillance landscapes round the world. He included images he had taken of underwater internet cable ‘choke points’. He had used nautical charts, Edward Snowden’s files and GPS technology to locate some of the nodes where multiple cables come together. These are the sites of NSA wiretaps, which enable the US to intercept much of the world’s internet traffic. Along with murky blue-green images of barnacled, seaweed-covered cables, Paglen displayed a two-foot-long model of the nuclear submarine USS *Jimmy Carter*. It was launched in 2004, and according to the *New York Times,* ‘extensively modified from its basic design, given a hull extension that allows it to house technicians and gear to perform the cable-tapping and other secret missions’. Its intelligence and surveillance capabilities are no doubt now even more sophisticated.

After Russia’s invasion of Ukraine in 2022, the number of maritime encounters between Russian ships and European or Nato naval forces surged to a post-Cold War high. But even before then, these navies had been provoking one another in the Mediterranean, the Baltic and the Black Sea. In June 2021, HMS *Defender* sailed from Odesa (at the time still under Ukrainian sovereignty) to Georgia, passing within twelve miles of Crimea. Russia described this as a violation of its maritime borders; Britain called it a routine transit through Ukrainian waters. A year earlier, the US warship *John S. McCain* entered Russian waters in the Sea of Japan and was expelled after the Russian navy threatened to ram it (the US navy disputes this account).

The warship has a chequered history. In 2017, as it was overtaking the merchant tanker *Alnic* near Singapore, it turned towards port, directly into the path of the other vessel. Ten US sailors were killed in the collision and the *John S. McCain* suffered more than $100 million worth of damage. Subsequent investigations by the US navy and the National Transportation Safety Board indicated one of the causes of the collision was that the warship was following the then US policy of not transmitting its position on the Automatic Identification System (AIS). The technology, which now uses satellite systems, began as a ship-to-ship communication facility designed to help avoid collisions.

Ships turn off their AIS transponders for a variety of reasons. Naval vessels of all nations mask their position far more often than they acknowledge. Commercial ships try to evade detection by a perceived foe – the tax authorities, environmental and labour inspectors, pirates, coastguard vessels, border enforcers, EUNAVFOR, FRONTEX or Team America World Police – by disabling their AIS. Some use ‘spoofing’ technology to transmit their location from false co-ordinates (this can result in digital mapping anomalies such as ships appearing to sail on land).

Western governments aren’t the only bodies that are unhappy about ghost ships and shadow fleets. Unregistered and undetectable fleets cut into the profits of the big tanker companies. After the first Trump administration imposed sanctions on Iranian and Venezuelan oil, the number of ghost ships carrying these countries’ oil tripled and the price of old ships due to be scrapped increased because they started to be redeployed as ghost ships. Since 2022, sanctions on the ports, producers and infrastructures that allow the circulation of Russian oil have led to the further spread of these shadow fleets.

Meanwhile, states are increasingly weaponising their control over strategic maritime passages. The ability of Iran and the Yemeni Houthis to shut down commercial shipping travelling through choke points near their shores has changed the balance of power between the US and its far less powerful adversaries. Iran and the Ansar Allah administration in Yemen (also known as the Houthis) have invoked the UN Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS) and its definition of territorial waters to assert their right to the Strait of Hormuz and the Bab al-Mandab Strait respectively, and have used domestically manufactured armaments, including drones and inexpensive missiles, to force the closure of shipping lanes. Around 12 per cent by value of global maritime trade passes through the Bab al-Mandab Strait, at the southern end of the Red Sea corridor; between 20 and 30 per cent of the world’s oil, as well as significant quantities of aluminium, sulphur, ammonia and urea, move along the Strait of Hormuz. Both Iran and Yemen have used AIS information to target commercial vessels that are owned or operated by, or carry goods to and from the US, Israel and their Gulf allies. Yemen targets ships carrying Saudi oil through the Red Sea, while Iran has announced that it plans to sell the Israeli-owned ships it has seized to compensate Iranian fishermen. These vessels are chartered and operated by the world’s largest shipping firm, Switzerland-based MSC, but are owned by Zodiac Maritime, whose main investor is the Israeli magnate (and major donor to London arts institutions) Eyal Ofer.

In a recent paper for the S. Rajartnam School of International Studies in Singapore, the former Indonesian deputy minister of maritime affairs, Arif Havas Oegroseno, describes the implications of what he calls the ‘sea denial trap’: ‘Every projection of naval force into littoral Asia now runs through waters where the trap can be set – legally through coastal state rights under UNCLOS, geographically through straits and island chains, and kinetically through weapons any middle power can field. The era in which a superior fleet could have access at acceptable cost is closing.’ That era was defined by the 19th-century military strategist Alfred Thayer Mahan, who argued that vast naval power would inevitably allow empires to hold sway over weaker nations. ‘Mahan taught the world to count battleships,’ Oegroseno writes. But ‘the century ahead will belong to those who count choke points, legal entitlements and the price of admission.’

Oegroseno sees the pivotal historic moment as the First United Nations Conference on the Law of the Sea, held in 1958, at which the great naval powers – the US, the UK and European colonial metropoles – faced off against the sovereign states of Latin America, Asia and Africa. The arguments revolved around the definition of territorial waters and the rights and privileges of foreign navies when sailing near the coasts of other states. Between February and April that year, the conference’s First Committee held 66 meetings. The most revealing session was the fifth, at which Alberto Ulloa Sotomayor of Peru and Sir Reginald Manningham-Buller of the UK both spoke. While Manningham-Buller (whose daughter Eliza went on to run MI5) insisted that territorial waters should extend only three nautical miles from shore so as to allow foreign ships freedom of navigation, Third World countries demanded a wider band (it was eventually set at twelve miles). Ulloa argued that it was significant that ‘certain important maritime states [which] started by asserting their rights of dominion over the sea, [have] become advocates of the freedom of the seas’ now that newly decolonised states were demanding sovereignty over their shorelines, adjacent seas and continental shelf. Both Manningham-Buller and Ulloa recognised that ships were shards of sovereign power, carrying with them the force of law and jurisdiction of their home countries. Manningham-Buller saw this power as a right possessed by nations with large navies; Ulloa, recognising the imperial impetus behind the notion of the ‘freedom of the seas’, wanted to circumscribe the ship’s power in law. This is the basis of the current struggle between the US navy, which claims the world’s oceans as its own, and the Iranian government, which wants to assert control over the Strait of Hormuz.
