# 正文

Olga Freidenberg,​ a Russian literary scholar born in Odessa in 1890, best known as a cousin and youthful confidante of Boris Pasternak, had what looks on the face of it like a successful Soviet career. Child of a family of the pre-revolutionary intelligentsia, which in the early Soviet state was both a minus and a plus, she benefited from the revolution, entering university with the first class to admit women in the 1917-18 school year. In 1925, feeling that her scholarly merits had not been sufficiently recognised, she appealed to Anatoly Lunacharsky, People’s Commissar of Enlightenment in the first Bolshevik government and a notorious soft touch for ‘bourgeois intellectuals’. Lunacharsky recommended her to the academician Nikolai Marr, whose theory of language had some correspondence with the literary theory that Freidenberg herself was developing. Marr became her patron, putting her on the track that led to her appointment as head of classical philology in an institute that merged with Leningrad University in 1932, and three years later to the award of a doctoral degree (not a PhD, but a step higher, on the German model). She was reportedly the first woman to achieve this status in literary studies. After a wartime hiatus, when she remained in Leningrad while her university evacuated to Saratov, she resumed her position as professor and department head at Leningrad University and held it until a year before her retirement in 1951.

But Freidenberg, who was completely uninterested in the feminist aspect, was far from seeing her own life as a success story, and most Russian commentators – drawn to the trope of ‘the tragedy of the Russian intelligentsia’ – have agreed. It’s true that her time was marked by suffering, disappointment and endless professional conflict. The intellectual world in which Freidenberg lived was riven by factionalism, broadly speaking between communists who called themselves ‘proletarian’ and their more Westernised, cosmopolitan opponents, who were at risk of being labelled ‘bourgeois’. The Communist Party, though constantly insisting it represented the ‘proletarians’, tried to stay out of these fights in the 1920s, and in 1932 Stalin attempted to end them by closing down the ‘proletarian’ cultural organisations. But the tradition of mutual malice remained, and Stalin himself – bored, no doubt, by the end of inter-factional fighting within the Party, though it was one of his big political achievements – developed a taste for intervening.

Freidenberg, though always at risk of falling into the ‘bourgeois’/‘cosmopolitan’/‘formalist’ camp, was effectively protected by her patron, Marr, a personal favourite of Stalin’s. When her first book, *The Poetics of Plot and Genre*, ran into trouble in 1936, Freidenberg – like many other loyal Soviet citizens of the time – wrote to Stalin, in his capacity as benevolent father, complaining of unjust harassment by colleagues. He passed the letter on – with the instruction ‘fix this’ – to the head of the Central Committee’s School Department, Boris Volin, who intervened on her side. But even that did not spare her the soul-wrenching experience of being ostracised and scapegoated by colleagues who were happy to see her humiliated. The Great Purges were just beginning, a time when arbitrarily declared ‘enemies of the people’ were routinely denounced, arrested and banished. Freidenberg’s brother, Sasha, was one of those random victims, seemingly pulled in by the fact that his wife was having an affair with her boss, an Old Bolshevik unmasked as an ‘enemy’. Freidenberg would later write eloquently of ‘the horror of our uncertain position’, even (or particularly) for the privileged professional and political Soviet elite to which Freidenberg belonged. ‘Whispers, rumours, intimations, conjectures … Tongues kept wagging about me. The pack was closing in.’ Life was being ‘poisoned secretly, invisibly, as witches and sorcerers were hounded in the Dark Ages’.

Stalin was not the villain of the piece for Freidenberg at this point. Almost the opposite: after all, he had recognised the justice of her cause at the university and come to her rescue. It’s hard to get a clear picture of her attitudes in the 1930s, as her ‘diary’ of the period was written retrospectively, after her eyes were opened to the realities of Soviet repression. But it seems that before the war, far from being some sort of secret dissident, she was a regular ‘Soviet person, who wanted to explore, understand, respect and build new things’ – that is, someone with standard Soviet values who identified with the goal of ‘building socialism’. When things went wrong, she did not blame them on Stalin but (following the official Soviet line) attributed them to ‘acts of sabotage, the tricks of local scoundrels, and the deliberate distortion of Party instructions’. Who knows, she may even have shared her lover Israel Frank-Kamenetsky’s enthusiasm for collectivisation as a form of cultural enlightenment for the backward peasantry, though in retrospect she described this as a ‘foolish infatuation’ which, out of love, she tolerated.

But then came the war and the Germans’ siege of Leningrad, a more than two-year blockade which starved the city’s residents, including Freidenberg. That experience changed her thinking irrevocably. She now divided her life into the periods before and after the blockade, turned strongly against the Soviet order and, in particular, became preoccupied with Stalin – whose public cult rose to new heights in the postwar years – and the malignity of his intrusive presence in people’s lives. Everyday life was ‘stuffed like sausage’ with Stalin, as she wrote in her diary in November 1943:

> You couldn’t go to the kitchen, sit down on a chamber pot, have lunch or walk out onto the street without Stalin turning up behind you. He got into your guts and into your soul, broke into your brain, plugged up every hole and gap, chased a person down, rang his bell, got into his bed under the blanket, haunted his memories and dreams.

All these intrusions, as she now saw, had a purpose, namely control and destruction of the individual. The manifold inconveniences and discomforts of everyday life, she realised, were not unintended consequences of regime policies but integral to a strategy of control over the population via ‘everyday terror’. ‘A deliberate system of “difficulties”’ had been created by the Soviet state: ‘Now there is no water, now, no electricity; now the telephone is broken, now the radio is silent.’ Waiting in line for transport or in shops was part of the same system: ‘Torturers! … The queues are deliberate.’ A flu epidemic was the regime’s fault too (it was ‘not in its plans’ to avoid the outbreak). The vicious endemic squabbling (*skloki*) among faculty at her university, with its ‘denunciation, slander, surveillance, backstabbing [and] clandestine accusation’, was another aspect of the strategy of control. All the troubles of Freidenberg’s life could be laid at the feet of ‘Stalin’s blood-soaked regime’. Stalin had ‘strangled’ her scholarship and debased her honour. Even the death of her lover, ‘crushed by a drunken machine’ in a traffic accident, was Stalin’s fault.

This revelation of the Soviet regime’s essential nature was developed in a series of unpublished postwar autobiographical writings that Irina Paperno calls a ‘diary-theory’ – that is, ‘a record of daily life infused with theory’. Before the war, Freidenberg had made intermittent jottings (*zapiski*) about her life, but it wasn’t until after the beginning of the siege in September 1941 that it became a regular and systematic practice, though not literally a diary (the jottings, which are undated, often cover several months). After the war, Freidenberg continued her diary writing, now focused on the repressions and squabbles she experienced in her academic life, while simultaneously writing autobiographical notes on the interwar period. Both were informed by her new theory of Stalinism as ‘everyday terror’ and Stalin as an Orwellian Big Brother.

Sometime after the war, the Leningrad Public Library inquired about the possibility of acquiring Freidenberg’s personal papers for its archives. The proposal initially ‘uplifted and inspired’ her – but then she remembered her new theoretical understanding of the Soviet regime and declined to ‘swallow the bait of the Soviet surveillance system’. Still, the episode prompted her to start curating her papers as an archive, which she kept in an iron chest in her apartment. On her death in 1955, the chest and its contents were bequeathed to a friend and languished, more or less unknown, until the 1970s. By that time, a regular, if clandestine, traffic to the West of Soviet ‘dissident’ material had developed, and Freidenberg’s papers were secretly shipped out of the Soviet Union to the Pasternak Family Trust in Oxford (Boris’s father, the painter Leonid Pasternak, had not returned to Russia after travelling to Berlin for medical treatment in 1921). This archive was the basis for Elliott Mossman’s publication in 1982 of a volume of letters between Freidenberg and Boris Pasternak, which in addition to the letters included many of Freidenberg’s autobiographical jottings, under the heading ‘Diary’.

Russian literary scholars have had mixed feelings about Freidenberg because of her reputation as a ‘fierce polemicist’ in academic debates; and the absence of a complete Russian edition of her autobiographical writing was evidently related to the fact that her notebooks contain many damaging and offensive attacks on colleagues. In 2015, her papers were transferred to the Hoover Institution, where they came to the attention of Paperno, a distinguished Russian-American literary scholar at Berkeley with a particular interest in Soviet memoirs. In her edition of the writings, Paperno has sensibly decided to present them not in the chronological order of Freidenberg’s life but in the order they were written – meaning that the prewar ‘diaries’, written in hindsight through the lens of theory in the late 1940s, appear after those from the blockade. This is not a comprehensive publication of Freidenberg’s jottings (more of which are to be found in the ‘Diary’ sections of Mossman’s book), but rather an analysis with extensive quotation, concluding with a 23-page chapter in which Paperno summarises and contextualises Freidenberg’s political theory. Paperno’s purpose is ‘to present Freidenberg’s notes as a source that offers theorised insights into key moments of 20th-century history, rather than provide a biography of their author’.

‘Siege’ became a key metaphor in Freidenberg’s analysis of her own life and that of Soviet society (‘The Siege of the Human Being’ was the original subtitle of her autobiographical notebooks); and she experienced Leningrad during the blockade as a ‘domestic concentration camp’ into which she had been forced by the ‘bloodthirsty serpent’, Stalin. She blamed Leningrad’s sufferings during the blockade as much on Stalin as on Hitler; in her mind, as Paperno puts it, ‘the two tyrannies, Stalin’s and Hitler’s, worked in tandem to put “man” (the human being) under siege.’ This might seem a surprising equation, given that the blockade was a product of a German military assault on the Soviet Union, not to mention that Freidenberg, as a Jew, belonged to a category Hitler had put down for extinction. But her Jewish origins weren’t a matter of much interest to Freidenberg, whereas Stalin was something like an obsession. In her view, he had ‘untied Hitler’s hands and thus let the war begin’ when he signed the Non-Aggression Pact of 1939 (broken by Hitler when he attacked the Soviet Union in June 1941). The pact was a token of ‘friendship’ between tyrants that caused her ‘moral nausea’.

Freidenberg’s account of the siege is truly appalling. Life had reverted to its primitive state: they now ate more raw food than cooked and worshipped fire, when they could get fuel to light it. ‘We were no longer protected by everything that civilisation had developed over many centuries. We had no houses, no dwellings, no fuel, no warm clothing, no running water or toilets, no sewage disposal to carry off human waste, no food, no light, no protection of the law.’ ‘Well-organised hunger’ was the term coined by Freidenberg’s fellow literary intellectual Lydia Ginzburg (a near neighbour in Leningrad during the siege, though they do not seem to have been in contact) in her own blockade memoirs: the coexistence of actual famine with a still functioning bureaucratic system of differentiated distribution via rationing made a deep impression on them both. Some memoirs of the Leningrad siege tell stories of heroism on the part of the embattled population, but not Freidenberg’s. It was a Hobbesian world of every man for himself, in which nobody could be trusted. Even altruistic people who were generous to Freidenberg – an old friend and a former student came round sometimes with food – were castigated because their gifts were not regular and induced a sense of dependency. Personal relations, notably those between Freidenberg and her mother, with whom she lived, deteriorated sharply; Freidenberg shared her privileged ‘worker’s’ ration with her mother (classified as a ‘dependant’ and therefore on a lower ration), but resented it.

One of the worst things for Freidenberg was the breakdown of the sewage system, and of the civilised convention that people should not defecate in public. When Freidenberg’s large bathtub became full to the brim with excrement, it was an ‘intrusion of primordial chaos’. It was beyond her strength to carry it all out, bucket by bucket, down the stairs, so she had to put up with it: ‘I have grown accustomed to reckoning only with the edges of the filled bathtub and looking solely at its rim. Had the level risen? Would it overflow today, or not? – Nothing else interested me.’ (Freidenberg’s focus on excrement is probably one of the reasons her diaries and autobiographical writings have not been published in Russian. Such subjects as excrement and incontinence were not acceptable to the prudish Russian/Soviet intelligentsia.)

It would be possible to dismiss Freidenberg’s sense of Stalin’s (or the Stalinist state’s) malevolent presence in every aspect of her life as paranoia, induced by the terrible experience of the siege and reinforced by her own solipsistic view of the world. Indeed, the first reaction of many readers to Freidenberg’s theory of ‘everyday terror’ inflicted by bureaucracies might be a degree of recognition: which of us, working in a university or other large corporation, has not at some time felt that its bureaucratic processes were set up with malice aforethought to torment us? But Paperno, while recognising that readers may not necessarily warm to Freidenberg, makes an interesting case to the contrary. It is Paperno’s intention to turn Freidenberg’s diary-theory into a ‘fact of intellectual history’ that scholars will have to take account of. Freidenberg, she points out, offers a theoretical analysis of the Stalinist system that is close to the concept of totalitarianism as developed by Hannah Arendt and popularised in fiction by George Orwell. Freidenberg as a scholar was well grounded in the German philosophical tradition out of which Arendt’s writing sprang – but she appears not to have read Arendt or any other Western writer on totalitarianism, or even to have known the term. Given that Arendt’s theory, as well as that of the other main US-based theorist of totalitarianism, Carl Friedrich, drew on first-hand observation only of Nazi Germany, extrapolated to the Soviet Union, this makes Freidenberg’s ‘diary-theory’, based purely on first-hand Soviet observation, a potentially valuable document.

In Freidenberg’s theory, Hitler and Stalin are equated in a ‘double barbarism’ in which ‘two tyrants created a new system of government, unknown to Aristotle.’ The leader cult, particularly prominent in the postwar period and a constant irritation to Freidenberg, was foregrounded and carefully observed through a pioneering linguistic analysis (there have been many others since), including the interesting detail that people not only picked up Stalin’s vocabulary but some of his quirks of pronunciation. The Soviet state is a primeval monster – Freidenberg, like the German philosophers, used the image of the Babylonian goddess Tiamat from ancient myth – squashing people together so that they lose all individuality and must live in ‘organised loneliness’, in Arendt’s phrase. Marxist-Leninist ideology is in itself of no intellectual interest to Freidenberg, but she pays keen attention to the methods of its inculcation and ritual observance. Paperno even detects a ‘biopolitical’ aspect, anticipating Michel Foucault and Giorgio Agamben, in Freidenberg’s attention to the effects of control on the individual human body.

As well as insisting on its value as political theory, Paperno sees Freidenberg’s writing as a powerful ‘self-ethnography’, comparable with Victor Klemperer’s diary of Germany in the Second World War, *To the Bitter End*, published first in German and then in English in the 1990s. I don’t entirely buy this. Unlike Freidenberg, Klemperer was not a solipsist, and he tried to record what he saw without imposing preconceptions of its meaning. Freidenberg, believing that ‘the past shows its true face only in the future, retrospectively,’ and having by her own admission ‘never been able to make barriers between scientific theory and the immediate apprehension of life’, did the opposite: what she saw and recorded was what had a ‘meaning’ for her – in other words, what could be slotted into her theory. But that doesn’t mean her writings are without ethnographic interest. On the contrary, she provides an excellent ethnography of many key Soviet everyday practices, such as the writing of appeals and denunciations to the authorities, advantageous modes of self-presentation and other strategies of individual self-defence and advancement.

Freidenberg excelled in these basic Soviet citizen survival skills, as attested both by the documentary evidence she offers (she kept copies of all her appeals) and by her unusual success rate. She was clearly an energetic and fearsome participant in the academic squabbling that she came to regard not only as systemic but as a product of Stalin’s intention. In the battles of mutual public denunciation among scholars, she more than held her own, and even on one occasion provided a written denunciation of her colleagues’ political and personal failings at the request of the secret police. This was surely a cause of private shame (one of Freidenberg’s counts against the Stalinist system was the way it forced her, as well as others, to ‘debase her honour’), even though she claims in her diary that she was just setting the record straight for posterity (‘I was not writing denunciations, but only giving testimony as a witness’).

Benefiting​ from an element of luck as well as political adeptness, she not only got her university job and status back at the end of the war – despite having already reached 55, the official retirement age for women at the time, and the fact that she had failed to evacuate as instructed – but also kept her feet in the tumultuous years of the ‘anti-cosmopolitan’ campaign that followed, even though she was both Jewish and vulnerable to charges of formalism and links with the West. She even seems to have emerged more or less unscathed out of the disgrace of her patron, Marr, whose theories, partly shared by Freidenberg, were publicly denounced by Stalin in 1950. All this is not to discount the fact that these were miserable years for Freidenberg, who appears to have entered clinical depression at the end of the war as she experienced the coldness of colleagues returning from evacuation. But the sword of Damocles that hung over her, and fell on so many around her, did not in the end fall on her.

Outside the professional realm, Freidenberg’s battles with the authorities over her apartment, rations and other privileges were legion. Her self-identification was carefully tailored to the situation: sometimes ‘valued scientific worker’ and ‘Marxist’, at other times ‘ordinary worker’. One of the longest battles was with the Leningrad housing authorities, which wanted to turn her family’s residence into a communal apartment. Many ‘bourgeois’ intellectuals fought this battle and most of them lost, enduring for decades the miseries of sharing kitchen, bathroom and lavatory with lower-class strangers (Svetlana Boym’s *Common Places*, published in 1994, gives the classic description). But Freidenberg, after years of bureaucratic struggle in the late 1920s, unexpectedly prevailed. The apartment above was communalised, but Freidenberg and her mother kept two rooms of their family apartment, with their own kitchen, bathroom and lavatory, as a separate unit.

During the siege of Leningrad, Freidenberg’s challenge was to keep her Category 1 ‘worker’s’ ration after leaving the employment that qualified her for it; she managed this, though it was an exhausting bureaucratic and legal struggle. After the war, her battle, also successfully waged, was to get back the job she had lost in 1942; on retirement, it was to resist efforts to lower her pension as a result of interruption of service during the war. All these problems stemmed from the same cause: the fact that Freidenberg had not evacuated with her university in 1942. This is a topic on which Paperno gives little information, but the story can be traced from Freidenberg’s diary entries and her letters to Pasternak, himself in evacuation, in the Mossman volume.

The senior scientific workers of Leningrad University were eligible for evacuation to Saratov at the end of the first winter of the blockade. But when most of her colleagues departed in March, Freidenberg stayed behind. The reason she gave was illness (scurvy, cured after a few months by a friend’s timely procurement of vitamin C), but her letters and diary indicate that she strongly resisted the idea of leaving for a variety of reasons: distrust of the authorities, fear of the unknown, attachment to her city and apartment, concern about the effect of the journey on her 82-year-old mother.

Another chance came up in the summer of 1942, when the Academy of Sciences conducted a supplementary evacuation. This time, Freidenberg embraced it, persuaded now of the advantages of Saratov – where, as she wrote to Pasternak, ‘awaiting us there were a room in a central hotel, special food rations, work, a salary, a normal life.’ She and her mother packed up, sealed their apartment, and on 12 July were driven to the train that was to take them to Lake Ladoga. But there were unexplained hold-ups, and the packed train without toilet facilities failed to set off. Freidenberg’s mother took it all in her stride, but Freidenberg herself couldn’t bear the overcrowding and was appalled to see colleagues defecating in public. In a frenzy she insisted that she was ill and they must disembark immediately and go home. Remarkably, a driver turned up to take them and their luggage back to Leningrad. Their apartment was unsealed and they moved back in, remaining in the city for the duration of the blockade (though Freidenberg’s mother suffered a stroke in November 1943 and died a few months later). As for the stalled train, after another couple of miserable stationary days for the passengers, it finally trundled off, and the colleagues were successfully evacuated to the Volga, where they stayed for the next two years.

It was the experience of the blockade that gave Freidenberg her theory of the Stalinist regime’s intentional ‘everyday terror’. Ironically, however, it wasn’t the Stalinist regime that had forced her to endure a full dose of that terror: it was the result of her own choices about evacuation. If she had followed Big Brother’s orders, she would have spent two of the three terrible winters of the siege in safety in Saratov.
