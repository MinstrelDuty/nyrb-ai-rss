# 正文

In​ 2019, the Corbridge Roman Town museum, just south of Hadrian’s Wall, had to downsize. Its new storage facility was smaller than the old one, and the archaeological finds in its collection of more than fifty thousand stone fragments, ceramics, weapons and grave goods couldn’t all fit inside. The curators assessed the collection and identified around a hundred stones that were ‘currently valueless’. They decided to rebury them close to the site where they had been excavated. A grass-covered mound marks the spot.

Reburying is a form of ‘deaccessioning and disposal’ – the combination of terms used by museums for the legal uncoupling and physical removal of an object from a collection. It is particularly appealing because it is reversible: if a future generation has a different opinion of an object’s value, it can be retrieved. But reburying is only an option for archaeological collections. For other types of objects in museums, including artworks and specimens, the main options are destroying them, selling them and giving them away (or back). None is ideal, but disposal is necessary as collections grow and the space and money required to maintain objects that aren’t exhibited, examined or otherwise found interesting becomes harder to justify. In *Deaccessioning Museum Objects* (Routledge, £24.99), Jennifer Durrant likens the process to pruning a tree: just as cutting back its branches allows it to grow better, deaccessioning leaves room for stronger new acquisitions. But it is a legally, ethically and even emotionally fraught undertaking, seemingly at odds with the museum’s basic tenets of preservation and stewardship, as well as the sense of perpetuity that its care is supposed to confer on objects.

As long as there have been museums, they have had too much stuff. This is partly because many of them were formed through the donation of private collections en masse. For all the riches these collections offered, there was also a lot of dross. In ‘The Use and Abuse of Museums’ (1883), the economist William Stanley Jevons complained about the number of objects on display in the British Museum and the South Kensington Museum (as the V&A was then known), as well as in local museums, where ‘any awkward article which a person of the neighbourhood wanted to get rid of is handed over to the museum and duly stuck up, labelled with the name of its donor’. The following decades saw a cooling of this eclectic, indiscriminate approach to acquisition, along with a new emphasis on quality over quantity. However, the ‘squirrel instinct’ returned in the mid-20th century following the losses of the two world wars and an intensified concern for preservation. This was particularly evident in folk and ethnographic collections, where curators rushed to capture artefacts representing ways of life they feared were disappearing. In 1947, Portsmouth City Museum reported that it had acquired a collection of more than seven hundred ‘domestic utensils and appliances used in the English House’. Those seven hundred items are now at the top of Portsmouth City Museum’s deaccessioning list; many of them are mass-produced, too commonplace for a museum collection.

Until relatively recently, there were no laws and few guidelines governing disposal (beyond the conditions attached by donors, who might stipulate that their gifts remain in the collection, or even on display, in perpetuity). When the Museums Association was founded in 1889, its first aim was to find a ‘means of interchange of duplicate and surplus specimens’ – a sign that although overcrowding was already a problem, redistribution was the preferred remedy. It wasn’t until 1977 that the association published an ethical code stating that a museum’s function was to preserve its collections for posterity and that disposal should be a last resort. Little guidance was offered on the way this last resort might be exercised. Various Acts of Parliament from around the same period, which gave major museums national status, were also vague with regard to deaccessioning. Even the British Museum Act of 1963, often cited to justify institutional resistance to restitution (a subject Durrant does not address), hedges its bets, prohibiting the removal of an object from the collection except when it is ‘unfit to be retained’.

What constitutes unfitness is open to interpretation. The Museums Association’s ethical guidelines for 2025, which describe deaccession as ‘necessary’ and ‘urgent’, offer several grounds for considering it: an object may be damaged beyond repair; a duplicate; too resource-intensive to maintain; of low research value; outside the collection’s scope; or the subject of a restitution claim. The more of these conditions that are met, the easier it is to make the case for an object’s deaccession and disposal. Yet none of these categories is straightforward. ‘Damaged beyond repair’ can be a useful description for something you would rather get rid of anyway: a mouldering stuffed giraffe, say, or a railway carriage filled with asbestos. Damage of some sort is par for the course, and if there is a desire to keep an artefact, conservators can almost always make it stable enough to preserve, if not exhibit. As for duplicates, many similar archaeological stones or sherds might seem expendable, but this is not the case for other types of collection. In a natural history museum, a drawer of ladybird specimens is a drawer not of duplicates, but of individuals.

The argument that an object stuck in store for decades is a good candidate for deaccession is also a blunt instrument. Some objects may have considerable research potential without being suitable for display: photographs and drawings, for instance, which researchers can request to see, or a painting with a badly cracked surface but intriguing iconography. When it comes to deaccessioning for lack of research interest, there are cautionary tales. In 1949, the V&A sold at auction some 18th-century giltwood chairs of unknown provenance. It was discovered shortly afterwards that they had belonged to a set commissioned by the Venetian doge Paolo Renier; by then the chairs had been bought by the king of Libya and broken up to make mirror frames and stools. Finally, disposing of an object on the premise that it is outside the collection’s scope is also risky, as the remit of a museum can change. In the 1950s, Birmingham Museum and Art Gallery sold its impressive archive of South Asian metalwork because it did not fit the museum’s vision of being a collection of British art. Fifty years later, the museum described this as an ‘act of irrevocable rashness’, noting that the objects would have acquired new relevance as the South Asian communities of modern Birmingham grew.

The decision to deaccession largely comes down to curators’ expertise and judgment. This can feel dauntingly subjective or even taboo, a dereliction of curatorial care. The term ‘disposal’ does little to dispel that feeling, and in 2018 the Arts and Humanities Research Council suggested that it be replaced by the term ‘degrowing’. Curators fear they may fall prey to personal, institutional or even generational biases in what they choose to deaccession, and will be condemned for their lack of foresight (as happened in Birmingham). It is certainly wise to understand an object as fully as possible before getting rid of it, but waiting just in case its value or purpose reveals itself can lead to paralysis. Durrant interviews curators who talk about the ‘pain of parting’ even with unwanted objects. In the early 2000s, Nick Poole, then CEO of the Collections Trust (a museum management charity which grew out of the Museums Association), called for a ‘crap amnesty’, arguing that a sense of communal consent might make curators feel less guilty about getting rid of things. In the same spirit, the National Museum Directors’ Council has published deaccessioning success stories: in 2001, the National Maritime Museum disposed of its early 20th-century paddle tug *Reliant*, keeping one of its engines, donating another to a different museum and recycling the rest for scrap, saving 868 square metres of storage space.

Durrant encourages curators to separate the motive for disposal from the method. This matters particularly when the method is selling: the decision to get rid of an object should not be made in order to raise money, but on ethically defensible and intellectually rigorous principles. To treat a museum collection as a cash reserve is short-termist at best. It also goes against the statutory obligation of national museums to act in the public interest (often they hold their collections in public trust rather than owning them outright). Local museums may not be bound by the same legal obligations, but they are still expected to follow professional guidelines. At the very least, when an object is sold, other accredited museums should be given first refusal, in order that it might stay in the public domain. This is different from the situation in the US, where many collections have traditionally been treated as assets, and the pandemic seems to have further loosened the restrictions on trade. (In countries such as France, Italy and Greece, the question of selling is largely moot, because deaccessioning of any kind is heavily restricted.)

In 2013, Croydon Council sold some of its collection of antique Chinese ceramics, including Ming Dynasty bowls. It put its highest-value pieces up for auction with Christie’s while retaining less valuable objects for its museum. The decision was unilateral, despite warnings that it fell short of ethical standards and could result in exclusion from professional museum bodies and ineligibility for National Lottery funding. The council said that it could no longer afford the level of security needed to keep such high-value objects safe. Although this is a recognised reason for deaccessioning, it is frustrating that a local authority’s inadequate infrastructure should force it to sacrifice part of its cultural offering. The £8 million raised by the sale was to be used to refurbish a local theatre and music venue, while the objects themselves vanished into private hands.

Legal limitations and sanctions by professional bodies are only part of the deterrent. Selling museum objects almost always provokes public outrage. Croydon’s plan to sell the Chinese ceramics led to a petition and angry articles under headlines such as ‘They’re flogging our cultural gems.’ For curators, the fear of public condemnation may be a more powerful deterrent than any formal penalty. Durrant advocates transparency: demystifying the process and showing not only what a museum is doing but the way it arrived at its decision. Hastings Museum and Art Gallery recently put on display a stool used by a council officer at the coronation of George VI that was coming up for disposal – a sort of ritual of farewell. Other museums have invited the public to participate more directly with displays such as ‘Curator v. Chaos’ and ‘Chuck or Cherish’, in which visitors are asked to vote on whether an object should be deaccessioned.

Museums can still aspire to permanence, but their collections have to evolve. Acquisitions are the sexier part of museum work, but deaccessioning may be the more consequential one, and perhaps also a more meaningful legacy for a curator or director. It is what makes a collection sustainable. Ensuring that objects stay in the public domain should be an absolute priority, but there’s no reason why the public domain should stop at national borders. The restitution of objects where there are legitimate, documented claims from other countries or communities (and a commitment to keeping them in public collections) seems like a good place to start. Given all the checks in place, this is – contrary to the frequently cited argument – hardly a slippery slope towards giving back everything that originated elsewhere. And alongside the argument that an object belongs, morally or historically, in its country or community of origin, there is also a less obvious argument for restitution: it can be good for the institution which is giving back the object. It frees space and resources to better look after other objects, or to acquire something else legitimately.
