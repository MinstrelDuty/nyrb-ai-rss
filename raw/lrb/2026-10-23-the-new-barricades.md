# 正文

O​n the evening​ of 16 October, Emmanuel and Brigitte Macron were filmed from the stalls as they danced in a private box at a Céline Dion concert in Nanterre. Macron appeared a little guarded, but Brigitte was animated, clapping and waving her arms, her head bobbing up and down. ‘The dates match up,’ read the caption of a social media post that went viral the following evening: Marie Antoinette was guillotined on 16 October 1793. Despite three weeks of blockades and protests that had already led to more than six thousand arrests (most of them of minors), Macron claimed to have been surprised by the scale of the protests on 17 October. His address to the nation the following evening focused on ‘the unbridled violence in our towns and cities’: ‘Looking at these images, we’ve all asked ourselves, what on earth are they thinking? How can French people do this to France?’ By this time another video of Brigitte was circulating online, showing her singing along to Dion’s ‘Dans un autre monde’.

The call to mobilisation began to spread at the start of September: first on TikTok, then across social media platforms via AI-generated graphics announcing ‘le retour des gilets jaunes’. Rising fuel prices were one of the triggers for the mass movement of 2018, which was largely made up of rural and suburban workers. The source of this year’s call to action hasn’t been identified and can’t be traced back to any one group, but by mid-September leaders of left-wing parties had started to relay the October date to their members. Fabien Roussel, secretary of the Parti communiste, called on the French people ‘to mobilise as we did in 2018’. Marine Tondelier, of Les Écologistes, said she would support the demonstration ‘with both hands and both feet’, a promise that now seems somewhat macabre. The date was probably chosen for being a Saturday at the start of the school holidays, but it also has political resonance: on 17 October 1961, Maurice Papon ordered the Police Nationale to attack Algerians protesting in support of the FLN. The number killed is still disputed: the state has acknowledged only forty deaths, while historians estimate around two hundred. Some were battered to death, others thrown into the Seine. This time, graffiti across Paris read: ‘Nous sommes les descendants des Algériens que vous n’avez pas noyés.’

These descendants are part of a national movement of lycéens, the scale of which has not been seen since May 1968. Lycées, which cover the last three years of education, leading up to the baccalauréat, are large institutions, often with more than two thousand pupils. Their decline is the result of several decades of under-investment, exacerbated since the 1980s by regional inequalities, the result of handing over school management to local authorities. Contrary to the reporting of most metropolitan media outlets, the student protests began in the overseas département of Mayotte, where, at the end of August, a group of mothers, supported by unions and a student delegation, began to block access to the Lycée du Nord to protest – among other things – the explosion of class sizes and the lack of a canteen. One mother criticised the length of the school day: her child had to leave home at 5 a.m. to get to school on time, she told journalists, only returning at 6 p.m. The movement spread to French Guiana at the beginning of September, when lycéens presented a headteacher with a seventeen-point list of complaints, including staff shortages, absent teachers and cancelled lessons. In mainland France, the cause was taken up at the Lycée Antoine de Saint-Exupéry in the Paris banlieue of Créteil. After teachers went on strike, protesting abysmal staffing levels and the lack of a student nurse or social worker, pupils blockaded the building. The news spread, and others joined the protest, including two local MPs from Jean-Luc Mélenchon’s La France Insoumise (LFI).

In other lycées, the most neglected of which are in the Paris suburbs, students have reported rats, cockroaches, leaking roofs, broken windows, no heating in winter, unusable toilets and missing toilet doors. In Bagnolet, a banlieue just outside the périphérique, students at Collège Georges Politzer said that despite their complaints about the dirty water issuing from their taps, it took months for evidence of faecal matter to be confirmed. At the height of the protests, more than six hundred lycées were blockaded across France. Lycée Paul Éluard has been the most prominent, in part because the tower blocks of Saint-Denis – in the heart of Seine-Saint-Denis, the poorest département in metropolitan France – are central to the national and international imaginary of the banlieues.

When Greta Thunberg joined the Paul Éluard protesters on 13 October, the anchors of CNews – the most popular (and most right-wing) 24-hour news channel – questioned the presence of ‘la p’tite Greta’. They fixated on her keffiyeh, claiming that the protesters were ‘confusing their causes’. Thunberg spoke in general terms about police violence and the state’s neglect of young people, though she could have linked the protests to her climate activism by pointing out that students across France sat their baccalauréat exams in June in 40°C heat. By the time refurbishments began at Lycée Paul Éluard this summer, three years after the collapse of a suspended ceiling and classroom floods, most classes had been moved to prefabs where windows open only a few centimetres and temperatures were in the thirties by 9 a.m. in September. Climate inequality has been largely ignored, if not mocked, by the French establishment; in June, the popular television presenter Yann Barthès joked from his air-conditioned studio about those living *sous les toits*: ‘We’re all in the same boat. It’s very hot. Whether you live in an attic flat in Paris … or in the countryside.’

Education provision varies vastly across France, and the disparity extends to the university application system, Parcoursup, which is widely seen as discriminatory and has left more than 120,000 applicants without a place this year (its reform is one of the students’ main demands). Lycéens from better-resourced rural or private inner-city schools, recognising this, blockaded their buildings in solidarity. Banners were hung outside the prestigious Henri-IV and Louis-le-Grand lycées, with slogans such as: ‘Nos privilèges devraient être des droits’ – ‘Our privileges should be rights.’ Social media has allowed lycéens in the banlieues to discover that their middle-class counterparts listen to the same Black artists as they do when they protest.

One of the more surprising protagonists to emerge from the solidarity movement is Elwan Benoît, a 17-year-old child of the Parisian elite, who, with his florid idiom and curtained hair, looks as if he has been telegraphed from a Truffaut film. ‘The national police force was created under the Vichy regime, and we feel it hasn’t changed since,’ he told the far-right influencer Vincent Lapierre. The right-wing media has deemed him arrogant and threatening. YouTubers set about analysing his body language, in particular, his way of smiling when speaking on a national talk show – his first time in a studio – and pronounced him a grand manipulator. An image of Benoît kissing Mélenchon on the cheek at a protest boosted the far right’s argument that the youth has fallen prey to the *ultragauche*. This is the government’s position too. ‘When are you going to be held to account for inciting pupils to commit acts of violence?’ Laurent Nuñez, the minister of the interior, asked Mélenchon in early October.

Most police violence has been unleashed on the banlieues, but not all. After a bin was set ablaze outside a lycée in the small Normandy town of Alençon, a 14-year-old girl was dragged away in a headlock by a police officer. In Metz, another teenage girl was filmed while restrained on the ground. An officer took the opportunity to stroke her hair and breasts. In Tours, a teenage boy lost an eye after being hit by a rubber bullet. In Lens, a boy lost his left hand and part of his right after police set off a stun grenade. The case against a policeman who shoved a baton between the buttocks of a student of colour is one of 36 being investigated so far. Sabrine Kharbach, a student organiser, reported a police officer telling her that ‘we’re not dealing with kids here. These days, lycéens are big; in the banlieue, they’re 1.85 metres tall and weigh 100 kilos.’ Another organiser, Inès Nesrine Boutaybi, was told in a meeting with the regional council that she and her peers were ‘spokespeople for Hamas’. Some left-wing officials have tried to intervene. A video of the LFI mayor of Saint-Denis, Bally Bagayoko, placing himself between the young protesters and the police has gone viral. After his election in March, Bagayoko ordered a stop to the use of rubber bullets by municipal officers, but he has no such control over the national police. A guest on CNews said he was acting like ‘the divine king of the village’ and that he needed to be told that ‘we’re not living in a theocracy.’

‘Bismillah, c’est les ténèbres’ has become the central slogan of the protests. It was extracted from a Snapchat message which lycéens say was written by a police officer trying to imitate ‘le langage des djeuns’ to infiltrate a group of student organisers. If Macron manages to push through his social media ban, which was blocked by France’s highest court in August, under-fifteens will lose access to the TikTok and Instagram accounts of the Police Nationale, whose content mills have been working overtime during the last few weeks, publishing choice angles and cuts of violence against the police as well as helpful ‘informational’ videos: ‘If violence breaks out, the right thing to do is to keep your distance and follow the instructions of the police, who are not your enemies.’

The official Paris route for the 17 October march began on place de la République. It was meant to start at 2 p.m., but by 11.30 police had already started using tear gas. The concentration of officers at the start of the route made it difficult for protesters to proceed, and word spread to the main railway stations that it wasn’t worth heading east. Spontaneous demonstrations started to break out elsewhere. Saint-Lazare, Opéra and Madeleine – wealthy areas popular with tourists – were soon overwhelmed by protesters and, when police attempted to move them on, they quickly spilled over onto the Champs-Élysées. When the police in République ran out of tear gas early in the afternoon, they began throwing simulation grenades.

These were the first national demonstrations in which many lycéens had taken part, but they knew what to expect, having grown up with images of protests and police violence. They see themselves as resurrecting the barricades of 1968, and reclaim ‘La Marseillaise’ when they march. If one of the police strategies was to make an example of the protesters to deter others, it didn’t work. Lycéens spoke of having ‘nothing to lose’. The final straw for many was the RIPOST legislation (‘Réponses Immédiates aux Phénomènes troublant l’Ordre public, la Sécurité et la Tranquillité’), pushed through this summer at the height of the heatwaves, which expands police surveillance powers and restricts public gatherings. It was passed by a coalition of Macronist and far-right MPs and was immediately used by the police to justify puncturing inflatable paddling pools and extinguishing barbecues at the foot of banlieue tower blocks.

Fourteen thousand police were deployed in Paris for Pope Leo XIV’s visit at the end of September; on 17 October, it was reported that only ten thousand had been deployed across the whole of France, half of them in Paris, though the government has disputed this figure. Had the authorities underestimated the numbers of protesters? According to the CGT union, 800,000 people took to the streets nationwide, while Nuñez put the figure at 550,000. Perhaps three weeks of lycéen repression had depleted police numbers (when a call for voluntary back-up was issued at 5 p.m., few officers responded). Or perhaps Nuñez wanted to show the public the inevitable result of thousands of frustrated young people – many of whom have grown up with only the necessities of life and can’t get jobs – taking to the streets. We know the result: the pillaging and looting of hundreds of shops, public and private property graffitied and destroyed. Still, the police made choices regarding what was safeguarded. In Amiens, only the confectioners Trogneux, Brigitte Macron’s family’s business, received protection. In Paris, near République, dozens of small businesses – many run by immigrants – were ransacked and burned.

Were it not for the level of destruction, it’s unlikely the nation would have heard from Macron, who had left it to Nuñez to repeat that no restraint would be exercised towards the lycéens, even after the multiple cases of mutilation. When he finally made his speech on 18 October, Macron chose to focus on the gilet jaune aspect of the demonstrations, assuring the public that he was headed to the Gulf to sort out the petrol issue. He laid the blame at the feet of the *ultragauche* (read: LFI), *casseurs* (read: youth) and *délinquants* (read: Blacks and Arabs). He used the word ‘violence’ to talk about the plundered tourist office in Lyon, but there were no words about the reported deaths of two protesters in Paris. He condescended to the lycéens’ ‘inquiétude’ and ignored their concrete demands.

It is unusual to see this level of unrest so close to elections. Very few of these young people – not even Benoît, who already sounds like a party leader – will be able to vote. It remains to be seen whether the protests are good news for Mélenchon and the LFI. Disappointing much of his base, Mélenchon, who has always advocated for peaceful protest and revolution ‘at the ballot box’, tweeted: ‘Those who share our cause have no place in looting and vandalism.’ On 19 October, Mediapart reported that police intelligence officials had been told to blame the LFI for the unrest, even though the head of one of the police unions reported that 80 per cent of looters had no clear political affiliation.

A few days before the 17 October protests, I attended a screening of Claire Simon’s *Écrire la vie*, a documentary about lycéens’ experience of reading Annie Ernaux (Ernaux is an LFI supporter; at the launch of Mélenchon’s presidential campaign in Saint-Denis in June she read Éluard’s ‘Sans âge’, a poem about the renewal that comes with militancy). Despite the news, I neglected to assess the state of the dozen or so lycées that appear in the film because I was transfixed by the lycéens’ discussions, as they grappled with and disagreed over questions of class and gender. The film gives the lie to the state’s characterisation of young people as *casseurs* who just want to skip school. Lycée Félix Eboué in French Guiana, blockaded since early September, features in the documentary. A teacher says to his students: ‘We saw in Annie Ernaux’s Nobel Prize speech … that her vocation as a writer isn’t to make up stories or characters. Remember: I will write to …’ and the whole class responds in union, ‘venger ma race’. Another national day of protest has been called for 24 October, and Nuñez has said that this time he will send in the army. Curfews are in place across several cities, and helicopters survey the capital at night. The justice system can move fast when it wants to: a man has just been sentenced to six months in prison after taking what turned out to be baby formula from a looted pharmacy, and images have shown young protesters packed into tiny, dirty cells, waiting judgment for the events of 17 October. But I doubt the lycéens will be cowed.

*23 October*
