# 正文

Kate Wilson​ met Mark Stone at a meeting of a group known as NASA – the Nottingham Assembly of Subversive Activists – held at a community hub and café called the Sumac Centre in November 2003. He told her he grew up near where she had in London. He asked lots of questions. He was an excellent listener. His taste in music and political ideals were similar to hers. He had a brother in America and an elderly mother in Ireland, a sob story (absent father) and a shady past (smuggling drugs). But in fact he was an undercover officer with the National Public Order Intelligence Unit (NPOIU). A few days later, after Wilson mentioned to Stone that she needed to get to London, he contacted his handler, EN31, who recorded: ‘Source has indicated that Source may be able to give her a lift. I subsequently agreed this course with Source, as it will give Source 2-3 hours alone with Katja and endear Source to her.’ Soon the pair had begun a relationship. Stone moved into a shared house with Wilson and her friends. He met her parents, and stayed with her at their home in London on at least nineteen occasions. He gave her a second-hand bike. He sold her his old van. He went with her family to Ikea to buy furniture for her brother. He visited her in Barcelona. He went to her grandma’s ninetieth birthday party.

Stone, like Wilson, was a dedicated activist. He cracked squats, wrote statements, shaped projects and organised actions. He convinced Wilson to travel with him to Dublin in 2004 for a series of protests at an EU summit. As Wilson had feared, the Bring the Noise demonstration descended into violence after some of the marchers refused to be corralled by riot police equipped with water cannon. Stone was hurt and they were both arrested. Wilson was strip-searched three times before they were mysteriously released without charge. She was badly shaken; Stone just seemed angry, insisting that he was going to sue the Gardaí. That Christmas, he told Wilson he was going kickboxing in Thailand. After a tsunami struck on Boxing Day, Wilson feared he’d been killed. When she met him off the plane he was distressed, and talked about digging a child’s body out of the devastation. It seems likely that he never went to Thailand, but was driven to the airport by his handler to fake his return.

Stone lied to his targets, and also seems to have found it hard to be entirely honest with his handlers when his masculine pride was at stake. Many of the people in Wilson’s circle believed in open relationships based on trust and communication, and most practised what they preached. At first, she and Stone were so ‘involved with each other’, as she writes in her account of what happened, that there was no question of either of them seeing anyone else. But when Wilson met another man she liked, Owen, at an Earth First! camp in the late summer of 2004, Stone seemed thrown. ‘Where I come from, a guy will punch you for looking at his pint, let alone his girl,’ he told her. They talked things through. A few days later, he told his handler that another woman on the activist scene ‘really fancies Source and this has upset her boyfriend’. This was an unusual comment: there are vanishingly few references in the log books to intimate relations involving undercover officers, even though Stone reportedly had sex with at least eleven women while undercover. Who was the ‘upset boyfriend’? Wilson arranged for Stone and Owen to meet, but Stone kept all mention of Owen out of his reports until long after he and Wilson had broken up. Soon after Wilson met Owen, Stone got together with a friend of hers, ‘Lisa’ (she has preserved her anonymity), on a camping trip to the Lake District, suddenly proposing to his handler that he move in with her. Wilson felt that she and Stone were drifting apart; early in 2005 she left the UK, and they ended things. The relationship had lasted 475 days. Stone said goodbye to Wilson, debriefed with his handler and moved straight in with Lisa, who lived at Cornerstone, a housing co-op in Leeds. They were together for six years. He was in the mourners’ car with her family at her father’s funeral.

Stone kept in touch with Wilson and all three continued their activism. In 2009, Wilson was worried that his mental health was deteriorating. Faking a breakdown was a typical exit strategy used by undercover officers, and indeed he resigned from the police in March 2010. But he still attended activist events and continued his relationship with Lisa. Apparently, he had reinvented himself as a corporate spy. However, he had probably been made to hand in his fake documents and, while he and Lisa were in Italy that summer, she found a passport under the name Mark Kennedy and a phone with messages addressed to ‘dad’. She enlisted friends to do some digging: they found a marriage certificate and his children’s birth certificates, all of them listing Kennedy’s occupation as ‘police officer’. Lisa and her friends confronted him and he confessed.

As Wilson says, ‘I think people just assume there must have been *some* serious crime *somewhere*; because otherwise, what were the police doing there?’ But undercover police, we now know, spied on a wide range of left-wing and community groups from 1968 onwards, including the Labour Party, Greenpeace, Schoolkids against the Nazis and Uist Hedgehog Rescue. The activists Wilson worked with fought for environmental causes and against globalisation; they opposed war, challenged multinational corporations and supported social justice. They were anarchists, anti-capitalists and vegans. They formed groups like the Clown Army – who came to demonstrations in full costume, with red noses and squirty flowers – and the W.O.M.B.L.E.S (White Overalls Movement Building Libertarian Effective Struggles), who dressed in padded white overalls on protests to face down police violence. They participated in demonstrations and direct actions, campaigned for local post offices, set up squats and housing co-ops, and sold ‘samosas for social change’ at 50p a pop. They mounted what EN31 called an ‘aquatic attack’ on a military vessel coming to London for an arms fair: a dozen or so people rode inflatable dragons around the Royal Albert Dock, most of them using their hands as paddles; some were briefly arrested under a by-law banning swimming.

Wilson’s book alternates between the story of what happened after Kennedy was unmasked, told in the first person, and what came before, told in the third person and using the name Katja for herself. Only some way into the book does she mention that this was what her close friends called her. The effect is to disorient the reader slightly, just as the activists were thrown into confusion by the discovery of Kennedy’s identity. Almost immediately, Wilson experienced ‘a growing discomfort around people; an almost unconscious avoidance of the places and situations that reminded me of him; a festering anger; and a sensitivity I had never felt before’. She began having panic attacks. She couldn’t sleep through the night, a ‘mass of questions boiling’ in her mind. She lost her place at medical school, unable to study. Her relationship with her boyfriend disintegrated. Betrayal, she writes, ‘destroys your ability to trust’, but it took many years for her to realise that her refusal to make the same mistake again had made it impossible for her to ‘have community’. The effects on other women in her network who had also been deceived into relationships were similarly devastating. Some, as a result of the timing of these relationships and the destruction of their ability to trust, ‘lost the option to have biological children’. Police infiltration had a chilling effect on the activists’ political movements too. ‘All the old centre users stopped coming in here after that cop was exposed,’ Wilson was told when she went back to the Sumac Centre. Many campaign groups collapsed.

Wilson joined a group with seven other women, including Lisa, who brought a claim against the police for infringing their human rights and sued for civil damages based on the torts of deceit, misfeasance in public office, negligence and assault. Between them, they had had relationships with five different officers, lasting a total of around thirty years. One of the women wasn’t even an activist when she met an undercover officer at a party; soon he had moved in with her, apparently to build his cover. She worked in accounting.

Most of the women were forced to settle for compensation and an apology in the civil case because the justice system didn’t rate what they’d lost very highly: even if they had gone on to win, they might have been made to pay the police’s legal costs, which would have bankrupted them. Only Wilson, who had lost a highly valued career as a doctor, was able to fight on. The police had a cunning plan: they accepted all her accusations in order to avoid a trial – for which they would have to disclose evidence and put up witnesses – but contested the value of her claim. This meant that *she* was effectively put on trial: ‘Lawyers, psychologists, psychiatrists and employment specialists … were picking over my failures and struggles with mental health to determine whether they were my own fault or the result of police abuse.’ Victory left a bad taste. Meanwhile, the police had successfully lobbied to get her human rights claims sent to the secretive Investigatory Powers Tribunal (IPT), which claims to provide ‘the right of redress to anyone who believes they have been the victim of unlawful action by a public authority using covert investigative techniques’. In the ruling dispatching the case there, the judges stated ‘there is no doubt’ that in setting up the Tribunal, ‘Parliament intended to override fundamental human rights.’

Early on, one of Wilson’s lawyers described the police lawyers’ approach as ‘madder and madder’. Another, years later, told Wilson: ‘Honestly, I have never come across *incompetence* as a litigation strategy.’ Defences were submitted and withdrawn. The police claimed that the officers in question were bad apples, then tried to imply that the sexual relationships were in fact ‘lawful for all purposes’. They claimed that the women were asking to be deceived because they’d entered into open relationships. They changed their legal team and asked for more time. They missed deadlines. Their lawyers admitted that Wilson’s sexual relationship with Kennedy had violated her human rights and said that, given this admission, the tribunal shouldn’t bother hearing her larger case. They argued that the tribunal should make a ruling ‘based on hypothetical “assumed facts”’ and that disclosing police files risked harming undercover officers and operations. They said that instead of disclosing files, they would make available a sworn witness statement drawn up in line with their legal ‘duty of candour’. This generated one of the most dubious documents in the whole saga. ‘I bet they wheel out some doddery old cop, on the verge of retirement, maybe with a terminal disease or something,’ an activist in the Undercover Research Group predicted.[\*](/the-paper/v48/n21/footnotes/madder-and-madder-footnote-1) ‘Here, say this under oath, and then get a pat on the back and a golden handshake and go off to play golf.’ In fact, Sir Stephen House had resigned, mired in scandal, as chief constable of Police Scotland in 2015; in January 2019 he was given the job of deputy commissioner of the Metropolitan Police just in time to sign a witness statement about events he hadn’t witnessed. He had never met Kennedy, and none of the other officers involved agreed to speak to him. Several years later, a police barrister accepted that no weight could be attached to House’s opinions, and asked the IPT to ‘disregard them’.

In 2019, the police finally began to disclose substantial numbers of files to Wilson. By this time, her funding had run out and she was representing herself. The files were disorganised, context-free and heavily redacted. She wondered whether the police had decided that, since they couldn’t halt the disclosures, they would release so much material she would be unable to make sense of it. Luckily, she had friends and family willing to sort, index and analyse. (Eventually she got a new pro bono legal team.) As her knowledge of the scope of the undercover operations expanded, so did her case. Back in 2011, she had argued that her relationship with Kennedy infringed her right to private and family life, as well as her right to freedom from inhuman and degrading treatment. Later, after realising that so many undercover officers had deceived so many women into sexual relationships, she added the argument that systemic sex discrimination in the police force had compromised her right to enjoy all her other rights. As the files revealed just how many undercover officers had insinuated themselves into her activist circles, she claimed that this ‘level of interference’ had breached her political rights – the rights to freedom of expression and freedom of assembly and association. In the end, she came to believe there could be no lawful basis for these operations at all. Her case was now about much more than her sexual relationship with one officer: it was about the practices, the scale and the justification of the whole undercover operation.

After he was unmasked, Kennedy appointed Max Clifford as his publicist and gave an interview to the *Daily Mail* in which he claimed there was ‘no way’ his cover officer ‘could not have known about a relationship … I didn’t have to spell it out to him. I couldn’t go to the loo without him knowing.’ The police concurred. EN31, Kennedy’s principal handler for most of his deployment, had ‘stated positively’ he ‘did not know that MK was having a sexual relationship’ with Wilson. But even Stephen House admitted to the IPT that ‘such denials are not automatically conclusive of the question of knowledge’ (in other words, EN31 lied).

It’s true that the sexual activities of undercover officers were almost never mentioned in the log books. But the logs were clearly dodgy. A crucial volume from the early months of Wilson’s relationship with Kennedy went missing; a copy turned up in the possession of EN31 after the IPT case had concluded. When Kennedy visited Wilson in Barcelona in 2004 the files recorded him as ‘OFF’, a term not used anywhere else (‘annual leave’ and ‘rest days’ were the terms usually recorded); no authorisation for him to travel abroad as an undercover operative could be found. The following year, he was at the eco-camp for activists protesting at the G8 summit in Edinburgh along with his ex-girlfriend (Wilson), his current partner (Lisa), another woman with whom he was openly in a sexual relationship and at least five other undercover officers. How could those officers have failed to know? One of them, whose alias was Lynn Watson, was friends with Lisa, who testified that Watson had asked about her relationship with Kennedy and said they seemed ‘sweet’ together.

Watson appears never to have mentioned Kennedy’s relationships with any of these women in the logs, though she did find time to report a one-night stand of Wilson’s she heard about ‘on the grapevine’. EN59, the senior investigating officer during much of Wilson’s relationship with Kennedy, later claimed he ‘had no knowledge of the sexual relationship’, and thought Wilson was just a member of the group, ‘not a specific person of interest and consequently I did not seek to glean a greater understanding of the individual in any material manner’. EN59 also signed off on the purchase of the bike Kennedy gave Wilson and was briefed every few hours when they were arrested together in Dublin. Very occasionally, someone slipped up: in 2005, a decision log recorded ‘permission’ for Kennedy to let a woman live with him for two weeks, noting that an officer ‘verbally reminded UCO [undercover officer] re Health and Safety – intimate relations etc’. But in the end the silence spoke loudest. It wasn’t a question of which officers knew: they all knew, and they all conspired to make sure it wasn’t written down.

Under the National Intelligence Model, intelligence-gathering is supposed to meet standards of necessity, proportionality and lawfulness. Yet the very first application for authorisation for the operation that targeted Wilson didn’t meet these criteria; it referred to ‘persons involved in extremism’ who used the Sumac Centre, but provided no definition of extremism and nothing concrete linking illegality to the centre. Apparently, extremist politics could mean participation in the peace movement or opposition to ‘extreme right-wing politics’. The application referred to ‘several serious assaults’ that had occurred during direct actions against foxhunting, without making clear these had been committed against hunt saboteurs, not by them. Later authorisations repeated and referred back to this insubstantial ‘intelligence case’. Some were baffling: one from May 2005 named Wilson as a subject of the operation, describing her as a ‘main’ organiser of the Cornerstone housing co-op, even though (as Kennedy well knew) Wilson had never been an organiser there and was living in Barcelona. External reports on the NPOIU repeatedly noted that its operations lacked clear goals, making necessity, proportionality or success impossible to measure. Sometimes Kennedy’s activities were counterproductive: dozens of convictions were overturned and trials collapsed because of his involvement – often he had taken a leading role in instigating and carrying out actions.

A decade after Wilson joined the legal case against the police, she won a resounding victory, with the IPT finding that the NPOIU operated a policy of ‘don’t ask, don’t tell’ regarding sexual relationships between undercover officers and targets; that the operation which targeted Wilson involved multiple human rights violations, including sex discrimination; and that its authorisation was ‘fatally flawed’ – it ‘could not be justified as “necessary in a democratic society”’. By this point, a judge-led inquiry into undercover policing since 1968 was well underway. It was announced in 2015 by the then home secretary, Theresa May, in the wake of a pile-up of horrific revelations about undercover operations: officers lying in court; stealing the identities of dead children; attempting to discredit the parents of Stephen Lawrence, the teenager murdered in a racist gang attack in 1993; fathering children with women they deceived into relationships and then abandoned. It was supposed to take three years; more than ten years later, it’s still going, and has so far cost more than £140 million.

When Wilson first spoke out she used the pseudonym Lily, but in 2015 she decided to reveal her real name. Many of the police officers involved do not want their names revealed even though, as participants in the inquiry, they have been given protection from prosecution and it is well established that there is no threat of reprisals from the activists they targeted (risk assessments during Kennedy’s deployment concluded that ‘these persons do not show any propensity for violence when a suspected source is identified’). In 2018, the chair, Sir John Mitting, said he was minded not to grant EN31 anonymity, but last year he changed his mind based on a doctor’s report that outlined the ‘impact which the threat of disclosure of the real name of EN31 is having on the mental health and well-being of EN31’. Wilson and many other victims protested, arguing that the principle of open justice should weigh heavily in this case, and that full transparency is vital for victims suffering from PTSD: knowing the truth is the foundation for processing what happened. Wilson acknowledged that the threat of disclosure must have caused EN31 some ‘anguish’, but pointed out that the inquiry had left this threat hanging, when it could have published EN31’s name years earlier. ‘I would seek to reassure EN31,’ she wrote, ‘from personal experience, that the fear and anticipation of losing anonymity are far worse than the reality.’ But she insisted that it was ‘impossible to convey how skin-crawlingly creepy it is that … EN31 remains an anonymous figure’. For now, he is still, as Wilson puts it, ‘protected by the state’.

Meanwhile, the powers of the police have been expanded and the right to protest ever more tightly circumscribed by the Police, Crime, Sentencing and Courts Act of 2022, the Public Order Act 2023, the Crime and Policing Act 2026 and by the proscription of Palestine Action, a decision that conflates protest with terrorism. A Human Rights Watch report issued in January this year noted the erosion of rights to ‘freedom of expression and peaceful assembly’, and its cost: ‘silencing dissenting voices, discouraging civic participation, eroding democratic accountability, and ultimately fraying the fabric of democracy itself’.
