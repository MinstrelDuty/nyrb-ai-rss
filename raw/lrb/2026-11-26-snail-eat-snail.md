# 正文

Had​ I been told, growing up in South-East London in the 1970s, that one day I would live in a house with a garden visited by parakeets, and that on a short walk from the door I could see Himalayan balsam and Egyptian geese, I would have assumed that I’d moved to another continent. But in fact the parakeets, balsam and geese all came to me and all three were here, in the UK if not in my neighbourhood, long before I was.

Much of Britain’s flora and fauna, more than two thousand species in total, came from elsewhere: rabbits, hares, grey squirrels, fallow deer, sycamore, sweet chestnut, brown rats, house mice, rainbow trout, slipper limpets, honeybees and others that are harder to spot. In 2023, the Intergovernmental Science-Policy Platform on Biodiversity and Ecosystem Services (IPBES) estimated that humans have taken more than 37,000 wild species to new homes. On average two hundred are moved each year.

Most introductions fail. Plants and animals arrive in small numbers, and must contend with incumbents better adapted to their habitat. Of those that survive, most coexist with indigenous species, but around one in ten are more disruptive. Deliberately introduced predators have caused some of the most notorious conservation failures. Lake Victoria was once home to more than 350 species of fish that could be found nowhere else. In the 1950s, the Nile perch, which can grow to be more than a metre long and weigh two hundred kilos, was introduced to the lake for sport. The perch have since caused the extinction of at least two hundred other species of fish. In another case, the rosy wolfsnail, introduced to Pacific islands including Hawaii with the aim of controlling the population of the previously introduced giant African land snail, has instead eaten more than 130 other snail species to extinction. IPBES reckons that invasions have been implicated in 60 per cent of recorded extinctions. Its 2023 report conservatively estimated the annual cost of dealing with invasive species at $423 billion; the cost increases fourfold every decade. Most of this money is spent dealing with weeds, pests and diseases.

Daniel Simberloff is one of the world’s leading ecologists. He has studied biological invasions since the 1980s, when the discipline of ecology began treating them, in his words, as ‘a single phenomenon of global extent’. In 1999, with his student Betsy von Holle, he came up with the notion of ‘invasional meltdown’, the idea that introduced species can amplify one another’s effects. The canonical example is Christmas Island in the Indian Ocean. Here, yellow crazy ants, accidentally introduced in the first half of the 20th century, fed on the honeydew of another introduced species, the lac scale insect, and killed native red crabs in huge numbers. This led to an increase in the population of yet another introduced species, the giant African land snail, which is eaten by the crabs but not by the ants, which in turn damaged native forest plants. (Since the introduction in the mid-2010s of a Malaysian micro-wasp that selectively kills the lac scale insect by laying eggs inside it, the red crab population has significantly recovered.)

The effort to build a general understanding of what makes species invasive, what makes ecosystems vulnerable to invasion and the best way to manage wild nature in the light of that knowledge was spurred by the growth of the environmental movement, and of conservation science more broadly, over the course of the 20th century. Before that, while the effects of introductions had long been recognised, they were in the main treated as problems of pest control, and therefore as a matter not for ecology so much as for the older disciplines of agriculture, forestry or medicine. In the 17th century, travellers to New England listed the plants that had ‘sprung up since the English planted and kept cattle’. Indigenous Americans knew that when they found honeybees in their woods, the colonists weren’t far behind. In the 19th century, naturalist-travellers such as Charles Darwin and Joseph Hooker noted that European plants had become dominant across large areas of Australia, New Zealand and elsewhere. Pests and diseases of crops and trees, such as the Colorado potato beetle and chestnut blight, were known to have been accidentally shipped across oceans, and it didn’t take long to work out that taking rabbits to Australia had been a bad idea.

Today’s thinking about the response to invasive species acknowledges that in many places conservation is no longer just a matter of keeping out but – as with rewilding or mitigating the effects of climate change – a process that requires active intervention, and with it decisions about what to preserve, restore or let go. This is an instance of what Aldo Leopold, in an essay from 1949, called a ‘land ethic’. ‘An ethic, ecologically,’ he wrote, ‘is a limitation on freedom of action in the struggle for existence.’ It requires that we weigh the competing rights and claims of different groups, and then find the most effective and humane way to act. In this respect it is like politics, and also like gardening; many of the controversies that Simberloff discusses could be seen as disagreements about which comparison is the more apt.

Most conservation scientists and organisations have settled on a set of principles and methods. They take a cautious approach to accidental introductions, preventing them or snuffing them out where possible. Many jurisdictions check imports for stowaways and have lists of invasive species to watch out for – the UK’s includes Asian giant hornets and raccoons – along with procedures for monitoring and responding to arrivals.

Where invaders are already established, decisions are guided by considerations of the harm an invader does to native species and human welfare, as well as practicality. Efforts at eradication are most common on islands, which often have plants and animals that are found nowhere else and have evolved without having to face predators or competitors. Successful campaigns have shown that native species repopulate once invasive species are removed: the culling of feral goats, pigs and donkeys on the Galápagos Islands was followed by a resurgence in native plants, and poisoning the rats and mice on South Georgia cleared the way for ground-nesting birds to return. The most ambitious current purge is New Zealand’s Predator Free 2050, which aims to eradicate rats, feral cats, stoats and possums.

On continents, the aim is often to control rather than eradicate, or to defend a smaller area. South Africa’s Working for Water programme, launched in 1995, successfully employs people in poor rural communities to clear water-hungry invasive plants. Alongside physical (shooting, trapping, uprooting) and chemical methods, the other option is biological control: introducing a disease, parasite or predator, like the Malaysian micro-wasp on Christmas Island, that can check the invader. When it works, this is a cheap, long-term solution, but it can go badly wrong. Seven of the animals on the list of the hundred worst invasive species published by the International Union for Conservation of Nature in 2000 were introduced as biological controls. All are predators with broad diets, including the rosy wolfsnail, the stoat, the cane toad and the New Guinea flatworm, but even specialists sometimes eat things they weren’t meant to. In Australia, cactus moths were introduced in the 1920s and reclaimed millions of hectares from South American prickly pear plants, but decades after being released in the Caribbean, the moths now threaten native prickly pears in Florida.

There are many plausible theories as to why species run amok. Some focus on features of the invader, such as its catholic diet or the rapidity with which it reproduces. Some concern aspects of the invaded ecosystem: the lack of natural enemies, for example, or the effects of human activity. Others are historical, such as how often and in what numbers a species has been introduced. Simberloff argues that the progress and outcomes of an invasion depend on context. An introduced species can be innocuous in one place and invasive in another; it can linger unobtrusively for decades before suddenly becoming a problem; or it may boom and then wane. The response to any particular invasion must therefore be tailored to the situation.

Any effort to restore an environment encounters the difficulty of defining its original state. Neolithic farmers transformed Europe with crops, livestock, pests and weeds brought from the Middle East. When the Lapita people of South-East Asia spread across the Pacific more than three thousand years ago, they took rats and chickens along for food; Simberloff thinks the rats probably wiped out hundreds of island bird species. It is now understood that places which conservationists once saw as pristine wildernesses, particularly in the Americas and Australia, and increasingly in tropical forests, were in fact shaped by thousands of years of hunting, burning, cultivation and harvesting. And even if the ecological blueprints did exist, it isn’t clear that conservation decisions should be based on a species’ origins rather than its ecological effects or value to people.

Nevertheless, origins continue to shape the way naturalists and conservationists feel about wild things. Some see invasions as responsible for an unwanted diversity and a loss of distinctiveness, and their remarks to this effect can smack of xenophobia: Simberloff quotes the fish biologist Walter Courtenay’s description of Florida as ‘a biological cesspool of introduced life’. Some eminent conservationists have also been racists and eugenicists, their notions of purity leading them to conflate the preservation of nature with the control of human migration and reproduction. Simberloff refers to Madison Grant as ‘a titan of early 20th-century conservation’ who worked to expand the US National Park network and was instrumental in saving the American bison from extinction. He was also the author of *The Passing of the Great Race: Or, the Racial Basis of European History*; Hitler wrote to Grant calling the book ‘my bible’. Nazi Germany had a policy of favouring native plants over introduced species. The investor and property developer Charles Goethe, another follower of Grant, drew explicit parallels – still used by the modern far right – between saving American wildlife from invasive species and saving white America from non-Nordic races and genetic pollution. ‘The greatest national gains from a really completed National Park system,’ Goethe wrote, ‘can be expected in the accelerated building of a eugenically better nation. This would include gradual elimination of imbeciles.’ In 1948, the US National Parks Service made him its honorary chief naturalist.

Today’s invasion scientists are not oblivious to this history. The description of introduced species as ‘alien’ or ‘exotic’ is gradually giving way to terms such as ‘non-native’. In 2022, the Entomological Society of America changed the common name of *Lymantria dispar*, a European pest of American trees, from the ‘gypsy moth’ to the ‘spongy moth’. Simberloff doesn’t consider it fair or logical to assume that present-day invasion specialists inherit the attitudes of their forebears, but he does call for greater geographic and linguistic diversity in contemporary invasion science, which remains heavily concentrated in North America, Europe and Australia.

Simberloff’s account suggests that biological nativism is less common than the belief that wildlife is there to be improved. In the 19th century, he writes, ‘no taxon was safe from being hauled halfway across the globe in the name of better hunting, more fishing, better fruits … or simply providing reminders of home.’ Acclimatisation societies were set up for this purpose in Australia, Britain, France and its colonies, New Zealand, Russia and the US. It is to these bodies that we owe the presence of starlings in the Americas and Australia, and little owls and hedgehogs (a second-tier target of Predator Free 2050) in New Zealand. In 1871 the US set up the Commission of Fish and Fisheries with a remit to introduce freshwater species from eastern to western states, sometimes by means of a railway carriage converted into an aquarium. In the 1930s, Eastham Guild, a Boston socialite who had moved to Tahiti, released seven thousand birds belonging to 59 species from five continents. Only a handful survived, but one of them, the silvereye, is now the island’s most common land bird. To play this game, it helps to be rich and own land, but it isn’t essential. Many introduced reptiles and amphibians – Florida has around sixty non-native species, against 150 natives – are there because someone who kept them as pets either got tired of looking after them or thought it would be nice to see them in the wild.

The case for controlling invasive species rests on the judgment that the continued existence of native individuals or populations matters more than that of the animals or plants targeted for control, especially if the natives are in some way unique. To refrain from intervention is to allow suffering – a point Simberloff makes with a photograph from Gough Island in the South Atlantic showing half a dozen house mice eating a chick of MacGillivray’s prion, a critically endangered seabird. But some animal rights activists and biologists consider it indefensible to cause suffering and death to one living thing in the name of conserving another. This is a minority view, but sometimes it carries the day: plans to eradicate grey squirrels in Italy and hippos in Colombia (descended from four animals escaped from Pablo Escobar’s private zoo) have been thwarted by protests and legal challenges.

Invasion biology also has critics within ecology. Some value the increased local biodiversity that results from the benign majority of introductions. Some stress the instances in which introduced species have provided food, shelter and other benefits to people and wildlife. Some see introductions as belonging to a broader category of ecological change. Climate change is causing the widespread movement of living things, such as the little egrets from Southern Europe that used to be only occasional visitors to England but now stay all year round, or the *Aedes aegypti* mosquito, spreader of Dengue and Zika, found breeding in East London this summer. Habitat destruction means that some species will be more secure in their new homes than in their original ones. Those who make such arguments are sometimes accused of denialism. *Ecological Explosions* sets out all these controversies, ultimately endorsing the view that biological invasions remain a specific problem and should be dealt with accordingly.

The movement of living things, even across oceans, is something that has always gone on but, like extinction, it is now happening at a greatly accelerated rate. The Tōhoku tsunami carried nearly three hundred marine species from Japan to the Pacific North-West, mainly via floating plastic and timber, but at any moment several times this number are living on the hulls and in the ballast water of ships. There is little sign of a slowdown, despite the commitment made in 2022 by signatories to the UN Convention on Biological Diversity to halve the rate of introductions of invasive species by 2030. In *The Ecology of Invasions by Animals and Plants* (1958), the British ecologist Charles Elton described the way species migration was reshaping nature at the largest scale, blurring the distinctions between continents. ‘Make no mistake,’ he wrote, ‘we are seeing one of the great historical convulsions in the world’s fauna and flora.’

Different things still live in different places, but a number of cosmopolitan plants and animals – relatively small, but growing – occur wherever the climate will support them. The word ‘Homogocene’ has been used to describe this era of growing conformity; others have used ‘New Pangaea’, evoking the supercontinent that once united all the Earth’s landmasses. Anyone who lives in an urban or farmland setting, which is a large majority of the world’s population, is part of a ‘freakosystem’, a recently assembled and fluid biota drawn from multiple continents.

Simberloff attributes the failure to tackle biological invasions more to a lack of political and social will than to scientific uncertainty. Even so, his book shows that our impact on and responsibility for the state of nature exceeds our understanding or control of it. We don’t yet have conclusive evidence whether or not London’s parakeets are bad for native birds. As an addition to the city’s birdlife, I prefer egrets. But instead of regarding either of them as ornaments, pollutants or the feathered equivalents of human invaders or garden weeds, I would like a way to think about them that recognises their rights while situating them within a larger frame of wild flourishing. Conflicts are unavoidable. Leopold hoped a land ethic would provide ‘a mode of guidance for meeting ecological situations so new or intricate, or involving such deferred reactions, that the path of social expediency is not discernible to the average individual’. *Ecological Explosions* shows that such an ethic is a work in progress, and probably always will be.
