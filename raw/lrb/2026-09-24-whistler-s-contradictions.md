# 正文

The​ *Whitehall Review* called her a ‘veritable daughter of the people, with all the defiance of the *prolétaire* in her attitude’. Seventeen years earlier, in a letter to Henri Fantin-Latour, Whistler had compared the painter’s exertions to ‘the pain of giving birth’. *The Chelsea Girl* (1884) was no great labour, however. It was the ‘sketch of one afternoon’, Whistler told the organisers of a Chicago exhibition in 1893, ‘the first statement or the beginning of a painting – I am not *excusing* it mind – of course it is a damn fine thing.’ *The Chelsea Girl* is now back in London, at Tate Britain’s *Whistler* exhibition (until 27 September), striking a pose, as the poet William Wenthe puts it, ‘feet apart, hands braced on hips,/a stout A, like Henry VIII in Holbein’s/portrait, except, being small, she is looking *up*.’

‘The Chelsea Girl’ (1884)

With chest puffed and arms akimbo, the defiant Chelsea girl tries to fill the empty space around her. Her pose is not playful. ‘The children of the very poor have no young times,’ Charles Lamb wrote earlier in the century. ‘It has come to be a woman, before it was a child. It has learned to go to market; it chaffers, it haggles, it envies, it murmurs; it is knowing, acute, sharpened; it never prattles. Had we not reason to say that the home of the very poor is no home?’ Whistler’s Chelsea girl is such a ‘little woman’ about the city, forced to assume the ways of the adult world – a far more self-possessed Little Nell than Master Humphrey found (Dickens was a favourite author of Whistler’s).

His contemporaries would have called her a ‘coster child’; Walter Sickert used the term when writing about the painting’s merits in 1925. She wears a bold yellow ‘kingsman’ around her neck, the costume of fruit and flower sellers. But she is Tudor monarch in pose only. In the late 19th century, Chelsea was not wealthy. The girl stands strident in an alley where she would have hawked her wares from early in the morning until late at night. The ‘coster’ tag was thought to derive from the costards, or apples, street vendors often sold. Children who hadn’t earned enough might be expected to sleep rough rather than return home. Whistler is sympathetic to the coster child’s nerve and her attempt to hold her own. These children were famous for giving the police a good dressing down when required.

Whistler’s response to the West End riots of 1886-87 reveals that he could be scornful in ink of what he admired in paint. The trouble began on ‘Black Monday’, 8 February 1886, when, as William Morris put it, ‘an immense crowd of poverty-stricken humanity’ gathered in Trafalgar Square. They were jeered at from the clubs of St James’s, and in turn smashed shopfronts. Writing to his patron, the society hostess Elizabeth Lewis, Whistler made his allegiance clear. He expressed ‘great indignation’ that his correspondent might have become ‘one of the victims of the brutality of “The People” in the Park – How horrible!’ By ‘The People’ (an odd term of abuse from an American), he presumably meant the struggling classes from which his Chelsea girl had sprung.

Whistler went on to say that the ringleaders should be hanged. ‘And among them, do you know I fear I should begin with Master Morris!!’ (Morris, then of the Socialist League, had ambivalently supported the demonstrations.) In his ‘Ten O’Clock’ lecture of 1885, delivered at Prince’s Hall in Piccadilly, Whistler satirised socialists and reformers for trying to get the ‘pestered people’ to care about art. Their error, as he saw it, was a mistaken reading of Shakespeare’s ‘one touch of Nature … makes the whole world kin.’ Even if we are all subject to hourglass and sickle, Whistler insisted, the ordinary man is no equal of Shakespeare or Velázquez. Who is he to Holbein?

Yet there is a kinship of sorts at work in his portrait of the coster girl. (Whistler had his contradictions.) ‘It’s a game of bluff my dear Rose,’ he wrote to his solicitor during the *Whistler v. Ruskin* trial of 1878, ‘and we mustn’t be bounced out!’ The ‘game of bluff’ in *The Chelsea Girl* was one he often played himself. He was diminutive of stature and his followers sometimes sensed a porcelain fragility beneath his bravado. Mortimer Menpes described him as ‘slight, small-boned and extremely dainty’. Robert Goodloe Harper Pennington thought that Whistler permitted few to see the ‘tender’ flesh beneath the ‘shell’. ‘You are tender-hearted,’ Pennington wrote, though ‘I dare say you would smack me “good and hard” if I was within reach.’ The coster girl’s embarrassed defiance has something of this Whistlerian ‘shell’. Her forthright stare and bold stance can’t entirely offset her youth or the flush of her pink cheeks. Other followers described the resemblance differently. Bernard Sickert, Walter’s brother, called Whistler ‘half Mephistopheles, half child’, echoing Oscar Wilde’s description of the ‘Ten O’Clock’ lecturer as ‘a miniature Mephistopheles mocking the majority!’ Leon Dabo said that Whistler was as ‘pure-minded as a young girl’ about his art.

The Ruskin trial challenged Whistler’s standing as a reputable gentleman. In 1877 Ruskin singled out his *Nocturne in Black and Gold: The Falling Rocket* for brutal criticism. He had seen the painting at the newly opened Grosvenor Gallery and in June he attacked it in one of his *Fors Clavigera* letters. ‘I have seen, and heard, much of Cockney impudence before now,’ Ruskin wrote, ‘but never expected to hear a coxcomb ask two hundred guineas for flinging a pot of paint in the public’s face.’ The painter George Henry Boughton showed the paragraph to Whistler, wondering whether it might be libellous. Whistler decided to find out.

Ruskin’s slurs – ‘Cockney’, ‘coxcomb’ – raised questions of social standing, not merely of artistry and propriety. In the letter calling the case a ‘game of bluff’, Whistler wrote: ‘The other side is not at all so cock sure as they pretend to be!’ The trial then subjected the insults to a more humiliating kind of scrutiny. Lawyers had to debate what Ruskin meant by ‘Cockney’: something prejudicial, as in ‘dirty and disagreeable’? Or merely a person knowing little beyond his own sphere, just as a cockney born within the sound of Bow Bells knows little of the countryside? Whistler won, but it was a sorry victory. The jury awarded him one farthing, not the substantial sum he desperately needed. The legal costs bankrupted him.

Still, he never lost his bravado. ‘After all, it’s a Whistler,’ he consoled Lois Cassatt, sensing her disappointment at the portrait he had produced of her (she thought it wasn’t a true likeness). Lois and her husband, Alexander Cassatt, had admired Whistler’s decoration of the Peacock Room in Frederick Richards Leyland’s house in Kensington. (It is recreated at Tate Britain, though not entirely successfully.) Alexander’s sister, the painter Mary Cassatt, advised against the commission and recommended Renoir instead. (Mary liked Whistler better than she liked his paintings.) The Cassatts called on Renoir and saw his pictures but left unimpressed.

They had reason to regret choosing Whistler. The decision was made in early April 1883; years later, the portrait still had not been delivered. Ruskin’s claim that Whistler spent too little time labouring on his pictures couldn’t have been more wrong; he repainted them endlessly, often ruining them in the process. Sickert thought this was precisely what had happened to the ‘laboured’ *Arrangement in Black, No. 8: Portrait of Mrs Cassatt.*

His failure to send the Cassatts a finished picture embarrassed Whistler. ‘I have been so long over it and the man has been so nice about it, that I feel ashamed of myself,’ he told the artist Sidney Starr – though it was not uncommon for him to hold back portraits from purchasers for years. When Alexander paid him a visit towards the end of 1884, he heard Whistler, on being informed who had arrived, ‘gasp out, in a frightened whisper, “Mr Cassatt!”’ In September 1885, with the portrait still unfinished, Mary wrote to Alexander that Whistler was ‘dreadfully distressed’ because he ‘could not find a model to finish the dress with’. He had wanted Lois to sit for longer.

*The Chelsea Girl*, itself the unfinished inspiration of an afternoon, was given to the Cassatts as compensation for the delay over Lois’s portrait. The two paintings were delivered together in 1886 or 1887. They make an odd couple: an English urchin and an ‘American aristocrat’ (Lois was a niece of James Buchanan, the fifteenth US president). As Sickert noted, Whistler’s ‘coster child’ is a finer picture than *Arrangement in Black, No. 8*. Lois emerges as something between a China doll and an unconvincing Swinburnian dominatrix, perhaps betraying something of her reputed snobbishness. She wanted to be painted in one of her glamorous dresses, but Whistler insisted on riding gear. Set against a dark background, she all but disappears. The Chelsea girl, by contrast, seems to spring out of her boots and apron.

Whistler may well have had another painter in mind when he chose to present *The Chelsea Girl* to the Cassatts. Childhood had already become Mary Cassatt’s great theme. Would *The Chelsea Girl* hang in view of one of her mother and child paintings back in America? Those children led a very different life from Whistler’s coster girl. ‘Children, health, horses and money’ were the Cassatt family concerns, as Nancy Hale put it. Whistler disturbs the solid view of childhood found in Cassatt’s paintings. In her work, children are held and holding. The viewer seems to lean over the shoulder of an adoring mother as she cradles her child. Her babies and children hold fruit, mirrors, their mother’s hand; they cling to their nursemaids; they reach out for buckets and spades. Money and firm family architecture hold them in return: parents, grandparents, hired help, prams and plush armchairs, and even ‘Aunt Mary’, whom the Cassatt children came to prefer to their own mother.

*The Chelsea Girl* is held only by our gaze. She might be a ‘veritable daughter of the people’, but there is no mother to hold her. In Whistler’s picture, her hands are planted firmly on her hips, but they are sketchily drawn, as though in movement. These are hands that swap apples for pence. When Whistler was a child, his father criticised his ‘carelessness’: ‘Finish your work my boy!’ Yet a stubborn resistance to that injunction is what gives life to *The Chelsea Girl*. In *The Gentle Art of Making Enemies* (1890), Whistler implied that an inability to appreciate unfinishedness in painting betrayed a failure of negative capability – the capacity to dwell in uncertainty.

Whistler has not had a major retrospective in Europe in the last thirty years. The Tate show should have been an excellent opportunity for a reappraisal. It claims to bring together his ‘world-famous paintings’ alongside less well-known works, but in truth there are too few of the former. The Musée d’Orsay has lent the most famous picture of all, *Arrangement in Grey and Black: Portrait of the Painter’s Mother,* but what about its counterpoint, *Arrangement in Grey and Black, No. 2: Portrait of Thomas Carlyle*? What about *Symphony in White, No. 1: The White Girl*, which is key to his aesthetic philosophy? Without *Nocturne in Black and Gold: The Falling Rocket*, how can we assess Ruskin’s attack, or the fallout that followed? Other Nocturnes are included, but none is so daring. The portrait of Lois Cassatt isn’t a major work, but its inclusion would have been instructive. *The Chelsea Girl* doesn’t get a mention in the catalogue, though more than forty unexhibited works by Whistler do. She is the reason to visit, though – and hurry. She returns to Arkansas soon.
