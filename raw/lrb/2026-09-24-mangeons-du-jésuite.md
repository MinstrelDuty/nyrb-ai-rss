# 正文

Gustave Lanson​ , doyen of Enlightenment scholarship a century ago, described Voltaire’s early masterpiece *Lettres philosophiques* as ‘la première bombe lancée contre l’ancien régime’. It’s true that Voltaire would become a hero of the revolution (his ashes were ceremoniously moved to the Panthéon in 1791), and there were plenty of things about the book that Louis XV’s censors must have disliked. There were chapters celebrating freedom of conscience and habeas corpus, attacking religious persecution and press censorship, and advocating limited monarchy and the separation of powers. Worse, a superficially technical discussion of a passage in Locke (the proposition that God might ‘superadd’ to matter the power of thought) implied that immaterial and hence immortal souls were a fiction. *Lettres philosophiques* appeared in London in 1733 as *Letters Concerning the English Nation*; a year later, when the work was published in French in a riot of illicit editions and false imprints, a lettre de cachet was issued for Voltaire’s arrest and copies were burned by the Parlement of Paris (which turbocharged sales).

When Voltaire arrived in London in May 1726 (‘without a penny, sick to death of a violent ague, a stranger, alone, helpless’, he told a correspondent in his newly mastered English), he was fresh from a spectacular feud with the Chevalier de Rohan-Chabot, a powerful aristocrat, which had landed him in the Bastille to cool off. He stayed in England until the autumn of 1728, acquiring and alienating friends, and drafting his philosophic (in fact often vividly journalistic) letters. Voltaire looks innocent enough in a recently identified sketch by William Hogarth, which shows him sitting in Button’s Coffee House, with just a hint of his trademark hideous grin. The impression of decorum was false. At the Twickenham house of Alexander Pope, he responded to a polite inquiry about his health from Pope’s elderly mother by declaring that his schoolmasters, ‘those damned Jesuits, when I was a boy, buggered me to such a degree that I shall never get over it.’ Mrs Pope fled in horror. The anecdote has been disputed (its proximate source, the poet Thomas Gray, liked to embellish), and the Académiciens of the Lanson era were having none of it. But it fits the rambunctious pattern of Voltaire’s London years, as he lived it up in theatres and coffee houses, and hobnobbed with everyone from Tory grandees to radical Quakers. He insulted the dying William Congreve by pooh-poohing his self-image as no mere playwright but a gentleman; he picked a fight with the clergyman-poet Edward Young by trashing a famous passage in *Paradise Lost*; he quarrelled with the Duchess of Marlborough when she attempted to recruit him to polish her memoirs; he annoyed the distinguished list of subscribers to his 1728 poem *La Henriade* (‘He for subscribers baits his hook,/And takes their cash – but where’s the book?’ as Charles Churchill said of Samuel Johnson in a similar case). Gray even claimed that Voltaire would have been hanged for forging banknotes if he hadn’t left England in an unexplained hurry. Decades later, on a pilgrimage to Ferney, Voltaire’s retreat outside Geneva, James Boswell reported that he still spoke English in the argot of Covent Garden in its rakish heyday: ‘He swore bloodily, as was the fashion when he was in England.’

Voltaire was a pen name, an assumed and performed identity. Born François-Marie Arouet in 1694 (the baptismal register gives one date, Voltaire gave another), he was the youngest of five children (three made it to adulthood) born to a well-connected lawyer and his slightly more blue-blooded wife, who died when Voltaire was about seven. The Lycée Louis-le-Grand, scene of his Jesuit education between 1704 and 1711, was said to be the best school in Paris: Saint-Évremond and Molière were alumni; so, later, were Robespierre and the Marquis de Sade. After halting attempts to fulfil his father’s ambitions for him (he dropped out of law school and abandoned a junior diplomatic position), he immersed himself in libertine literary salons and earned his first taste of the Bastille (for a squib that offended the court) as early as 1717. With positioning that attracted solemn comment in the heyday of Freudian criticism, he chose the title page of his tragedy *Œdipe* (1718) to renounce his father’s name in public, replacing it with an anagram in neoclassical typography (‘Arovet l[e] i[eune]’ transforms to Voltaire), but more important, with an identity that had connotations of soaring flight (*voler*), acrobatics or vaulting (*voltige*), and of volatility – the abrupt, brazen menace of the volte-face. Rohan-Chabot thought the name an affectation, and he had a point; hence the feud. Later, Voltaire would repudiate his Arouet lineage in favour of a more glamorous option: the supposed liaison of his mother with a mysterious nobleman, musketeer and wit called Guérin de Rochebrune.

As for the Jesuits of Louis-le-Grand, they cut a predictably poor figure across Voltaire’s massive oeuvre, which runs to a shelf-buckling 205 volumes in the standard modern edition (a paradox, one of many, in a writer famed for his barbed epigrams and lethal concision). There’s occasional praise, if sometimes ironic, for Jesuit learning and humanity, but it’s overwhelmed by satirical venom. In the grim universe of *Candide* (1759), the optimistic yet syphilitic Pangloss traces the genealogy of his disease in a chain of scandalous misalliances, with a pederastic Jesuit in the central role. Syphilis becomes the inherited curse of colonial crime: ‘An old countess … contracted it from a captain in the cavalry, who owed it to a marchioness, who had it from a page, who had caught it from a Jesuit, who, during his novitiate, had inherited it in a direct line from one of Christopher Columbus’s shipmates.’ In his study of *Candide*’s sources and Voltaire’s working methods, Edward Langille cites the Oreillons of Jesuit-ruled Paraguay (Voltaire’s version of Montaigne’s Tupinambá), who think it better to eat their defeated overlords than leave their bodies for the crows: the Oreillons’ refrain – ‘mangeons du jésuite, mangeons du jésuite’ – should be seen ‘as a pent-up statement of revenge’ on Voltaire’s part. Jesuits are a more incongruous presence in *Letters Concerning the English Nation*, which builds much of its argument on Quaker egalitarianism and Anglican latitude, but Voltaire manages a few sideswipes. He exulted when the suppression of the Jesuit order gathered pace in his later years. ‘Écrasez l’infâme!’ he used to exclaim in his correspondence.

Numerous claims have been made for the significance of Voltaire’s English period, but if anything stands out, it’s the transformation of the tragedian and epic poet (*Œdipe*, *La Henriade*) who arrived in London into a wily ironist in the Scriblerian mode of Pope and Jonathan Swift. *Letters Concerning the English Nation* celebrates wits of the past like Edmund Waller, quick-thinking when confronted by Charles II, who ‘reproached the poet for not writing with so much energy and fire as when he had applauded the Usurper (meaning Oliver [Cromwell]); “*Sir*,” replied Waller to the King, “*we poets succeed better in fiction than in truth*.”’ The most striking thing about Voltaire’s book, however, is his sense that he was witnessing, indeed living amid, a ferment of satire, bookended by *Gulliver’s Travels* the year he arrived (he cherished his friendship with Swift, ‘the English Rabelais’) and *The Dunciad* the year he left: Pope was ‘the best poet of England, and at present, of all the world’. John Gay showed Voltaire the script of his anti-Walpole sensation *The Beggar’s Opera* before its first performance at Lincoln’s Inn Fields in January 1728, which he probably attended. It was a legendary event, marked by the prime minister’s presence in one of the boxes; when a song about corruption made the audience look his way, Walpole rose to his feet and called for an encore. Voltaire also spent time with Lady Mary Wortley Montagu, now at daggers drawn with Pope, who had learned about smallpox vaccination on her Ottoman travels and was popularising it in England, in tandem with Queen Caroline. At least ten thousand children, Voltaire wrote, ‘owe in this manner their lives to her Majesty, and to the Lady Wortley Montagu; and as many of the fair sex are obliged to them for their beauty’.

It’s not clear whether Voltaire also met, in 1728, the new kid in town, Henry Fielding. Fielding’s meteoric rise began a year or two later, when he cheekily styled himself Scriblerus Secundus and produced a dazzling series of comedies, ballad operas and increasingly political farces, until the 1737 Licensing Act stopped him in his tracks. Then came his groundbreaking mock-epic novels, *Joseph Andrews* and *Tom Jones*, and a brief but consequential career as a magistrate (he founded the Bow Street Runners, precursors of the Metropolitan Police); then his death in Lisbon, wrecked by hard living, a year before the earthquake of 1755. As Voltaire began his last year in England, Fielding was twenty years old and reckless (a botched attempt to abduct and elope with his 15-year-old cousin; a prosecution for assault), but he cut a dash. Given an entrée to the London scene by Lady Mary, his second cousin and a formidable enabler, he published his earliest surviving satire, *The Masquerade*, a day after the first performance of *The Beggar’s Opera*. Two weeks later, his debut comedy, *Love in Several Masques*, opened with an all-star cast at the Theatre Royal on Drury Lane, where it was Voltaire’s habit to sit in the orchestra and follow along with copy provided by the prompter in an attempt to perfect his English idioms. If you want to understand English comedy and the English language, he told French readers, the only way is to spend three years in London and ‘frequent the playhouse every night’.

‘Almost everything is imitation,’ Voltaire wrote in one of the French editions of *Lettres philosophiques*. Books were like a borrowed flame: ‘We go to a neighbour to get the embers, light the fire when we return home and then pass it on to others; it belongs to everyone.’ For the truffle-hunting positivists of the Lanson era, there could be no more frustrating attitude. When Lanson’s disciple André Morize announced in his 1913 edition of *Candide* that Voltaire’s source was ‘toute la production romanesque’, this wasn’t a celebration of infinite intertextuality à la Julia Kristeva. It was a reluctant acknowledgment that with his magpie habits, magical transformations and powerfully original intelligence, Voltaire would always give source-hunters the slip. More recent scholars have associated *Candide*, in its broad strokes or local details, with novelists and satirists including Cervantes, Fénelon, Gracián, Lesage, Prévost, Rabelais, Scudéry and Sorel. It’s nearly impossible, however, to catch him in a theft or even pin down a reworking. Perhaps *Gulliver’s Travels* swims into view in particular episodes (the coupling of monkeys with Oreillons is pure Yahoo) or overarching themes (the technology of slaughter; the arbitrariness of gold as a store of value). An obscure travelogue by Louis-Charles Fougeret de Monbron is known to have provided some basic materials. No earlier work, however, helps explain the strangeness and virtuosity of *Candide*: its deadpan ironies and unresolved contradictions; its zany narrative leaps and accelerations; its eerily crystalline utterance; its unruffled, even anaesthetised tone in the face of atrocity and disaster. Nothing heralds the madcap plot, which takes Voltaire’s hero on a dizzying tour of real-world and mythical places: paradisal Westphalia; the killing fields of war-torn Europe; inquisitorial Portugal and colonial Paraguay; unconquered forests and magical Eldorado; shark-infested Paris and carnivalesque Venice; the garden of lemons and pistachios that Candide cultivates at last. Political and philosophical issues (the depredations of colonialism and war; the absurdities of Leibnizian theodicy) suggest ways to make sense of this enigmatic text. So do more straightforward works like Voltaire’s poem on the Lisbon earthquake, which he framed as a refutation of Leibniz, Shaftesbury and other believers that (in Pope’s phrase) ‘whatever is, is right.’ Yet there’s always an untameable surplus of meaning, a capricious excess of style.

For Langille​ , *Tom Jones* provides a new solution to the puzzle. Published in 1749 as *The History of Tom Jones, a Foundling* and translated into French a year later by Pierre-Antoine de La Place as *L’Histoire de Tom Jones, ou l’enfant trouvé*, Fielding’s masterpiece is a bravura display of social satire, narrative pyrotechnics and destabilising ambiguities and ironies. Thematically, it pursues questions that were of central interest for Voltaire: allegiance and rebellion, the vagaries of providence, the instability of identity in a world of transgressive couplings and ancient secrets. Langille argues for the influence of *Tom Jones* with tenacity and skill, identifying coincidences of wording (some more persuasive than others) in the La Place translation and postulating a creative process in which Voltaire condensed, combined, transformed, contested and spun off from his source at dizzying tangents. Explicitly, Langille’s guiding light is Paul Valéry in his *Lettre sur Mallarmé*: ‘There are works in the likeness of others, and works that are the reverse of others, but there are also works whose affiliation with earlier productions is so intricate that we become confused and attribute them to the intervention of the gods.’

There are obstacles in Langille’s way. It’s no longer thought that Voltaire wrote sections of *Letters Concerning the English Nation* in English, but he did write personal letters and a pair of treatises (one on French history, one on epic poetry) in English, and even claimed that he had become accustomed ‘to think in English’. Why would he have read Fielding’s masterpiece in French translation, and in a translation that was slapdash, bowdlerised and heavily abridged? La Place was proud of his ability to elevate English barbarism into conformity with French politesse, to the point, in his version of Aphra Behn’s *Oroonoko*, of replacing the brutal climax of the tale (‘frightful spectacles of a mangled king’) with a happy ending. Fielding’s ending didn’t present a similar problem, but *Tom Jones* was far too English in other ways. La Place reconfigured the novel as a sentimental romance, smoothing Fielding’s exuberant style into a decorous monotone, simplifying his disruptive practice of ‘double irony’ (the phrase is William Empson’s), and eliminating his metafictional flourishes, including the playful opening chapters of each of its eighteen books. It’s revealing that when Mme de Graffigny devoured *L’Histoire de Tom Jones* within weeks of publication, the visual correlative she reached for was not rough, satirical Hogarth (the default comparison in England) but serene, harmonious Chardin. If La Place had wanted to purge everything that might have caught Voltaire’s attention, he could scarcely have done a better job. Given *Candide*’s demolition of philosophical optimism, one notable casualty was a deft punchline reworked by Fielding from Bernard Mandeville. There are philosophers who teach that virtue is the road to happiness and vice to misery, Fielding declares with disarming smoothness: ‘A very wholesome and comfortable doctrine, to which we have but one objection, namely, that it is not true.’

There are other difficulties. Voltaire’s one recorded mention of *Tom Jones* is dismissive (‘there is nothing of interest, save the character of a barber,’ he told Mme du Deffand). A copy of *L’Histoire de Tom Jones* survives in his library, but with no marginalia or other signs of close attention such as ribbons or paste-ins. No preliminary notebooks or authorial manuscripts exist to show Voltaire gathering material or drafting *Candide*: there is only a transcription in another hand (the La Vallière manuscript, copied by Voltaire’s young secretary, Jean-Louis Wagnière) which preserves an alternative text of the Paris chapter. These are slim pickings. Langille has answers on all such points, liberally using the argument from absence, but they’re still worth entertaining. Dogged throughout his career by plagiarism allegations, Voltaire was cautious about voicing admiration for other works and disinclined to leave evidence that might be used against him. He’s known to have manhandled the books he quarried, often ripping out relevant pages, and he’s also known to have owned duplicate copies of key texts; perhaps there was a working copy of La Place alongside the pristine library edition (now in St Petersburg, where Catherine the Great had Wagnière reassemble the Ferney collection after Voltaire’s death). On the related question of *Candide*’s relative silence about England and the English (there’s the famous passage about Admiral Byng, executed ‘pour encourager les autres’, but not much more), Langille calls Candide’s refusal to set foot in England a ‘textbook example’ of the ‘anxiety of influence’, suggesting ‘Voltaire’s need to distance himself from his English models’.

An alternative explanation would be that it shows Voltaire’s cultural and political disillusionment with the land of liberty he had once celebrated. After *Candide*’s attack on colonialism in Jesuit Paraguay and Dutch Surinam (Langille plausibly suggests the impact of *Oroonoko* here), a new instance is cited: Britain and France are now ‘at war over a few acres of snow across in Canada’ and it’s hard to say which nation harbours more criminals. For pithy critique of the Seven Years’ War, Voltaire was no match here for Johnson, who called ‘the American dispute between the French and us … only the quarrel of two robbers for the spoils of a passenger’. But the animus is clear. The golden age of *Letters Concerning the English Nation* was over, and the heroes of that work were all dead. ‘The time of Newton, Locke, Pope, Addison, Steele, Swift, is gone,’ Voltaire told his old friend Everard Fawkener, whose Wandsworth house had been his first refuge in England. ‘It seems all the world is degenerated.’
