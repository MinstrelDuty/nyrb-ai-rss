# 正文

Football players​ and managers often use the phrase ‘the dark arts’ to refer to practices that stretch, but don’t always break, the rules. Examples include time-wasting of various kinds, feigning injury and – most recently – what’s described as ‘grappling’, usually inside the penalty area during corners. Off the field, they include putting pressure on referees and the authorities, or the use of what Italians call *pretattica* – attempts to wind up opponents or influence a game before the players take the field. In Richard Fitzpatrick’s lively biography of the Argentinian-French player and manager Helenio Herrera, the dark arts go beyond rule-stretching to doping and bribery. Yet despite the book’s subtitle, this is an affectionate portrait.

Herrera, who was one of the most famous people in Italy in the 1960s, has already been the subject of many biographies and was the author of a rip-roaring if highly unreliable autobiography. His life was enveloped in layers of fantasy and invention. He even faked his date of birth. We can’t know for certain what went on in dressing rooms and team hotels or on training grounds when Herrera was a manager in the 1950s, 1960s and 1970s. What we do have are many, often contradictory, stories told by players, managers and journalists trying to sell papers – oral histories, essentially, many of them unverifiable. The tellers often have their own axe to grind, their own conspiracy to air.

This description applies most obviously to Ferruccio Mazzola, son of the Torino player Valentino Mazzola, who died in the Superga air disaster in May 1949 along with the rest of his team. Ferruccio was good enough to play for Lazio and Fiorentina, but never as good as his brother, Sandro, who became one of the greatest Italian players, winning everything with the ‘Grande Inter’ team of the 1960s, managed by Herrera. Ferruccio is the source for a number of the accusations in this book, but they should be treated with scepticism. He was one of the few to go public with claims of doping and cheating in the closed world of professional football, but he had scores to settle after being dropped from Inter as a young player and seeing his career stutter.

According to Fitzpatrick, Herrera ‘didn’t invent doping in Italy, but he practised it with such gusto that it cast a shadow over his work’. At the heart of his story is the mysterious death of Giuliano Taccola, a striker at Roma, where Herrera moved in 1968. Fitzpatrick picks through the evidence but doesn’t reach a firm conclusion. Did Herrera push his players too hard? Almost certainly. Was he alone in this? Almost certainly not. Was doping widespread in football in the 1960s and 1970s? Undoubtedly. Was it used at every major club? Probably. What kind of doping was it? Mainly amphetamines and painkillers, which affected different people in different ways, and could have negative effects. More scientific forms of doping did not arrive until the 1980s and 1990s.

Herrera’s Spanish parents moved to Buenos Aires, where he was born in 1910, then to Casablanca. He experienced grinding poverty and homelessness as a child. Football was his escape. He was a pretty good player for several French clubs before becoming the first celebrity football manager, with a fan base that followed him from team to team. He was loved by journalists because he supplied them with so much material: controversial statements and polemics, but also a magnetic presence on the touchline. He retired as a player in 1945, and managed Stade Français in Paris for three years before moving to Spain. In describing Herrera’s victories there – above all with Atlético Madrid and then Barcelona in the 1950s – Fitzpatrick is careful to pay attention to the tumultuous history of postcivil war Spain, where the idea of football as just another sport was laughable. The second half of the book concerns Herrera’s career in Italy, where he moved in 1960 to manage Inter Milan, creating its squad of legends.

His timing was perfect. Milan, like Italy generally, was booming in the 1960s – experiencing rates of growth rarely seen in the modern world. There was no unemployment. Fortunes were being made and Milan was at the centre of this economic miracle, which was reflected in, and connected to, football. In the 1960s, with its two clubs winning constantly in Italy and in Europe, Milan could claim to be the strongest football city in the world. An oil magnate owned Inter and poured money into the club; AC Milan was bankrolled by publishers and textile millionaires. Here, Herrera became a superstar, or a supervillain. A disciplinarian and control freak, one of his problems was also his greatest asset when he arrived in Milan: the Argentinian striker Antonio Angelillo, who had scored 33 goals in the 1958-59 season in Serie A (a record that stood for decades). It was said he had ‘paintbrushes in his boots’, but Herrera didn’t like Angelillo’s fondness for nightclubs and nightclub singers and sold him to Roma. It seemed crazy, but the transfer fee bought a genius from Barcelona, Luisito Suárez.

Herrera’s total control over the players and their lives wasn’t new in Italy, where footballers were expected to behave like athletes and drinking was frowned on. When Jimmy Greaves was signed by AC Milan in 1961 he was given a daily schedule, including bedtimes. He lasted ten games. Herrera liked to write motivational slogans on the walls of the dressing room and shout them out on the training ground. Some of them have become part of football folklore, such as the claim that ‘we will win on the bus on the way to the ground.’ Others were banal pop psychology: ‘Have confidence in yourself.’ He is associated with a particularly cynical type of defensive football, often seen as in some way Italian, known as *catenaccio* – the word for a bolt or lock. It’s true that the Inter teams he managed were very good defensively, but they were also masters of the counter-attack and not all of his players were defensive workhorses. Mario Corso, for example, ‘god’s left foot’, rarely ran far, was said to stand in the shade on hot days and didn’t track back, but he could change the game with a pass or a free kick, and Herrera kept him in the team for years. Giacinto Facchetti, a dashing, rangy left-back, was a new kind of player – an attacking defender – and Herrera made him captain. In his version of *catenaccio*, there was a sweeper, and the counter-attack was key. But *catenaccio* was also a state of mind, a dedication to winning at all costs.

The Grande Inter era began in 1963, when the team won its first Serie A title, and lasted four years. Inter beat Real Madrid to win the European Cup final in 1964; the score was 3-1, with Sandro Mazzola getting two goals. When I lived in Milan, I was once invited to the flat of Massimo Moratti, who was then chairman of Inter. On the wall was a painting by Lucio Fontana, known for his slashed canvases. It was in blue and black, Inter’s colours, with three cuts. I asked about the picture, and Moratti said it was called *Inter 3, Real Madrid 1*. Fontana was an Inter fan.

Doping first became a major issue in Italian football during the 1963-64 season. In March 1964, the Italian Football Federation docked three points from Bologna, who were then at the top of the league table, claiming that five of their players had tested positive for performance-enhancing drugs. There were doubts over possible tampering with the specimens and eventually Bologna got the points back, leaving them level with Inter. There was a play-off to decide the championship. Inter lost 2-0 and there were riots on the streets. The European Cup provided some consolation.

Herrera enjoyed controversy. In 1961 he sent out his youth team against Juventus in the final game of the season in protest at the replaying of the match (a pitch invasion the first time round had called Inter’s win into question). They lost 9-1. His team developed an international reputation for dodgy methods after a European Cup game against Liverpool in 1965. Liverpool won the first leg, at Anfield, 3-1. According to some accounts, the referee in the return leg, José María Ortiz de Mendíbil, was bribed by Inter through a middleman. After eight minutes, Mario Corso scored one of his trademark ‘dead leaf’ free kicks, rising over the wall and falling straight into the goal, but the Liverpool players claimed that de Mendíbil had signalled for an indirect free kick, which can’t result in a direct score. The second goal was even more controversial: Joaquín Peiró nicked the ball from Liverpool’s goalkeeper, Tommy Lawrence, as he was bouncing it in his own area and passed it into the net. Facchetti scored a third goal. Liverpool were out. Inter won the final, beating Benfica 1-0. The football journalist Brian Glanville, who loved Italy and Italian football, accused Inter of bribing Mendíbil, calling it the ‘golden fix’. Fitzpatrick doesn’t repeat this accusation, but hints at it. Again, there are only accusations, insinuations and doubts, based in part on what happened in the game itself. If Inter were bribing referees, it didn’t always work. It isn’t easy to fix football matches, particularly important ones.

Inter won the Serie A title again that season, and in 1966, but in 1967 the team suffered a series of losses, which have often been blamed – again without real evidence – on a ‘down’ linked to excessive doping, or on Herrera pushing his players too hard. They were beaten 2-1 by Celtic in the European Cup final in Lisbon. Perhaps they were tired; perhaps a ‘cycle’, as the Italians say, had simply run its course. Perhaps the economic miracle was over, and with it Milan’s centrality. Many interpreted the match as the death of *catenaccio*: after Inter went ahead with a penalty in the seventh minute, they stopped trying to score while Celtic made dozens of attempts on goal. Worse was to follow. Inter blew a healthy lead in the league, losing to Mantova 1-0 on the final day of the championship thanks to a mistake by their goalkeeper. Juventus took the Scudetto. Often, at the end of the season, Italian teams with nothing to play for allow teams that need the points to win. This time it was different. Two trophies gone. The next season Inter finished fifth. Herrera moved to Roma.

A few months later, in February 1969, the Roma striker Giuliano Taccola had his tonsils out. Herrera wanted him back in the team quickly. After a game in Cagliari in March, Taccola had a seizure in the dressing room and died of heart failure before he got to hospital. He was 25. The main accusation against Herrera is that he didn’t look after the health of a young player, but the press claimed he had driven Taccola to his death. Again, there is no definitive truth. Some blamed doping; others said Taccola had been forced to play when he was seriously unwell (though it was unclear what exactly was wrong with him).

Afterwards, Herrera’s career fizzled out, with short-term appointments that mostly ended in failure, though he did win the Copa del Rey with Barcelona in 1981, more than twenty years after his first managerial stint with the team. He retired to Venice with his much younger wife, Fiora Gandolfi. Fitzpatrick’s interview with Gandolfi is a scoop. She doesn’t hide Herrera’s philandering, but stresses his personality and verve. He died at the age of what everyone thought was 81, but turned out to be 87, and was buried on the cemetery island of San Michele with an effigy of the European Cup on his tomb.
