# 正文

In the mid-1930s,​ Mary Lavin was working on her PhD in Dublin. Her subject was Virginia Woolf. That she chose an author associated with such experimental works as *Orlando* and *The Waves* in a grey, conformist city that did more to send modernist writers into exile than to read them (Joyce and Beckett) tells us a lot about her confidence. Even more striking, though, is what led her to abandon the work. As she explained to Eavan Boland many years later, she called one day at the office of her future husband, a solicitor, and struck up a conversation with an elderly client. Told that she was writing about Woolf, the woman ‘said something like, oh, that’s strange because I just recently had tea with her’. Lavin was astonished, and in a most peculiar way: ‘Suddenly, I realised that Virginia Woolf was alive.’

She told Boland: ‘I hadn’t exactly thought of her as being alive in the sense that I was alive … In some strange way I had always thought of great writers … as having passed over the divide between life and death into a world of the preserved dead.’ As it sank in that Woolf was busy in ‘the world of pots and pans and dances and parties’ (like a version of Clarissa Dalloway) while she was stuck in libraries, Lavin found it hard to keep her mind on the job. Even though she had a freshly typed-up version of her thesis to correct, ‘those words of the old lady kept coming back into my mind. Imagine having tea with Virginia Woolf! And I turned over the typescript and began to write a story.’

The anecdote turns on a shift from criticism to creativity, but it has more to tell us than that. There’s a characteristic morbidity in the young Lavin’s thoughts about living authors being preserved as though undead. Her stories are drawn to graveyards and attempts to bring back the lost: a soldier killed in France, summoned by a fire; a bottle of lemonade left on the grave of a little boy. She wrote movingly and – given recent scandals – presciently in ‘The Lost Child’ about untended stones marking the place where the bodies of unbaptised babies had been more cast away than laid to rest. Even more frequent in her work than the death of children is that of spouses. Losing her first husband when she was 42, with three daughters to look after, and then, late in life, her second husband, an admirer from her student days, Lavin knew plenty about widowhood.

The anecdote also has contrary implications, one of which is that writing a story on the back of a critical study need not involve a rejection of criticism. It might instead alert us to the exercise of critical intelligence that is always behind Lavin’s work. She increasingly put her stories through multiple drafts, enlarging then reducing them, making innumerable critical decisions to conceal art with art. She said in later life that she wished she had been a critic, and that she read criticism like detective stories, to keep her mind active. John Montague, the poet and story writer, once told her that he distrusted the work of any writer who read so many critical studies. ‘I distrust the work of any creative writer who doesn’t,’ she replied.

Lavin published some of the finest short stories since Joyce, along with two novels, children’s books and a clutch of poems, and the scale of her achievement was recognised by J.D. Salinger, Eudora Welty and V.S. Pritchett. But as her liberal, educated Catholic Ireland slipped into the past, and lost some of its credibility by association with a disgraced Church, her work too faded from view. In addition, though she wrote primarily and sympathetically about female experience, her qualified relationship with feminism made her less attractive to those committed to women’s writing. Now she is being rediscovered. John Banville recently called her ‘a timeless master’. Colm Tóibín’s astute selection from her stories, *An Arrow in Flight*, will allow others to judge.

Much of what she wanted to say was there from the start. Take ‘Miss Holland’, the story written on the back of her thesis. After years spent in the company of her genteel father, his death obliges the spinster protagonist to move into a lodging house with medical students and office workers. She is more at ease with the marmalade tomcat that pirouettes in the garden than with the young people, who exclude her with ‘torrents of talk’ about the birth rate, the cost of living and dental services in rural areas. They are warm-hearted but uncouth: they put their elbows on the table. Miss Holland had felt for some time that ‘father and herself would eventually have to make changes in their way of life.’ That ‘new world’ has arrived.

You could take a wide-angle view of this story and relate it to conflicts in 1930s Ireland. Power was being transferred from a conservative establishment of well-off farmers, business leaders and professional men like Lavin’s first husband, who became a Fine Gael councillor, to people from small farms and back streets. The success of Fianna Fáil changed the make-up of the public sphere and fed into demands for reform. The country was heaving with youngsters looking for opportunity rather than emigration. In 1937, when Lavin probably wrote ‘Miss Holland’, Éamon de Valera’s government drafted a new constitution and reset Ireland’s relationship with Britain.

This is all relevant, but it cannot bring into focus ‘Miss Holland’, which, like all Lavin’s stories, grows out of psychology and circumstance. A subtly deployed free indirect style keeps us sympathetic to the protagonist while allowing a margin of irony. The effect is precise yet suggestive. As Tóibín says in his introduction, Lavin ‘was more interested in a character she had invented in all its strangeness and individuality than she was in wider society; she was more interested in families than politics.’ Though immersed in politics during her first marriage, in her stories she put values where they are to be found in most people’s lives, among choices and refusals that are not ideologically explicit.

‘Miss Holland’ highlights the challenge to the finer feelings posed by the plain people of Ireland. Its heroine traces with a tender finger an ornamental rosebud on her mantelpiece, caked over with dusty white paint, and whispers encouragements to it. She rehearses a conversation in which she will claim the attention of the other lodgers by comparing the prancing of the cat to flamenco. Before she can launch into this set piece, however, one of the young men triggers hilarity by announcing that, the night before, he had dealt with the cat’s mewlings by scaring it off with a shotgun blast. Miss Holland looks around the company with revulsion and leaves, but is ignored even in her exit.

The story that opens Lavin’s first collection, *Tales from Bective Bridge* (1943), probes similar issues. In ‘Lilacs’, the fortunes of a family spring out of a dung heap. Phelim realises as a young man that he can take manure from local farmers and sell it on to people for their gardens. This funds his marriage to Ros and the boarding school education of their daughters, but leads to complaint. ‘Oh Phelim,’ Ros protests. ‘If only it could be moved somewhere else besides under the window of the room where we eat our bit of food.’ Their daughter Stacy asks him to plant lilacs to block the smell. Kate, more forthright, sees no need for them to deal in dung at all.

‘Lilacs’ is the first of Lavin’s widow stories. Phelim sickens and dies. To the consternation of her daughters, Ros takes over the business, not even pausing to mourn, but supervising in her everyday clothes the arrival of cart-loads of muck. They are slow to grasp that marriage is stronger than death, and that Ros is bound to Phelim by ‘gold rings of dung’. Ros also knows that without it the family will go hungry. ‘Did it never occur to you,’ she asks Kate and Stacy, ‘that it might not be easy for us, three women with no man about the place, to keep going, to put food on the table and keep a fire on the hearth, to say nothing at all about finery and fal-lals.’

Lavin approves of Phelim and Ros because they ‘keep going’. They labour in the same way that she, writing in bed before her children got up, had to, or chose to. Yet she also sympathises with Stacy’s wish for a lilac tree outside the window. Like Miss Holland she hankers after a beauty in small things that would often be dismissed as fal-lals. ‘Lilacs’, Lavin explained, looks at

> the way, in Ireland, there is a constant effort to resist the advances of beauty in any form … I believe that this hurts me more than most people but also I am able to bear it better, because I believe that it dates back to a time when people in Ireland actually were afraid to show any sign of prosperity, even by a new tea-cup, or a new coat of paint, in case their taxes would have been raised by the absentee landlord’s vigilant agent.

These divisions are passed on. Kate, her father’s daughter, builds up the dung business rather than ending it when Ros dies, until she has enough of a dowry to make an advantageous marriage and leaves the smelly yard behind her. Stacy, withdrawn and sensitive, stays at home, dreaming only of such fal-lals as replacing an ugly red sofa. The future is unclear to her, except that she plans, as she tells the family lawyer, finally to remove the dung heap and replace it with lilacs. This is a false ending, though. A new inevitability emerges with the lawyer’s question: ‘But what will you live on, Miss Stacy?’

The sceptical clarity of these stories makes Lavin modern. Where did it come from? In Ireland, the history of the genre is full of confusing riches: the tales of Maria Edgeworth that encouraged Walter Scott to write about Scotland; Sheridan Le Fanu’s protestant Gothic; William Carleton’s stories from Tyrone, which drew on oral tradition. By the time Yeats edited *Representative Irish Tales* in 1891, responding to the fin-de-siècle fashion for stories, he had a large corpus to draw on. But it was reasonable for him to object that much of it was culturally deficient. Before Joyce’s *Dubliners,* Irish stories were often episodic, digressive and overly concerned to explain the Irish to English readers. The picture changed with George Moore, whose *Untilled Field* (1903) was heavily influenced by Turgenev and included brief, atmospheric tales about celibacy, emigration and the domination of rural Ireland by the Church.

The Irish short​ story, then, was launched into modernity by the Russians. Its leading exponent in the generation after Joyce, Frank O’Connor, was one of those who said that it all came out of Gogol’s overcoat. Lavin’s story ‘An Akoulina of the Irish Midlands’, which deals with the courtship of a waitress from a Catholic family and a Protestant hackney cab driver, directly adapts Turgenev, whose tales, along with Tolstoy’s *Kreutzer Sonata* and Flaubert’s *Trois Contes*, she presented to the young Thomas Kilroy. Among the Russians, Lavin, like Woolf, was particularly drawn to Chekhov. She valued his objectivity, a quality she claimed for herself. ‘A writer must be as objective as a chemist,’ Chekhov said: ‘He must know that dung heaps play a very respectable part in the landscape.’

In 1937, Elizabeth Bowen declared in the *Faber Book of Modern Stories* that the genre was young, comparing it to film. But she also went back to Russia, praising Chekhov for rejecting the ‘iron relevance’ of plot to ‘let in what might appear inchoate or nebulous’. Many disapproved of his anti-teleological drift. Galsworthy said that Chekhov’s stories were ‘all middle like a tortoise’, a description that the author of ‘A Story without an End’ would have enjoyed. In ‘A Story with a Pattern’, Lavin uses a similarly explicit title. Perhaps because it draws so immediately on the critical side of her thinking, it never became fully canonical. It was not included in either the one-volume *Collected Stories* of 1971 or the three-volume *Stories* of 1964-84, but Tóibín, recognising the value of the story to new readers, gives it a central position in *An Arrow in Flight*.

‘A Story with a Pattern’ describes an author being challenged at a party by the sort of general reader who is never slow to give advice. ‘You’ve got talent!’ the Reader tells her, but her stories ‘are very thin. They have hardly any plot at all.’ Before she can protest, he gets in an anti-Chekhovian dig: ‘Your endings are very bad. They’re not endings at all. Your stories just break off in the middle!’ The Author, almost as patronisingly, replies: ‘Life itself has very little plot … Life itself has a habit of breaking off in the middle.’ Her interlocutor, however, is not without horse sense. ‘But don’t you see?’ he persists. ‘It is just because life seems vague and disorderly, because it seems purposeless and chaotic, that people turn for distraction to books!’

To prove that such patterns can be found, he relates a story told to him by his father about a friend. Murty Lockhart was born clever, but with club feet. Other children played with him, but he could never fully join in; he became withdrawn and perhaps resentful because, once he reached adulthood, he bought up property in his town and charged extortionate rents. The Reader pauses. ‘I suppose,’ he says to the Author, ‘that’s where you’d end the story if you were telling it; with Murty not knowing why he was bitter against the town and the town not knowing either! But … That’s not even the middle of it.’ As his success grew, Murty married a good-looking woman, yet his insecurities so plagued him that he could not believe he was loved for anything but his money, and then, when Ursula proved indifferent to his presents, because she was actually in love with his intellect, he despaired even of buying her. When she announced that she was pregnant he accused her of adultery and she collapsed. Murty’s punishment and the end of the tale arrive in a single blow: ‘Ursula Lockhart had died giving birth to a stillborn child – whose feet were clubbed.’

The Reader makes a hash of telling his story by failing to mention Murty’s club feet until late on and by letting slip too early that he didn’t kill himself after Ursula’s death – a refusal of closure that weakens the pattern. Lavin is showing us that the Gothic neatness which comes down from the tales of Poe, with his insistence on ‘unity or totality of effect’, belongs to the moral fable rather than the modern story. Yet the folding in of the aftermath in order to preserve the pattern is oddly compatible with Chekhov’s injunction: ‘When one has finished writing a short story one should delete the beginning and the end.’ While ‘A Story with a Pattern’ is Lavin’s answer to the charge that her work is not purposeful, she makes the Author defensive because the Reader’s objections have some force. She often found that her tendency to stasis and sprawl met with resistance. O’Connor, for example, complained: ‘Sometimes she begins her stories too far back, sometimes she carries them too far forward, rarely by more than a page or two, but already in that space the light begins to fade’.

As Gráinne Hurley has shown in a recent book, it was a problem that kept occurring when Lavin published with the *New Yorker*, which placed a premium on brevity.[\*](/the-paper/v48/n20/footnotes/zeno-s-tortoise-footnote-1) As late as 1965, ambition and pattern pulled two ways in a marvellous, entertaining story called ‘The Mock Auction’, in which the housekeeper of a farmhouse enjoys status and gifts, and does not think to ask to be paid. When the owners die, she is caught up in the machinations of rural Ireland, having to switch loyalties and improvise some attachment to a house that declines into a wreck. It is a narrative that depends on its length, and the *New Yorker*’s request that it be ‘rewritten into a tighter, more astringent story’ would have been damaging, if even possible. Hurley tells us that, as she negotiated, ‘Lavin had two other endings for the story’ and reminded her editor Rachel MacKenzie that ‘I do not believe in the completely inevitable end – or shall we say that I do not believe that a story need inevitably end at a particular place.’ One of her earlier stories, ‘The Widow’s Son’, had capitalised on this plurality by giving the reader alternative endings, like something in Borges or Calvino. ‘The Mock Auction’, however, was judged ‘more a novella than short story’ and rejected.

Lavin was not exactly hostile to pattern, but she had her own views about where it could be found. The key is less Galsworthy’s tortoise than Zeno’s, or, if you prefer, Zeno’s arrow. Both illustrate the paradox that if you split forward movement into spatial or temporal units Achilles cannot overtake the tortoise and the arrow cannot fly. The arrow is at any moment occupying a space equal to its length. In that moment without duration it cannot get beyond its length. If the arrow is motionless in every such moment it cannot move at all. The paradox relies on not asking where the arrow comes from or will land, and these were factors that Lavin discounted without dismissing. ‘I do not think a story always has to have a beginning, middle and end,’ she said. ‘To me, a story is more like an arrow in flight, or better still like a flash of forked lightning.’

Tóibín’s selection is called *An Arrow in Flight* to remind us of these remarks, but it could almost be ‘A Flash of Lightning’. First, because Lavin’s flash admits what her arrow tries to deny, that even the most amorphous story is a block of text with a start and finish. Second, because the image tells us more about her plotting: ‘The inner shape of the short story reminds me of lightning in which the zig-zagging flash seems to gather force when it goes back in order to go forward with even more deadly intent,’ Maurice Harmon recalled her saying. ‘Its course is ordained and the line of development from the beginning to its destination is a true line.’ And third, because it correlates with what Lavin said about composition. ‘Sometimes a whole story flashes into the mind like a landscape lit by lightning. But when the flash has gone, you have to recover it.’ This is where the process of drafting and redrafting came in. ‘After several revisions there is a strange feeling of recognition.’ What is important is not so much the energy of the lightning flash but better knowledge of a landscape that one has not seen lit up before.

Yet Tóibín’s title valuably combines speed with the potential for loitering. The flight of an arrow is provisional yet definitive, with the ability, in the spirit of Zeno, to suspend time and disclose a truth. ‘Short story writers try to break the continuity of life,’ Lavin said:

> I myself at times have had such a feeling of life’s forever rushing past, that I wanted in my stories to arrest that onrush and examine an incident or observe a character in the same way that a scientist examines a specimen under a microscope, when you see the many small inner patterns below the large pattern of a whole life.

The source of her arrow imagery is not in physics or metaphysics but in psychology and morality, because she believed that people do not, and usually should not, change. Her stories are for this reason richer in incident than development, and fuller still of habit. O’Connor presents situations in which a choice and transformation occur, but Lavin lets the arrow of action idle while her characters’ feelings remain the same. To call her a romantic would be inadequate to the rooted, almost blind attachment that drives so many of her narratives. It goes deeper than an aesthetic, and is more fundamental than the fidelity encouraged by the Catholic Church. Marriage, which became more highly charged for her because of widowhood, has the same persistence as family. This is one way in which her work can now seem alien and yet is the more compelling for it.

A good example is ‘The Will’, the story that confirmed Lavin in her vocation. It draws on the plight of one of her aunts, who married against her mother’s wishes. The marriage was a failure and she was cut out of her mother’s will. Lally, Lavin’s protagonist, is more fortunate, because she knows she married for love even though she and Robert struggled and had to take in lodgers. Now a widow and worn out by work, she is, of all the children, the most distressed at her mother’s death. Coming home, she asks for clues that she had been forgiven and deflects her siblings’ attempt to give her money contrary to their mother’s intentions. It becomes apparent, in any case, that this gesture is not one of pure generosity but calculated to stop Lally humiliating the family by running a lodging house. Lavin is excoriating as she peels back their layers of meanness, and Lally has the author behind her when she reflects: ‘You were yourself always, no matter where you went or what you did. You didn’t change. Her brothers and sisters were the same as they always were … There was only one thing that could change you, and that was death. And no one knew what that change would be like.’

Lally almost misses her train back to Dublin because she stops at the local priest’s house to pay for Masses for her mother. The knife of disinheritance is given another twist when the canon tells her not to worry because her mother left ‘a large sum’ to have such Masses said. Lally’s reply – ‘It’s the Masses that other people have said for you that matter’ – may not be doctrine, but it is emotionally true. ‘She died without forgiving me,’ Lally laments. ‘I’m afraid for her soul.’ On the train her distress spirals, and the burning sparks that shoot past the carriage remind her of purgatory. If she gets to Dublin before midnight she will ring the bell at the Franciscan Friary ‘and ask for a Mass to be said there and then’. With the money left over from the lodgers’ rent, she can have ten more Masses said and ‘light some holy lamps at the Convent of Perpetual Reparation’.

This is the positive side, you could say, of not changing, but Lavin is just as interested in the negative. In the grimly comic ‘At Sallygap’, Manny is married to Annie. When he was young, he planned to leave Dublin for Paris to seek his fortune as a musician. He went so far as to board the boat, before losing his nerve and returning to Annie on the quayside. His fiddle was thrown from the boat and (a little too symbolically) smashed. The marriage is miserable. Annie tries to make up in anger the passion she hoped for in bed. Manny stages a little rebellion by walking part of the way home from the hills above Dublin, where he has gone to source eggs for their shop, but then shrinks into eating his dried-out supper. Nothing will change. In ‘A Happy Death’, Ella nags Robert to give up the work that sustains him, even though sickness has degraded him from librarian to janitor. She wants to bring back the well-dressed young man with fine skin and a taste for poetry whom she remembers from her courtship. Unchanging in the face of change, she poisons the relationship until he goes into hospital, where she reanimates their lost romance with books of verse, fancy fruit and other displays. His reticent love for her outlasts all the cruelties and wells up on his deathbed to distract him from the routines of religion that she has so carefully procured. It is an ending of some complexity, but awful for the reader.

Lavin’s preoccupation with emotional tenacity had, from the outset, an effect on her technique. ‘The Green Grave and the Black Grave’ (1940) is hypnotic in its use of Irish-inflected English to bring out the devotion of an inland woman to her island husband. She gives up what should one day be her black grave in the earth to go fishing with him and share his green grave in the sea. These deaths belong to such an unrelenting series of losses that the narrative as well as its idiom put one in mind of J.M. Synge’s *Riders to the Sea*. But when Lord Dunsany, an early supporter of Lavin’s, complained that ‘it was written in imitation of Synge,’ she refuted him: ‘The repetitive style was simply an attempt to create echoes of waves breaking on the shore.’ It was, in other words, a lyrical experiment, a version of *The Waves*. The Irish storyteller whom Lavin most admired among her contemporaries, Liam O’Flaherty, was brought up on Aran and wrote in both Irish and English. He would have understood not just her incantatory style here but her focus on the elemental as an aspect of the poetic. ‘The modern short story or sketch,’ he wrote, ‘seems to have become a poem and where ideas and images attain lordship over poetry it ceases to be elemental and universal.’

Lavin was explicitly a poet at this time, and ‘Miss Holland’ was followed by a lyric whose recurring first line would eventually be inscribed on the family monument in Co. Meath:

> Let me come inland always  
> To the uneventful plains  
> Where cattle break a pathway  
> Through the slender rains.  
> Let me come inland always  
> Where beeches freely scatter  
> Green light dappled deeper  
> Where the leaves are doubled.

This is lovely but old-fashioned, as though modernism had never been. When the wind picks up, however, and Lavin describes a hedge thrashing like a ‘hawthorn galleon’, the poem takes off and the green goes into the black in a way that suggests how the short story came together.

Lavin knew​ Ireland so well because she started as an American. Born in Massachusetts, she lived there with her ill-matched Irish parents until she was ten and was taken by her mother to visit her family in Athenry in Co. Galway. After a stay of eight months, they went to live in Dublin. Despite a Catholic upbringing, taught by the nuns at Loreto College before going to University College Dublin, she had a margin of detachment that went with her American citizenship, which she kept until her seventieth year.

Going to Athenry as a child showed her Irish society in ways that wouldn’t have been possible if she had been brought up in the town. ‘For all I know,’ she once said, ‘it was the shock to eye and ear that made me a writer.’ The mean-spirited provincialism, the priest-ridden respectability and lack of imagination are shrewdly represented in her work, but with tolerance as well as satire. Before she worked on Woolf, she wrote a thesis on Jane Austen, so she was ready to make sense of a complacent social order. What she never loses sight of is the cost to those who are excluded. In ‘Sunday Brings Sunday’, parents, employers and the Church conspire to leave a servant girl so ignorant about sex that she gets pregnant without knowing how it happened. Next stop for her, a Magdalene laundry. Sarah, in the story named after her, has children by a series of men without becoming ostracised, partly because she lives with her brothers – or, in an earlier version, since Lavin revised her stories even after she published them, her uncles – and partly because she is a regular at Mass. Eventually, the wife of one of her lovers complains and she is kicked out by her family and left to die with her baby. There is little in Irish writing as powerful as these denunciations of patriarchal abuse.

Lavin’s mother was one of the uptight Mahons of Athenry even when living in Dublin. Tom Lavin, her father, was more interesting. Brought up poor in Roscommon, he left school barely literate but had a way with people and horses and did well in America. Winning the trust of a wealthy family, he persuaded them to invest across the Atlantic and buy the grand Bective House in Co. Meath, which he managed on their behalf. It was here, with her father, that Lavin spent the weekends. They were not gentry, but they had the trappings. Many Lavin stories, from ‘Miss Holland’ onwards, testify to the complexity of father-daughter relationships, and the way women could be left behind as a consequence of their loyalty. Equally telling are the tales in which servants who run big houses take on airs and graces, put on other people’s clothes and do not own themselves.

After her father’s death, Lavin became something like the heir of Bective House. Her husband took up the administration of the estate and they lived in one wing. Even when photographed in tweeds outside the house, however, they could not belong to it in the same way that Bowen did to Bowen’s Court, or as the Naylor family belongs to Danielstown in *The Last September* (1929). This starts with a celebrated passage which funnels the countryside into the estate: ‘About six o’clock the sound of a motor, collected out of the wide country and narrowed under the trees of the avenue, brought the household out in excitement on to the steps. Up among the beeches, a thin iron gate twanged; the car slid out from a net of shadow, down the slope to the house.’ There is visibility and audibility that becomes faintly ominous in the narrowing of the drive, the net and the thinness of the gate. As the Anglo-Irish War advances, Danielstown shrinks into the ‘rug’ of its ‘demesne trees’ planted on Irish land. ‘The house seemed to be pressing down low in apprehension … to gather its trees close in fright and amazement at the wide, light, lovely unloving country, the unwilling bosom whereon it was set.’

At the end of *The Last September*, three big houses are set ablaze. The prose remains commanding though elegiac: ‘At Danielstown, halfway up the avenue under the beeches, the thin iron gate twanged … as the last unlit car slid out with the executioners bland from accomplished duty.’ Bowen, with more manipulation than Lavin would allow, takes us back to the start of the novel and reverses the relationship between house and countryside: ‘The sound of the last car widened, gave itself to the open and empty country and was demolished. Then the first wave of a silence that was to be ultimate flowed back, confident, to the steps. Above the steps, the door stood open hospitably upon a furnace.’

The obvious contrast is with Lavin’s ‘The Joy-Ride’ (1946). In this story, the Manse is not lived in by its owner but left in the hands of a couple of disaffected, inept butlers by an estate manager who drives off to the Galway Races – exactly the sort of destination that would attract the racehorse-managing Tom Lavin. Opening the shutters, as instructed, but then smartly closing them, Crickem and Purdy hitch up one of the hunters to a trap, raid the pantry and head out for the day, but not before rootling in the cellars by the feeble light of matches to steal three bottles of curaçao. Their jaunt is fruitless. They fail to pick up any women, despite some flaunting by lasses with ‘bright bold eyes’, the curaçao doesn’t agree with them and, as they ride home, they see to their alarm smoke rising from the direction of the Manse. Recriminations begin. Crickem accuses Purdy of not stamping out the matches as they burned low in the cellar. It comes as a huge relief, when they stop off at a pub that’s doing a roaring trade because of people coming to see the fire, that it’s a big house called Liscard which is alight. But this is another of Lavin’s false, or incremental, endings, because, as at the end of *The Last September*, more than one house has been affected. The pub customers are preoccupied with the fire at Liscard, but the Manse burned down first and spread its sparks.

As usual in Lavin, the psychology of the butlers, with their dim-witted mutual aggravation, matters more to the story than the large-scale politics, which boil down to the point that, like other big houses of the mid-century, the Manse is more a victim of neglect and underinvestment than of the active hostility that caused Danielstown to burn down. Unable to maintain her own house, Bowen sold it in 1959, and it was promptly demolished. You would have to over-read words such as ‘richest’ and ‘shadowed’ in Lavin’s account of the butlers’ joyride to find any Bowen-like conflict in the landscape:

> They took the Slane road, that ran through the richest deepest part of Meath, and soon they were going uphill and down, the countryside hidden from them at one moment as they dipped into a small valley between high ditches and revealed at the next moment when there was a rise in the road, or when they rolled up over one of the many railway bridges that rose up like hoops in the flat parts of the land to give a magnificent view across the entire county, with its plains diversified by pasture and grain, and shadowed by the woodlands of its great demesnes.

The butlers are kept going as a grammatical subject by dribs and drabs of ‘they’, but the real subject is the countryside that crowds and stretches the eye and dominates the syntax.

This reflects Lavin’s cultural priorities, which were very different from Bowen’s, but also her experience. In 1946 she bought land near Bective House and built a house. There’s a revealing account of the location of Abbey Farm at the start of ‘A Likely Story’, an adaptation of a folk tale in which a boy is kidnapped by the fairies but is kept safe by a thorn stuck in his finger. When Lavin sets the scene it is hard to know whether the landscape feels magical in anticipation of the story or whether the magic of the story was prompted by where she lived:

> Do you know Bective? Like a bird in the nest, it presses close to the soft green mound of the riverbank, its handful of houses no more significant by day than the sheep that dot the far fields. But at night, when all its little lamps are lit, house by house, it is marked out on the hillside as clearly as the Great Bear is marked out in the sky. And on a still night it throws its shape in glitter on the water.

Unlike Danielstown or Bowen’s Court, this is an anciently peopled place, where little lamps glitter companionably among the little people.

Descriptions of Abbey Farm are sometimes darker. ‘In the Middle of the Fields’, published in the *New Yorker* in 1961, puts the widow Vera on a footing that is endangered by the fecundity of the pastures: ‘Like a rock in the sea, she was islanded by fields, the heavy grass washing about the house.’ Remoteness gives her security, living alone as she does with her children, but she is dependent on male neighbours for help. One day Bartley Crossen agrees to cut the ‘ugly tufts of tow and scutch that whitened the tops of the grass’, but he comes back in the evening to reschedule the work. Not just that: ‘He leaned forward. “What about a little kiss?”’ The story does not turn out as we might expect. Crossen, who is a married, well-respected man, is mortified by his own behaviour, and she gets them both off the hook by saying, and maybe believing, that he had been incited by the same sense of loss that afflicts her. To see her come down the stairs with her hair loose before bed stirred memories of his first wife, who died young.

The several​ Vera stories were written in the 1960s when Lavin was living much of the time in Dublin, and they move on the axis familiar to her, between Co. Meath and the capital. This was not the limit of her world. She took her children on European holidays in a sports car, flying a tricolour. She was lionised in America. She had flair and independence. Yet the emotional geography of bereavement was only too familiar. After losing her first husband, Lavin spent time in a psychiatric hospital. She knew the intensity of Vera’s feelings from within, and although she found this group of stories difficult to work on, preferring the ease of writing about things that drew in less of her, from a priest going slightly bonkers to the building of a sandcastle, they are among her best works.

Tóibín has been an advocate of Lavin’s work for decades and his choice of stories for this selection is admirable. *An Arrow in Flight* begins with ‘Trastevere’, a *New Yorker* story from 1971, in the latter part of Lavin’s career. Set in Rome and Manhattan, it deflects any charge of narrow Irishness. Yet he also includes ‘The Great Wave’, a story about a natural disaster and a bishop in a coracle that Banville has called ‘Oirish as Oirish could be’.

The major omission is the lack of any of the late work published in *The Shrine* (1977) and *A Family Likeness* (1985). One or two of these stories suffer from an over-extension but they have a lot of experience behind them, digested, as Lavin prescribed, into wisdom. They deal with everyday indignities: the way it feels to become odd and dependent as you age, and to turn into your mother. If there is autofiction in them, it is distanced by a character, Ada, who, as Lavin exactingly said of Vera, ‘was not me, but who was whom I was writing about through knowledge of myself’. At around the same time, pushing sixty, Lavin said: ‘You do your best work as you grow older.’ That would be too large a claim to make for the late stories, but they deserve attention. Tóibín’s selection will win Lavin new readers; an edited complete works is needed next.
