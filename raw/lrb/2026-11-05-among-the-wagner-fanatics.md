# 正文

Of all​ Wagner’s operas, *Lohengrin* is the most swan-concerned. It’s true that *Parsifal* has a narratively propulsive swan-shooting incident in Act 1, which is a great relief for audience members feeling desperate about the lack of action up to this point. (Everyone on stage has been standing still and shout-singing about the king’s bath for 45 minutes.) It is in *Lohengrin*, however, that swans actually matter. A mysterious knight arrives on a boat drawn by a swan to rescue Elsa, who has been falsely accused of murdering her brother, Gottfried. The knight marries her on the condition that Elsa never asks his name. Inevitably, she ends up doing so, at which point he reveals himself as Lohengrin, a knight of the Grail, and departs – but not before turning the magical swan back into Elsa’s lost brother.

It isn’t necessary to care about any of this to have a nice time at the Lohengrin thermal baths, a short drive from Bayreuth, the Bavarian town where the Wagner festival has been held almost every summer for 150 years. The baths have indoor and outdoor pools of various therapeutic temperatures, seven *textilfrei* saunas and expansive shady lawns where visitors can sprawl out on their swan-embroidered complimentary towels.

You can’t identify a Wagner enthusiast just by looking at someone, especially if they are in swimwear. As I studied the other guests at the baths, it seemed possible that none of them had ever thought about Wagner, that they were able to order a Lohengrinsalat at the adjoining restaurant, Bistro Elsa, without Wagner crossing their minds. It seemed unlikely, though. To be in or even near Bayreuth is to have Wagner drilled into your consciousness. The festival lasts for a month, consisting exclusively of repeat performances of his operas, and everything in the town is oriented towards him. Along with all the swans, Wagner’s face is everywhere, on busts and billboards and flyers for hair salons, staring down from ads for Pilates studios with that off-puttingly candid look in his eye and the trace of hysteria about his pressed lips. Hotels and bars and pharmacies are named after his works; the gift shops sell cookie cutters in the shape of his profile and postcards that say CHE GUEWAGNER. His silhouette is on the traffic lights near the festival theatre, a little green man with a baton and a beret bearing up valiantly under the weight of the famously enormous head. The only other place I have visited with as dominant a presiding spirit is Disneyland, though even that doesn’t fully capture it. Imagine if Disneyland had been built by Mickey Mouse himself, as a monument to his own maniacal, epoch-defining genius. That’s Bayreuth.

To get to the baths from our Airbnb on Sentaweg (heroine of *Der fliegende Holländer*), we drove down Rienzistrasse (named for Hitler’s favourite opera, the autograph score of which he had with him in the bunker), and then Tannhäuserstrasse, before briefly losing our bearings somewhere beyond Siegfried-Wagner-Allee, where a young blonde woman floated across the street into the arms of her boyfriend. Her long hair streamed behind her, and though I hadn’t yet been in Bayreuth for 24 hours, the word that came to mind as I watched her was ‘Rhinemaiden’. Even the fields of corn on the outskirts of town had started to look operatic, rolling beneath the brassy Bavarian sunlight.

I am married to a man who loves Wagner. He can’t listen to *Tristan und Isolde* for longer than a minute without closing his eyes and tilting his face towards heaven. I don’t have the same sort of nerve endings. Listening to Wagner can sometimes produce a jolt of euphoria, or make me cry. At other times, it makes me feel the same as when someone asks me to watch a video on their phone: put upon and worried about getting bored. ‘The Ride of the Valkyries’ makes me think about the Vietnam War. The goosebumps I get from the ‘Pilgrims’ Chorus’ don’t prevent me from reflecting on how lovable it is when Bugs Bunny rides a fat little horse while dressed up as Brünnhilde in ‘What’s Opera, Doc?’, the theme from *Tannhäuser* blaring as he descends the hill. His rabbit feet, side-saddle! Until very recently, it had never occurred to me to go to Bayreuth. If someone had asked, I’d probably have said I wasn’t allowed.

From its inception, in 1876, the festival has cultivated a mystique that suggests it is not a place that welcomes the casual Wagner listener. Everything about it, in fact, seems intended to call into question the existence of the casual Wagner listener. The festival theatre was built for the premiere of the *Ring*, the culmination of Wagner’s long-held dream of having his own opera house for the annual staging of his works. He chose Bayreuth because it was unfashionable and hard to get to: he wanted a location where nothing could distract visitors from total immersion in his music. The idea was that even turning up amounted to a proclamation of devotion. It’s not meant to be fun. On visiting the Sistine Chapel, Wagner is supposed to have looked up and declared: ‘It’s as in my theatre. You understand that there’s nothing to joke about.’

Most descriptions of the festival contain the word ‘shrine’ or ‘pilgrimage’ and proceed on the understanding that what happens in Bavaria every year is something other than a commercial enterprise or social occasion. The word ‘cult’ is generally applied only when discussing Wagner and the Nazis, but the concept of initiation rites is widely invoked. To attend, we are told, is a privilege granted only to those who have spent years hunched over studying the composer’s work, rotating that giant head of his in their minds. In *Bayreuth: A History of the Wagner Festival* (1994) Frederic Spotts notes that conductors and singers often feel ‘intimidated by the knowledge that a Bayreuth audience can be counted on to know the scores so well that the slightest error is bound to be detected’.

Until recently, getting a ticket was a great ordeal. Applicants were required to submit written requests, accompanied by evidence of their interest in Wagner, simply in order to secure a spot on the decade-long waiting list. Membership of a Wagner society helped boost a petitioner’s chances, but nothing was guaranteed. Once the insanely expensive set of *Ring* tickets was somehow in hand, the next set of hardships would present themselves: the lack of surtitles, the absence of air-conditioning, the competitively attentive nature of an audience of Wagner fanatics ready to reprimand the daylights out of anyone caught squirming or chatting. The seats, the seats, the celebrated hardness of the seats, which are spoken of as being uniquely hostile to the human spine, as categorically different from normal wooden chairs as Bayreuth is from a normal opera festival.

In the past few years, however, Bayreuth’s mystique has taken a bit of a hit. A significant portion of the tickets are now available for purchase online, on a first-come, first-served basis, taking no account of credited time on waiting lists or years spent elbowing one’s way up the ranks of the Wagner Society of Northern California. Anyone can buy them, as long as they make it into the online waiting room on the dot – as our friends did. It seemed pathetic, life-denying, to turn down the seats that were offered, like turning down an opportunity to see the Earth from space or sit in on a meeting of the Skull and Bones society. No alarm sounded underneath the festival theatre when I said yes.

The theatre stands in a park on a hill. It hasn’t changed much since Wagner last saw it in 1882. Early attendees complained that it wasn’t sufficiently grand or outwardly embellished – Colette compared it to a gasometer – but it’s imposing enough, hulking there at the top of the hill, at the end of a tree-lined avenue named for Wagner’s son.

I got a proper look at it for the first time on our way to a performance of *Der fliegende Holländer*. It was a nonsensically hot afternoon (the heat is another thing that people tend to emphasise when reflecting fondly on Bayreuth’s endless miseries) and all around us were profusely sweating people in formal attire, speaking to one another in hushed, anxious voices. The young men, of whom there were a surprising number, looked especially worried. Many had scores under their arms. A fragile little girl in white lace, her hair tied in a bow, walked hand in hand with her grandmother. Representatives of ancient archetypes, previously unknown to me, strolled past: the very tall, very elderly man with white hair scrolling down to his waist; the red-haired, red-dressed woman kneeling down to tie and buff her husband’s shoes; the constantly smiling barefoot youth in a bow tie. It was easy to imagine that nothing meaningful had changed over the past 150 years, and that we were all still performing the parts Wagner had written for his ideal audience.

This fantasy began to dissolve on encountering the *Silenced Voices* exhibition, about a third of the way up the hill. It relays the histories of Jewish musicians who performed at Bayreuth before being excluded, exiled or sent to the camps. It draws a straight line between Wagner’s antisemitism and the rise of the Nazis, going into particular detail about the decisions made by his despicable wife, Cosima, who took over management of the festival after his death and worked to transform it into a fortress of German nationalism, proscribing every form of ‘Israelite impurity’.

Would a committed antisemite have signed off on an exhibition that condemned his family for making their disgusting bigotries the basis of their managerial policy? Thinking for too long about what Wagner would or would not have approved of is a surefire way to go crazy, but I felt confident that it wouldn’t have been his sort of thing. He would probably have liked the current set-up at Wahnfried, the house that he built for himself in the middle of town. He didn’t live there for all that long, spending increasing amounts of time in Venice before dying of heart failure in 1883, at the height of his fame. It was after his death that Wahnfried came into its own as a factory for the production of the Wagner myth, overseen first by Cosima and then by Winifred, his English-born daughter-in-law. The house is now a museum, with a garden and a café serving Rheingoldspritz. Visitors can walk through strange reconstructions of the rooms, mostly empty of furniture, and inspect Wagner’s personal possessions: his piano, his spectacles, his berets and boots. On the second floor is the actual Sterbesofa, the one he died on in Venice. It looks too short for a man to have a heart attack on, but as I learned elsewhere in the museum, Wagner was exactly the same height as me: 165 cm. If I were in a palazzo on the Grand Canal, having fled Bayreuth because of delicate health and an inability to tolerate the rattling of carriage wheels, I could have lain down and died on that sofa with room to spare.

For anyone unwilling or unable to travel to Bayreuth to experience the peculiar atmosphere of Wahnfried for themselves, there is a moment in Hans-Jürgen Syberberg’s *The Confessions of Winifred Wagner* (1975), filmed at the house, that gives some indication of what it is like. Winifred, then 78 and in hideously boisterous form, is reminiscing with great clarity and warmth about Hitler’s daily routine when he stayed with the Wagners during his annual trip to Bayreuth for the festival, ‘the only holiday he had all year’. We learn that Hitler woke up late every day, took long walks in the garden and obliged the Wagner children to stay up with him until 4 a.m. every night. She tells us that he often brought Goebbels and Speer with him. Winifred then hitches up her top lip to bare her big yellow teeth in a wheezing laugh and confides to the camera that although Hitler was a vegetarian, she and her family were sometimes able to tempt him with a plate of liver dumplings. That’s Wahnfried.

*Der fliegende Holländer* has a running time of two and a half hours. No interval, no halfway Holländerspritz. Two and a half hours is not a long time in the grand scheme of a life, or really even in the grand scheme of a single repulsively hot day. This is what I told myself as we took our seats in the darkened theatre, apologising over and over again to everyone who had already sat down, and who turned their stony faces upwards as we passed, tilting their chins in a way that simultaneously acknowledged the apology and rejected it. But it is sufficient time to develop a precise awareness of how much swivelling of the head and crossing of the legs will be tolerated before the human owl sitting twenty metres away turns the flare of his gaze in your direction. It is long enough to understand that the luminous, shimmering quality of the theatre’s acoustics, the way sound pours over and through the theatre, is not a feature detectable only by experts. The acoustics make the music seem like something miraculously revealed rather than created by the orchestra. Any idiot can appreciate this, even someone whose presence at Bayreuth amounts to a sort of practical joke she is playing on herself.

It’s enough time to remember that *Der fliegende Holländer* is more or less about a ship, but not long enough to accept that this particular production has no ship in it – you keep waiting for the moment when the tattered sails heave into view. They never do. Two and a half hours does, however, easily accommodate several interludes of putting your sunglasses on and taking them off again, just to feel something – as well as a protracted period during which you can sit there and blow air out of your nose as hard as you can, without making a noise, to see if you can ruffle the wispy ringlets of the man sitting in front of you (impossible). At least three people in your immediate vicinity can faint in this span of time, and get whisked swiftly and unobtrusively out of the theatre while everyone else in the audience keeps their eyes pinned to the stage, even when it seems briefly that one of the fainting people may have died, having let out a sharp yelping noise before going scarily limp.

In his account of going to Bayreuth in 1891, Mark Twain writes that ‘this audience reminds me of nothing I have ever seen and of nothing I have read about except the city in the Arabian tale where all the inhabitants have been turned to brass and the traveller finds them after centuries mute, motionless and still retaining the attitudes which they last knew in life.’ The description could apply today – the quality of the audience’s attention was as noticeable, and as singular, as the acoustics. This is not to say that they didn’t start falling asleep in large numbers all around us almost immediately after the lights went down. But even the sleeping was awe-inspiring in its way, being accomplished with no adjustment of posture whatsoever. People just put their hands on their knees, closed their eyes and went totally still, the way you would if challenged to emulate a stone lion.

The mood in the theatre is genuinely daunting, so much so that I ended up feeling intimidated by my own husband, who sat ramrod-straight and apparently riveted throughout the performance, face flickering with insight, understanding, sympathy. As the curtains closed and the applause began, he turned to me, and I braced myself for whatever perceptive observation was coming. ‘Did you see,’ he shouted above the drumming of the feet, ‘how that guy’s son fell asleep on his shoulder? The guy behind us.’ ‘That wasn’t his son,’ I bawled into his ear. ‘It was his BOYISH WIFE.’ ‘That’s amazing,’ he screamed, only barely audible above the whistles.

One thing about being bored to a point beyond tears is that it throws everything that comes afterwards into joyful, delirious relief. To walk out into the foetid evening, taking care not to trample on the train of someone’s ice-blue gown. To throw back bottle after small bottle of fizzy water that costs something like €15 a pop in an ultimately ineffective attempt to replenish lost electrolytes. To stand beneath the silvery moon with your friends and eat a delicious pretzel in the shape of a treble clef, Holländerspritz in hand.

In his history, Spotts asks: ‘Who would go to the inconvenience of travelling to an out-of-the-way place to endure the discomfort of sitting on a hard seat for hour after hour in an auditorium without air-conditioning at the height of summer except out of deep devotion to Wagner’s works?’ The answer, as Spotts himself demonstrates at length, has always been ‘loads of people’. There have always been gawkers, and those who are there because they want to prove to themselves that they are as capable as anyone else of submitting to a bizarre and gruelling cultural ritual, and people who won’t be shushed, and who sprint out after performances to bag spots under the trees where they and their friends can scream with laughter and show one another their wasp stings.

Alban Berg moaned about this in 1909: ‘Left and right of the Festspielhaus a Festspiel beerhouse and a Festspiel restaurant. I arrived at the theatre in devout mood to find the entire audience … disporting themselves in those places. People strolled around laughing and chattering, feeling they simply must have a drink.’ He complained too about Siegfried Wagner, the Master’s son, fending off autograph hunters while chomping his way through a ‘Parsifal steak’. The French musicologist Albert Lavignac, who admittedly doesn’t seem like the most stable person, expressed his disappointment in 1896 that it was ‘impossible to make spectators pass an examination before permitting them to enter the hall’. The festival had become too fashionable, he wrote, attended by rich women who didn’t know how to pronounce Siegfried properly. In his dispatch from the very first festival in 1876, Tchaikovsky spent as much time discussing the food and criticising the ‘horde of false admirers’ as he did discussing the music. It’s not true that Bayreuth is a fanatics-only zone. It never has been.
