# 正文

It is somewhat​ intimidating to write about a subject you are just beginning to explore – in this case, Stoicism. But I brought it on myself. I chose the subject of Stoic philosophy because I was newly engrossed in it and did not want to turn my mind to other matters. So this is my own doing. But please forgive any lacunae.

I was, early on, asked by a friend how I came to be interested in the Stoics. I wouldn’t tell her, except to say, ‘by chance’. I wanted to try to write the answer here, and if you tell someone something ahead of writing it, that may take away all your desire to write it. My interest was sparked by chance, but there were probably several reasons for my responding to that chance.

Before I explain, I would first like to describe my reaction to [an essay in the *LRB* of 22 January 2026](/the-paper/v48/n01/geoff-mann/good-failures)*,* not because it awakened my interest in the Stoic philosophers, but because reading it revealed to me the way two of my assumptions have changed over time, especially under the influence of my reading of the Stoics.

I was attracted by a review discussing two books, *Everything Must Go: Why We Are Obsessed with the End of the World* by Dorian Lynskey and *Hopeful Pessimism* by Mara van der Lugt. I am one of those who believe we are facing the end of the world – the end of the world as we know it right now, and the end of the world I knew in my early childhood in the 1950s, a world which at that time seemed to me, safely enclosed as I was in a peaceful college town, living on a quiet, leafy street of houses in a neighbourhood where I roamed freely, returning to a yellow house harbouring an intact family, with enough to eat, and unaware of my parents’ financial and political worries, an unchangeably safe world. (I mention this because it is so often what we grow up knowing that conditions our expectations of life.) I am not *obsessed* with the end of the world, but I now act within that framework, within the assumption that the world is sliding into catastrophe. As for ‘hopeful pessimism’, I’m afraid that pessimism generally outweighs my very conditional modicum of hope, though I respect those who are hopeful and hope they are right.

I found, as I read the essay, which opened intriguingly and relevantly by describing the plot of a Georges Perec novel, that I disagreed strongly at a couple of points with the perspective of its author, Geoff Mann, and, further, with his assumption that his readers would share his perspective. And this surprised me, because it revealed to me that my thinking had really, measurably, shifted from what I take to be some common assumptions.

Mann’s first assumption is revealed by his posing the problem of ‘what kind of meaning life has in a future with nobody in it’, no one ‘to know, let alone care, that we ever existed’. First I baulked at ‘nobody in it’, because that word ‘nobody’ assumes that only humans have any real presence, any value as survivors; it assumes an anthropocentric viewpoint, which I no longer have, though it is probably true that any remaining forms of life will not care that we ever existed.

Maybe we humans will be gone. But there are many other species, and each one, like us, necessarily sits at the centre of its own existence. I do question in what sense we are better than other species, and more worth saving*.*

Early in my reading life, the poems and especially the journals of Gerard Manley Hopkins, including his drawings, and Thoreau’s observations from Walden Pond, and, later, Annie Dillard’s reports from Tinker Creek, showed me how close attention could reveal the most minute details of natural life, drawing one into profound sympathy with those forms of life. Then, years later, my unthinkingly anthropocentric position was jolted, though not yet changed, on the last page of a piece of fiction I can’t identify now, about a widespread plague. The book ends within the viewpoint of the victorious bacterium, utterly indifferent to the absence of humans. More recently, Ed Yong’s *An Immense World* (2022) has tabulated, in almost overwhelming abundance, the specialised sensitivities and skills of even the smallest species.

This growing respect for the natural world, combined with the Stoic philosophers’ repeated emphasis on our own smallness and the brevity of our lives, mere dots in eternity, has led me to entertain the idea that if humanity goes, while other species remain, the earth will almost surely be better off, in its very, very slow recovery, given our fatal tendency to meddle, usually for our own convenience, our own pleasure or our own imagined prosperity.

The second point in the essay at which I baulked was this. Mann asks, concerning the prospect that the end of the world is near: ‘How should we live now if that is a real possibility?’ and goes on to discuss the range of possible responses to the different forms of apocalypse presented in Lynskey’s book. Perhaps Mann left out some, but his list reads: ‘Abandon all hope and commit suicide; descend into unbridled brutality; rush to avert approaching doom; party till the last minute then cosy up with a glass of wine to watch the meteor come in.’

I could see the likelihood of all these possible responses. But what I was waiting for and missed was the one that would be most useful, I thought, which was the Stoic philosopher’s response. I’m guessing that it would involve understanding, resignation, acceptance and, especially, the recognition that our lives are very brief in any case and that we can’t control anything outside ourselves, only our own minds and our own responses. Stoicism seemed to me to be a philosophy that could accommodate not only one’s fear of death but even one’s dread of the apocalypse.

I’m not happy that humanity may disappear, and I’m not really comforted by the idea that other species may survive. But I like to exercise my mind with the thought that things might be all right, in the end, in the far, far end, for other species, if not for us.

The intellectual stimulation of wrestling with such questions – using and developing what the Stoics call my inborn gift of reason to test out what they propose, including some ideas against which my emotions immediately rebel – is one thing that has kept me engrossed in my reading. I believe the Stoics when they say that by developing our reason, we may conquer pain, grief, superstition and the fear of death. They do also say it isn’t easy.

As I told my friend, I was led to Stoic philosophy by chance. Faced with a tedious, repetitive task, I decided to listen to something while I carried it out. I looked through a few options and chose a *Hidden Brain* podcast on which the philosopher and former evolutionary biologist Massimo Pigliucci appeared, talking about Stoicism and describing the way he had been helped even in everyday misadventure by the teachings of the Stoics. I may have been primed to select that particular podcast by at least three things: my growing interest in the Romans, fed by having lived briefly among the ruins of a Roman settlement in Gaul; my habit since adolescence of making regular, though only partly successful, attempts at self-improvement; and my more urgent, impatient need, after the latest deaths in my family, finally to accept the idea of death with more equanimity.

Pigliucci’s description of his experience of Stoicism was both matter-of-fact and inspiring. After the task was finished and the podcast over, I wondered if I had any books in the house on which to feed my newly aroused curiosity. Since the Stoics believed that perfection as a Stoic philosopher was achieved by conquering one’s fear of death, I thought it quite possible that slowly reading the Stoics over a few months might help me to face not only those deaths that had already occurred, but also the looming, inevitable prospect of more deaths to come, though I doubted I could manage the kind of *welcome* the Stoics would prefer – welcoming adversity as an opportunity to become stronger in character.

The only book I found upstairs had belonged to my father. It was a small, old paperback with yellowed pages called *Essential Works of Stoicism* – a selection edited by the classics scholar and teacher Moses Hadas. Included in this selection was the whole of Marcus Aurelius’s *To Himself,* as Hadas calls it, per the original Greek, but more commonly known as *Meditations*. These meditations were written down by Marcus in diary form, solely for his own benefit, apparently, not to instruct students or fellow philosophers. For this reason, I believed that I would be encountering his thoughts in a more immediate and intimate form, unedited and closer to the source. I would, in a sense, be alone with him as he exhorted himself to overcome his faults and work towards his goal of becoming more virtuous – most likely in a tent, on a battlefield, somewhere near the Danube River, where his armies were fighting Germanic tribes. The meditations probably date from the last ten years of his life, 170-180 CE.

The first pages of Hadas’s introduction, which fell out as I turned them, were vehemently annotated in heavy pencil by my father, who had a habit of reading the beginnings of books and responding thus to their ideas, not always continuing to read the book to the end – though of course one can be greatly challenged in one’s thinking by even the beginning of a book. From the marks he made, I gathered that he was as interested as I now was, for instance, in the Greek word *ataraxia*, new to me, which Hadas defines as ‘unruffledness’ and which is elsewhere defined as freedom from emotional turmoil, a desirable state of being at the core of the Stoic ideal.

My plan was to read Marcus and ponder what he said, as he read and pondered his own sources. I imagined him writing alone in his tent, in the evening or morning, though some entries in the *Meditations* imply the occasional intrusion of perhaps irritating people. He wrote, not in his native Latin, but in Greek, the preferred language for expressing philosophical ideas. (I eventually collected four different translations of the *Meditations*, including the first into English, by Meric Casaubon, done in the 17th century, and saw that what seemed to be crucial words were translated quite differently from one book to another, but I know no Greek, so I could not try to decipher the original. The quotations I give here are from Robin Hard’s translation.)

A notebook of persuasive arguments directed to himself, *Meditations* was evidently never meant for publication. As in most private notebooks, each entry, arising spontaneously, inspired by something outside the notebook not known to us and coloured by passing moods, does not always follow from the entry that went before, and so our interest is stimulated by not knowing quite what is coming next. One entry may be dry and brief, such as, for instance, ‘If something is not right, do not do it: if something is not true, do not say it,’ whereas another is expansive and even playful in tone, as is this one, about finding it hard to get out of bed. I will quote most of it:

> Early in the morning, when you find it so hard to rouse yourself from your sleep, have these thoughts ready at hand: ‘I am rising to do the work of a human being. Why, then, am I so irritable if I am going out to do what I was born to do and what I was brought into this world for? Or was I created for this, to lie in bed and warm myself under the bedclothes?’ ‘Well, it is certainly more pleasant.’ ‘So were you born for pleasure, or, in general, for feeling, or for action? Do you not see how the little plants, the little birds, the ants, the spiders, the bees, each do their own work and play their part in the proper running of the universe? And will you, then, for your part, refuse to do the work of a human being? Will you not hasten to do what your nature requires of you?’ – ‘Yes, but one needs one’s rest too.’ – Quite so, but nature has set limits on that, as she has on eating and drinking, and yet you are going beyond those limits, and beyond what is sufficient.

Also vivid were Marcus’s close analyses, the anatomist’s eye with which he stripped away cultural adornments and saw into the essences of things: a nicely basted piece of roast pork is merely a dead pig, he says; a high-status item such as a cloak of Tyrian purple is merely sheep’s wool dipped in the ‘blood’ of shellfish; a human being is ‘today a drop of mucus, and tomorrow a mummy or a pile of ash’. The drop of mucus being the drop of semen from which we all began. In Marcus’s graphic terms, we begin as the drop of semen entering the ovary and we end as a body lying in the earth or ashes in a jar.

He was a perpetual student, to judge from his correspondence with Marcus Cornelius Fronto, an early tutor of his – one of a possible total of eighteen – and a lifelong friend. A letter he wrote to Fronto when he was in his twenties, giving an account of how he spent the day, begins: ‘I studied from three in the morning until eight, eating my meal bit by bit; and from eight to nine I walked up and down very contentedly in front of my bedroom in my sandals.’ Twenty or so years later, Fronto was still suggesting exercises and recommending essays and books. We benefit from Marcus’s dedication to study; when we read his thoughts, they come from a mind already intensely schooled in the ideas of Epictetus and many others.

He sometimes took a book with him to read during a banquet or a spectacle such as a gladiator fight, for which people resented him, feeling he held himself to be superior to the entertainment – as he certainly did.

Besides being a scholar, a philosopher, eventually a military general and one of Rome’s five ‘good emperors’, Marcus was a father and a husband, speaking of his wife with affection and describing his children lovingly in letters to Fronto. The many entries on death in the *Meditations* must in part have been responses to the deaths of his own children, only six of whom, out of thirteen, survived.

Moses Hadas was the father of one of my best childhood friends, Rachel Hadas, who grew up to be a poet. I wrote to Rachel about finding her father’s book and about my new interest. She told me she loved the Stoics – something you don’t often hear a friend say. Then, generously, quite in keeping with one of their precepts, she ordered for me Ward Farnsworth’s *The Practising Stoic* (2018). Seneca wrote: ‘Our common life is founded on kindness and harmony; it is bound in a compact of mutual assistance, not by fear, but by love of one another.’

Over last winter and spring, I read Farnsworth’s book, as a sort of primer, alongside Marcus Aurelius’s *Meditations* in a newer edition, the Hard translation. Farnsworth presents in greater depth just a few of the Stoics – Seneca the Younger, Epictetus and Marcus Aurelius – while also bringing into the discussion Epicurus, Cicero and Plutarch, as well as a selection of thinkers in succeeding centuries who were influenced by the Stoics, including Montaigne, Adam Smith and Samuel Johnson, so that by the end of the book I had come to feel surrounded by a varied and deeply engaged cohort. While distinguishing between the Stoics he presents, Farnsworth concentrates on their overlaps and similarities, and I, in turn, took from those shared beliefs what was helpful to me.

Farnsworth acted as guide while I absorbed the principles of something new to me. By providing context for his many quotations, he resolved mysteries, clarified references, explained anecdotes and people: one Stoic compares the pointlessness of an overly prolonged life to a certain overly long book, and Farnsworth explains that the book is the *Annals* of the tedious historian Tanusius Geminus; the exemplary hog on shipboard, untroubled, as we ought to be untroubled, by a violent storm, was witnessed by Pyrrho the philosopher and the story repeated by Montaigne. Out of Farnsworth’s generous sample of philosophers, I might have chosen Epictetus to read at greater length, or Seneca, and probably still will – although Macaulay compared any continuous reading of Seneca to dining on nothing but anchovy sauce.

The Stoics​ address the same emotional and ethical problems we face now, though the trappings of their lives were different and therefore the metaphors they resort to are sometimes exotic to us. (Plutarch with his ‘wine-jars of good and evil … stored up in our spirits’; Seneca comparing our life as it elapses to a water-clock coming to its last drop.) Farnsworth’s chapter headings show the range of their concerns, among them judgment, perspective, desire, wealth and pleasure, emotion and what others think. I am interested in all of these subjects, but here I’m choosing to concentrate on the problems we face in our day-to-day lives and on how we contend with the problem of adversity, mainly death.

But first, a clarification. Our common use of the adjective ‘stoical’ has little to do with the vividness, nuance and flexibility of the many and varied articulations of the philosophy of Stoicism, or the sort of vulnerable, imperfect, patiently evolving practice I witnessed in reading Marcus Aurelius. The philosophy is not an abstract one: it is meant to be an approach to daily life. The precepts seemed to me almost all useful and sensible: ‘finish what you start,’ for example, is wonderfully effective applied to a small thing, like a household task, or to a larger thing, striving to live every day as though it were your last. As Seneca says, ‘Let us order our minds as if we had come to the end. Let us postpone nothing. Let us balance life’s account every day.’

The Stoics explicitly did not expect anyone to gain perfection. If the attainment of virtue – by which they meant wisdom, courage, justice and temperance – ultimately promised peace of mind and happiness, then progress towards virtue, they said, is progress towards these benefits. Everything seems to depend on developing our self-control and power of reason – which I found could take considerable practice. Our first, spontaneous reaction might be emotional, but we are not to become overly attached to it. The sometimes confusing distinction made by the Stoics between benign and destructive emotions is concisely explained by the classicist A.A. Long in *Epictetus: A Stoic and Socratic Guide to Life* (2002):

> The unimpassioned mentality … of the ideal Stoic is not equivalent to a complete absence of emotion … It signifies a mind that is free from ‘irrational’ passions such as lust, craving, anger, dread, jealousy, envy, irritability and worry. The Stoics set against these ‘morbid’ emotions a category of attitudes that they called ‘good feelings’ … such attitudes as cheerfulness, sociability, respectfulness, kindness and affection.

My interest was sustained, as I read, by several pleasures and challenges: the ingenuity of the Stoics’ arguments along with the aptness and surprise of their analogies; the experience of being transported from my own life, via their metaphors and allusions, to their own lives and landscapes; my attempts to test out Stoic precepts in daily practice; and the continual exercise of my reason both to discover ramifications of an idea and also, sometimes, to struggle against my emotional resistance.

Some of the Stoic precepts address the challenges of our daily life as we attempt to live it effectively; and some address the prospect of dying, the moment of dying, or the deaths of our loved ones. I will first mention some of the precepts concerning living, which, as Marcus says, we are used to doing. Then, as we do in life, we will come to death and the precepts concerned with death.

The Stoic principles concerning such recurring problems as self-discipline, moderation in one’s habits and getting along with others, especially difficult people, are illustrated and argued in a variety of ways by a host of different thinkers. Here I am summarising and generalising, and occasionally commenting:

*Accept the people you live among.*

*If a bad person does something bad, why are you surprised?*

*Don’t be disturbed by another person’s opinion; how is it that you value yourself above all, yet you listen to the opinion of another more than your own?*

*Do not be disturbed by negative criticism from another person.* Either the person is right, in which case you can try to change your ways; or the person is wrong, and you can ignore what they say.

And vice versa. *Before you criticise another person, take care to examine whether you’re guilty of the same fault.* I tried asking myself, for instance, before criticising my friend, even if silently, for being late so often: haven’t I, too, sometimes kept people waiting?

Seneca counsels us not to be *overbearing* but rather to be *gentle* and *courteous*. Those three adjectives have seemed, in practice, a quite comprehensive guide: steer away from *overbearing*, head for *gentle* and *courteous*.

*Regard all human beings as your fellows, no matter what you think of them.* Cicero: ‘We are born not for ourselves but for others.’

Following from that: *Expect public service to be part of your practice. Work for the community, for the common good.*

*Anticipating problems will help you tackle them better.*

*Think and plan carefully before you start a project.*

*Work hard at making progress in your thinking.* An athlete works hard to achieve more bodily strength and skill. Likewise, we need to work hard to make progress. It does become easier in time.

*Always look at things stripped down to their essential qualities.*

*We should not set too high a value on things we may lose at any time.*

*We should train ourselves to be content with simple needs.* I have always liked the ideal of the simple life. But just how simple is a simple life? There are degrees of simple.

Lastly, an intriguing question, in this age of over-consumption. *What is the proper limit of a person’s wealth?* Seneca answers: ‘Having what is essential and … having what is enough.’ But *essential* and *enough* are both matters of opinion, even for the smallest things. One person absolutely needs a dishwasher in her kitchen – it is *essential*! *–* but for my mother-in-law, all her life, two pans of water were enough. (One for washing, one for rinsing.)

According to Farnsworth, ‘overcoming the fear of death is considered by the Stoics to be one of the most important of all philosophical achievements, and the gain of an important liberty.’

The Stoics regarded death as what they called an ‘external’, that is, something outside us, something we can’t control and can’t avoid, and also the most frightening prospect the mind confronts. Our attitude towards it is generally irrational. We fear death without knowing what it’s like to be dead. But, they argue, our situation after death seems to be no worse than our situation before we were conceived, when no one was aware of us. Whether you do not begin at all, or whether you end – that is, die – the result is non-existence.

How do we overcome our fear of death?

Seneca says: ‘Learning how to live takes a lifetime, and – what may surprise you more – it takes a lifetime to learn how to die.’

Marcus points out that we have no alternative. In one of his most succinct, almost brutally blunt exhortations, he says to himself: ‘Either you continue to live in this world, and you are used to that by now; or else you take yourself out of it, and that by your own desire; or else you die, and your service is completed. There is no other alternative; so be cheerful then.’ This is another iteration of the basic Stoic idea – that we should not fret over things that are beyond our control. Marcus’s three options include dying by one’s own hand, because suicide was permitted at that time, excepting if one was a slave or a soldier. (If you were a slave, you belonged to someone else; if you were a soldier, to take your own life would be to desert your post.)

Marcus is uncertain, though, about what happens to the soul after death: ‘Alexander the Great and his stable boy were brought to the same level in death,’ he writes, addressing death’s equalising effect. But then: ‘They were either taken back into the same generative principle of the universe or were scattered one and both into atoms.’ Nor does he know, he admits at another point, whether the soul remains for a while after death, before being reabsorbed into the universe, or is reabsorbed immediately.

*Let death become a familiar thing.* Montaigne asks us to let death become a familiar thing: ‘Let us spend time with it, let us get used to it.’

*Death is closer to us than we imagine, in any case.* We may choose to spend time with it in our thoughts, but we should remember that it may, in fact, be closer to us than we think. Farnsworth cites Melville, another reader of Seneca and Montaigne, declaring, in *Moby-Dick*, that if we are good philosophers, we should be no more terrified of death sitting in a wave-tossed whaleboat than we are when sitting comfortably at home before our evening fire, with a poker by our side instead of a harpoon.

*Death can be seen as a continuous process.* Whether death is close to us or not, it can be regarded as a continuous process – because it is always drawing closer. Seneca’s metaphor was the water-clock, emptying drop by drop. For Montaigne, ‘Every day is a step towards death. The last one arrives there.’

*The brevity of life*. Marcus urges himself to make his way ‘through this brief moment of time’ and to accept his end ‘with a cheerful heart’.

I do see what an abyss of time lies behind us and what an infinitude of time lies ahead of us, when I am asked to see it. But as I am living through this brief moment, so brief by comparison, it does expand infinitely, and my days often pass very slowly, even the minutes and the seconds, especially when I’m waiting for something.

*Annoyance and the brevity of life.* The thought of the brevity of our lives and the imminence of our deaths – whether tomorrow, the day after tomorrow, or in ten years – should help us to gain a useful perspective on our difficulties, both the petty ones, such as envy or a desire for fame or wealth, and also the graver ones, such as a death in our family.

Marcus advises: ‘When you are annoyed beyond measure and losing all patience, remember that human life lasts but a moment, and that in a short while we shall all have been laid to rest.’ But can I really, when I hang up the phone after an annoying conversation, say to myself, ‘Never mind – soon we shall all have been laid to rest’? I might more naturally draw on another Stoic precept: ‘You could see that this was an annoying sort of person; then what did you expect but annoying behaviour?’ Which includes yet another precept – it is sometimes helpful to anticipate trouble.

The narrator in Flannery O’Connor’s story ‘A Late Encounter with the Enemy’ remarks, about a 104-year-old grandfather: ‘Living had got to be such a habit with him that he couldn’t conceive of any other condition.’ The old man, a Confederate Civil War veteran, agrees out of vanity to attend his middle-aged granddaughter’s graduation, since he will be seated in a place of honour on the stage. But then, as the ceremony unfolds, he dies, clenching his sword ‘until the blade touched bone’. Living is a long-standing habit for us. Ever since we became aware of ourselves, we have never been anything but alive. We don’t seem to believe we will ever really die. That our death lies in some vague future, that we don’t know its appointed date and time, must be part of the problem. Then we are stricken by something like a terminal illness. That is when we say, *now* I know I will die.

About being afraid of one’s own death, the Stoics point out logically and pragmatically that, after all, one is still alive until the moment one dies, and then, after that moment, *one is no longer aware of anything*! Stoic philosophy aims to help us achieve an enduring sense of inner peace while we are alive, but also tells us we will not miss our life once we are gone.

True enough. But, faced with the imminence of your own death, you might, naturally, fiercely want to go on living – because you enjoy your life! I like to work! I like to look things up in books and take notes! I like to think about the city of Arles, on the Rhône! I *really* like to measure Roman roads! I want one more chance to talk to my old friends! I don’t want it all to end! Like a certain character in a short story of Lucia Berlin’s.

Lucia Berlin did not become widely known until after her death, although, because of the modest person she was, she did not seem to feel a great need for recognition. Her stories were often taken almost wholly from her own life, and she wrote several about her sister dying of cancer. In one of the stories, ‘Wait a Minute’, the character of the sister says, miserably, ‘I’ll never see donkeys again.’ That one sentence, spoken probably in real life, conveyed to me in a shockingly vivid way, not a fear of death, but the pain of the loss one imagines suffering by dying, the loss of one’s ongoing full experience of life.

She won’t, and we won’t, when we die, ever see a donkey again. This thought still gives me a pang. But, as the Stoics say, reasonably, you won’t miss this once you’re gone – you won’t be here to miss it. And it is within our power not to regret it in advance. Everything is within the power of our rational minds. Just as we don’t need to brood about all our mistakes in the past, which is over and done with and can’t be changed, we don’t need to dread the future, which is unknown and may be briefer or less painful than we might think.

In preparation for our own death, whenever it may come, a thought from Seneca requires some mental acrobatics, but is helpful. He says: ‘We must make it our aim to have already lived long enough.’ This is, in some odd way, a comforting thought. If we can manage to do it – *I’ve done enough, I’m done* – we have already come to terms with dying, perhaps, even, long before we actually face death.

Marcus Aurelius suggests something like the same idea: ‘Think of yourself as having died, and as having finished the life you have lived until now. The portion that is allowed to you beyond this, live out according to nature.’ I could, first, as Seneca advises elsewhere, balance my accounts. I could then imagine I had finished my life, in a satisfactory way, and lived long enough. I could then concentrate on the remaining days allowed to me, living each one as though it were the last – which was another of Marcus’s precepts.

When we​ contemplate our own imminent departure, the loneliness of it may be another part of what disturbs us, even though death is common to us all and happening all around us. Some of us may console ourselves by believing we will not be alone after our death. My devout grandmother, having no doubts about the afterlife, was happy, on her deathbed, that she would soon be rejoining her first husband (*not* her second).

When Marcus says to himself, ‘Let it make no difference to you whether you are shivering or warm if only you are doing your duty, or whether you are over-tired or have had sufficient sleep, or are greeted with disparagement or praise, or are in the process of dying, or are busy with something else,’ he seems to be familiarising the act of dying by putting it on a par with doing ‘something else’ – as though we had a choice. And in a way, he is, since he goes on to say: ‘For even the act of dying is one of the acts of our life; and so in that, too, it is enough to make good use of what the moment brings.’

You should take the same care in approaching your death as you once did in folding a piece of clothing. Even as you die, you live fully in the present. And remaining fully in the present is another aspect of not brooding about past misfortunes or fretting over an imagined dark future.

Far from merely accepting death, Seneca regarded mortality as a gift; we should be relieved to be able to depart this life. ‘Deny, now, if you can,’ he said, ‘that nature is very generous in making death available.’ Here, Seneca may mean a natural death as a relief from suffering, but he may also mean suicide, which, as Marcus pointed out, was also an option. My other grandmother, a kind and loving woman, and fond of her family, nevertheless chose to take her own life. She suffered from recurring bouts of mental illness, and in an interval of peace and lucidity, she decided she could not face more of these devastating and humiliating episodes.

The prospect of one’s own death may be easier to accept than the great absence left by the deaths of the ones you loved so much. But should my grandmother have denied herself the gift of death and chosen to remain alive, in periodic great distress, only so that we, her family, selfishly, would not be the ones to suffer from losing her? That is a question.

We are not only emotionally stricken by the death of a loved one, but regard it, especially when it is sudden, with incredulity. But he was always there, speaking up, with his strong opinions! But she was just beginning to feed herself, and she had red hair! Fully understanding that a loved one is gone for good takes time. In one situation after another, the person is, yet again, *not there* – to our surprise, each time – but finally, eventually, we absorb the new reality.

What Marcus says about this absence is quoted from Epictetus: ‘It takes a madman to seek a fig in winter; and such is one who seeks for his child when he is no longer granted to him.’

The child is no longer there. And yet, Marcus says, after all, death is a natural occurrence. Quoting Epictetus again, he reminds himself that the death of a child is as natural as the harvesting of grain.

Another entry about a child, this one arguing that the length of a life doesn’t matter, only its quality, I have so far found impossible to accept. ‘For look at the abyss of time behind you, and the other infinity which stretches in front of you; in view of that, what difference is there between a baby who lives for three days and a Nestor who lives for three generations?’ (Nestor was a legendary king in Greek mythology, the archetype of the wise old man.)

I have tried to figure out why I can’t persuade my reason to accept this proposition. I think it is because I, and we, so firmly identify a baby with a beginning. We instinctively, unreasoningly, feel that a beginning can’t become an end. We say you can’t end what is a beginning, and so we protest. We have become firm in our belief as to when it is appropriate or acceptable for a person to die. For instance, if I am close to eighty years old, it is now more acceptable for me to die, although, these days, as we live longer, one’s late eighties would seem even more appropriate; it would not outrage anyone’s sense of what is right and fair.

I have no doubt that Marcus doted on his children. Even his old tutor Fronto, scholar, teacher, consul, but also an affectionate father and friend, says to Marcus in one of his letters that he looks forward to kissing the ‘tiny hands and plump little feet’ of Marcus’s first-born child, Faustina. Faustina was also one of the first of Marcus’s children to die in infancy.

After wrestling with his proposition about quality over quantity of life, I now suspect that Marcus was urging himself to accept a logical, reasonable conclusion that he may not have accepted in his heart any more than we do. I don’t know if it’s the logic of the argument that is at fault, or our failure to be absolutely rational. Or does the fault lie with our particular, prejudiced point of view? In the eyes of history, or of the gods, or anyone else who does not know the baby, there may be little difference between its death and the death of an old man. Achieving some emotional distance, or perspective, according to the Stoics, helps with almost all problems – or all of them, in fact. But how to gain this perspective?

They recommend several strategies, including naming and describing the object or event; stripping it to its essentials; looking back at the present moment from an imagined future; observing our human lives here on earth from an imagined point far above, in the heavens. A familiar poem also expresses something like this long view, though here the observer remains on the earth.

Keats, in a sonnet that is a single sentence starting with the word ‘When’ – thus plunging us into his difficult emotional situation – arrives by the end of the poem at a solution for contending with his fear, which he first announces as the fear that he will die before he has written everything he would like to write and that he will lose his beloved, too. He is able to imagine, as a solution to his disturbance, adopting a different perspective – exactly what Marcus so often recommends – one that implies distance, as he says at the conclusion of the poem.

> When I have fears that I may cease to be  
>   Before my pen has gleaned my teeming brain,  
> Before high-piled books, in charactery,  
>   Hold like rich garners the full ripened grain;  
> When I behold, upon the night’s starred face,  
>   Huge cloudy symbols of a high romance,  
> And think that I may never live to trace  
>   Their shadows with the magic hand of chance;  
> And when I feel, fair creature of an hour,  
>   That I shall never look upon thee more,  
> Never have relish in the faery power  
>   Of unreflecting love – then on the shore  
> Of the wide world I stand alone, and think  
> Till love and fame to nothingness do sink.

But it is an odd conclusion, in a way: what is the shore of the wide world? One thinks of a cliff, or a seashore, or both: a cliff overlooking a seashore. And maybe that’s what Keats had in mind. I would not object to having available to me a literal shore, of a wide body of water, where I could go to stand alone and think. But Keats may have meant this metaphorically: a wide world in one’s imagination, which I suppose I do have. That verb ‘think’, dangling there, is also mysterious: ‘I stand alone, and think.’ What is he thinking about? This very problem?

When I reread it, I notice that although in the beginning he announces that he is worried simply that he won’t be able to write all that he wants to – a rather pure desire – by the end, he has summed up that wish with the word ‘fame’. Is it writing he wants to do, or fame that he wants to achieve? The desire for fame, and its falsity, was another subject the Stoics returned to again and again.

The Stoics, when making a point about human behaviour, regularly turned, for their analogies, to plants, insects, animals, water and minerals, with all of which they lived on closer terms than many of us do now. Marcus in the course of his meditations devises comparisons using, among others, a grapevine, a horse, a dog, a fig seed, a bitter cucumber, brambles in one’s path and ‘noxious things’ such as a lion’s gaping jaws, a thistle plant and a quagmire, besides the bees and other creatures hard at work while he idles in bed. He compares his own death to an olive ripening and dropping from the tree.

A single extended simile from Marcus starts off from a couple of lines of the *Iliad* in which Homer compares leaves scattered by the wind to ‘the generations of men’. In developing it, Marcus reiterates his ideas about the ephemerality of life:

> And what are your children but leaves, and leaves too these people who acclaim you with such conviction and sing your praises, or, on the contrary, curse you, or reproach you in secret and jibe at your expense; and leaves likewise those who will transmit your fame when you are gone. For all of these ‘arrive with the spring’, and then the wind brings them down, and the forest puts forth others in their place. This fleeting existence is the common lot of all things, and yet you pursue and flee each thing as though it will last forever. A little while, and you will close your eyes; and he who carried you to your grave will soon be lamented by another.

All those leaves that have fallen between Marcus Aurelius’s time and our own – I see them as having formed the approximately twelve feet of soil that lies between the surface of our earth today and the surface of the earth down below, in Roman times. You understand the depth of this soil when you venture into a residential courtyard in an old French city, hunting down the subject of a reference not in a guidebook, and find it, in shadow, under a flight of stairs: it is the very top of a Roman triumphal arch, standing just a few feet up out of the cobblestone paving, with a bicycle leaning against it.

Iwill conclude​ by describing a plant I have lately become acquainted with. It is not one we really want, not in the north-eastern United States anyway, but we may admire the hugely varied and ingenious strategies of even the least ‘desirable’ plants and animals. The Japanese stiltgrass, an introduced species that readily takes over woods, meadows, roadsides and more, is an annual and thus dies at the close of every season. While alive, it does everything to make sure of producing future generations: each of these tender green, slender, spindly grasses has several seed heads, and each seed head bears about a thousand seeds. Each seed is viable for up to five years. The seeds may germinate in the soil at the feet of their parents, but they also have the means of dispersal to go far afield: they are light and can float on air or water; and they are bristly, so they can catch on an animal’s or a person’s body. Each node of a grass stem can put down roots if it touches the ground. Stiltgrass can tolerate sun and shade both. If mowed down early in the season, its response is to begin again, but at a faster pace, growing up and flowering more rapidly in order to set seed before the end of the season. Stiltgrass is also negatively allelopathic, meaning it changes the soil chemistry around it so that the soil is less hospitable to other plants.

An individual plant succumbs at the end of the season. But throughout the growing season, it has allowed for its own impending death by ensuring the continuation of its species. Everything it does promotes this and nothing it does detracts from this: it takes perfect care. It prefers to live among a crowd of others, and together with that crowd it creates a crowd of offspring. It follows Cicero’s idea: it is born not for itself, but for others.

Although it is prodigal in its production of seed, the stiltgrass is not wasteful: the seed that loses its viability decomposes in the soil and thus feeds it, as does the body of each individual plant after it dies. There is an overall neat economy in the stiltgrass’s cycle of existence, the same that Marcus describes and that we recognise if we have chosen to allow our spent garden plants to stand or lie, undisturbed, over the winter. ‘The extraordinary thing about nature’s craftsmanship,’ he writes,

> is that, having established her own limits, she transforms into herself everything within her that seems to be decaying and growing old and useless, and out of these very things creates other new things in their place, so that it needs neither additional substance from outside nor any separate place for the disposal of decaying material.

We humans are quite good at planting our seeds and bearing our offspring, and we mostly try our best to nurture them, something the plants, insects and animals do directly, or, when not directly, then indirectly, by preparing a hospitable environment for the next generation when it hatches or sprouts or otherwise appears, as the stiltgrass does by enriching the soil and causing it to be negatively allelopathic for other, possibly competitive, plants.

We humans could set aside both pessimism and optimism about the future and be as pragmatic and foresightful as the stiltgrass is by nature. Yes, we could say to ourselves, I will have to depart, at the end of *my* season, which may come soon, or later, but in the meantime I will balance my accounts and do what I can, in my own way, to encourage the survival of the generations that come after me, the offspring of my community. And, if I have been evolving reasonably well as a follower of Stoic philosophy, by now I will know what my community is.
