Notes on contributors from ‘Fuck You’, Vol. 5, No. 5 (December 1963).

In August​ 1975, residents of Columbus, Ohio found something strange stuffed under their car windscreen wipers – a single sheet of typescript, which consisted of what looked at first glance like a series of small ads:

> FOUND: Photo album with family snapshots, left eye cut out of each person photographed. Vicinity Southern Hotel. 984-7321 before 10.

Another typescript followed in October, with more listings:

> LOST: Musical dildo, answers to the name ‘Malcolm’, fine filigree work at base of stem, hallmark partly erased, once belonged to Liberace’s candle – (animated). Byproduct of ladies kotex offered as partial reward. 681-2820. POB 3622, Vancouver, BC V6B 3Y6

These baffling bits of ephemera were the first two issues of the *Lost and Found Times*, produced by the poet John M. Bennett and the painter D.C. Landies. Having sent some copies through the post as a ‘mail art stunt’, they developed the alternative (and cheaper) distribution method of putting flyers on cars in a shopping centre parking lot. Landies died in the late 1970s but the *Lost and Found Times* continued for another thirty years, sometimes with variant titles (*Exploding Horse* and *Dog Cake Hat*). Bennett would print things people sent him, sometimes text, sometimes images, but always ‘unclassifiable materials … that no one else would publish’. Richard Kostelanetz, elder statesman of the American avant-garde, called it ‘the most persistently experimental literary magazine to survive for more than a decade in America’. To bemused Ohians peeling it off their windscreens, this description might have been surprising. Is it literature? For that matter, is it even a magazine?

These questions are prompted by many of the artefacts in Steve Clay and M.C. Kinniburgh’s *After Words*. Published in parallel with an exhibition last year at New York’s Grolier Club, it’s a generously illustrated guide to a sprawling field, which over time has generated its own tangle of niche subcategories: concrete poetry, mail art, sound poetry, found poetry, asemic writing, performance poetry, cut-ups and erasure. There is no shortage of labels, but like *Lost and Found Times*, much of this is material more easily defined by what it’s not, and sometimes it doesn’t much resemble poetry. In *The Desert* (2008), Jen Bervin reworks an existing prose book, using a needle and thread to obscure most of the text. Cecilia Vicuña’s *Chanccani Quipu* (2012) consists of long strands of unspun wool with letters stencilled on them, a reimagining of the ancient Inca writing system of knotted cords. These are experiments in pushing poetry to the boundaries of legibility, into new forms, and even off the page entirely.

Arranged in loose thematic chapters, *After Words* is a compendium and catalogue rather than a chronological account. But the start date of 1960 is significant, giving all this activity a point of origin: the explosion of independent, underground publishing known as the ‘mimeo revolution’. The mimeograph machine (sometimes known as the Gestetner) was a simple stencil duplicator, forerunner to the office photocopier. By the early 1960s these devices were inexpensive and portable enough for home use and, co-opted by the emergent counterculture, they generated a deluge of pamphlets, manifestos and magazines. No special skills or training were needed. You simply needed to type – or even draw – onto the stencil paper, wrap it around the ink drum and then crank the handle. As the drum rotated, ink squeezed through the holes in the stencil onto the waiting stack of blank sheets. As Clay put it in an earlier book, ‘for the price of a few reams of paper and a handful of stencils, a poet could produce, by mimeograph, a magazine or booklet in a small edition over the course of a few days.’ The ethos was not just ‘do-it-yourself’ but, in Ed Sanders’s words, ‘do-it-now’.

Sanders was the Che Guevara of the mimeo-revolutionaries, writing one of his earliest poems on prison toilet paper while briefly incarcerated in 1961 for taking part in an anti-nuclear protest. The following year he acquired a cheap Speed-o-Print duplicator, small enough to perch on the porcelain bathtub cover in his Lower East Side apartment, and launched *Fuck You/ A Magazine of the Arts*. Carrying work by the most experimental poets and writers of the time – William Burroughs, Gregory Corso and Charles Olson – it wasn’t so much printed as ‘zapped’, ‘puked’ or ‘stomped’, as the title page announced. (The odd boot print could reputedly be found on its pages of salvaged construction paper.)

The ‘little magazine’ is usually identified with an earlier period: the second decade of the 20th century, when a sudden flourishing of small-scale periodicals led to the rise of modernism. But with rare exceptions (notably Wyndham Lewis’s *BLAST*), these were shocking in content, not appearance. In 1918 the *Little Review* (like *Fuck You*, subtitled ‘A Magazine of the Arts’) began serialising James Joyce’s *Ulysses*, whose description of masturbation was enough to get it censored. Yet its pages, elegantly printed in Caslon, a respectable typeface, looked conventional. The re-emergence of the little magazine, half a century later, was a more rough and ready affair. The *Little Review* may have been banned for printing literary depictions of ejaculation, but unlike Sanders’s publication, it did not have actual spurting cocks scrawled on the contents page.

With its crude Courier typeface, *Fuck You* was the publishing equivalent of a punk 7-inch single – spontaneous and scabrous, capturing the raw energy of the moment. Or, to use an analogy more in tune with the early 1960s, these poetry magazines had the force of protest music, and amid the bohos and beatniks of Lower Manhattan, the two often overlapped. In D.A. Pennebaker’s 1967 documentary *Dont Look Back*, Allen Ginsberg, a frequent contributor to *Fuck You* and semi-permanent fixture at Sanders’s Peace Eye bookshop, poses in the background as Bob Dylan is filmed holding up the lyrics to ‘Subterranean Homesick Blues’. On the cover of 1965’s *Bringing It All Back Hom*e, Dylan is pictured surrounded by carefully curated records and paraphernalia. Behind him, propped up on the mantelpiece, you can see the distinctive pink cover of the single-issue ‘one-shot’ magazine *Gnaoua*, not strictly mimeograph but featuring poetry by Ginsberg. In late 1964, Sanders made his own move from print to music, opening another front in his ‘total assault on culture’. With his fellow poet Tuli Kupferberg, he founded a psychedelic garage rock band – the Fugs – whose most famous performance was an ‘exorcism’ of the Pentagon in protest at the Vietnam War.

The mimeo magazine lifted restrictions not only on what you could print, but the way you could present it. If anyone could now become a publisher, they could also become a typesetter, arranging the words on the page however they wanted. Instead of inserting the stencil paper in the typewriter as intended, you could take liberties, typing at strange angles, layering text, breaking it down and arranging the letters to create images or shapes. In this respect the trailblazer isn’t Sanders but d.a. levy, who, as the critic Karl Young has written, ‘was almost completely alone in making mimeo an art form in its own right’. Now revered as something of a cult figure, levy (he insisted on the lower-case spelling) embodied the archetype of the dishevelled outlaw poet. He remained in Cleveland, rather than moving to New York, living on less than a dollar a day and reciting his work on street corners. He died of a self-inflicted gunshot wound aged 26. Yet he was prolific and industrious, publishing his own and other people’s work through his Renegade Press and launching probably the most innovative of the 1960s mimeo magazines, the *Marrahwanna Quarterly*. It became a vehicle for cutting-edge concrete poetry type scattered across the page or clustered in dense, abstract patterns.

Having previously used letterpress printing, levy switched the magazine’s production process, exploiting the capabilities of the mimeograph and embracing its lo-fi aesthetics. There’s something defiantly amateurish in this presentation, refusing any kind of standardisation. Issues weren’t uniform in size or paper type, covers were constructed from scraps of repurposed newspaper: a copy from 1966 has a torn-out page of an old elocution manual inexplicably pasted inside. Like the *Lost and Found Times*, some had variant titles on the front – the *Mary Jane Quarterly* or the *Majoon Quarterly* – and others had no title at all, just a rough hand-painted doodle. More than anything, there’s a sense of haste and urgency: you can feel the force of typewriter keys as levy hammers out an embattled stream of consciousness editorial. ‘Again no paper … no money … tons of postage needed and equipment’, he fumes, while sending ‘HATE RAYS’ to the small-minded citizens of Cleveland and the police. (He had been indicted for obscenity, a case that was never prosecuted but which exacerbated his sense of persecution.) Sometimes he hits the wrong keys (typing ‘predd’ instead of ‘press’) and has to overtype with the correct letters; sometimes he scratches into the stencil with a stylus. Then there are the blotches and surface noise, the ink pooling in the hollows of the ‘o’s as he cranks the handle of the machine. More so than letterpress, mimeograph printing is a visceral snapshot of an instant in time, preserving the flux and mess of its own moment of creation. Even now, after sixty years, you come away from handling these things with ink on your hands, since one quirk of mimeo is that it doesn’t fully dry.

The scruffy mimeo aesthetic was new, but concrete poetry itself was not. The term had been in circulation since the 1950s, and the underlying practice of innovating with typography has a much longer history. (There are many precursors in the early 20th century, and as far back as 1633 we find George Herbert printing the text of a poem sideways in the form of two divine wings.) But mimeograph provided a new and radical impetus. For a while concrete poetry was an anti-establishment lingua franca, a means of communication between an international network of poets, publishers and thinkers. In Canada there were the magazines *Ganglia* and *grOnk*; in the UK, *TLALOC* and *Poetmeat*; in Germany, *Rhinozeros*. And – though it might be hard to imagine now – poetry was a subversive and dangerous occupation. In 1967, levy was charged with ‘contributing to the delinquency of a minor during a poetry reading’ (he’d read out a poem containing the word ‘motherfucker’). The previous year, Sanders had been charged with possession of obscene material with intent to sell, and the year before, *Poetmeat*’s editor, Dave Cunliffe, had been charged with sending obscene material through the post.

Experimental poetry was a rejection of the old order. According to levy’s ‘Para-Concrete Manifesto’, ‘after aeons of time & boxes of poets writing singing chanting & shrieking love poems; people still prefer to kill each other. The PEACE poets aren’t getting their message across … so fuck it.’ ‘Concrete’ was thus a protest against the exhaustion of lyric poetry, even language itself, which was retooled and subverted to new ends. Levy’s 1966 pamphlet *Visualised Prayers & Hymn for the American $God$* took aim at the Vietnam War, greed and the dark reality of the American dream, and had no use for poetic metaphor. It made its point through the typewriter keys, conjuring the American flag out of dollar signs and asterisks. Letters and symbols, severed from their normal function, could be treated as visual elements and manipulated as such. In the pages of the *Marrahwanna Quarterly*, the Canadian writer bpNichol described the poet as a kind of sculptor who could generate new kinds of meaning since ‘communication does not just mean language.’ Dom Sylvester Houédard, a Benedictine priest and exponent of contemplative concrete ‘typestracts’, claimed that we are ‘boxed into our language’: ‘It is the concrete poet who lifts the lid.’

But was this​ a subversion of a bankrupt system of language, or a search for a new, universal mode of communication, beyond existing limits? The tension between these ideas seems to be at play in levy’s *Tibetan Stroboscope* (1968), produced shortly before his death. Subtitled ‘an experiment in destructive writing, “other” communications and “concrete” prose’, its visually rich mash-up of images and text – part pop-art collage, part fragmentary poem – marked a development of his techniques. Its striking layered effects were produced using letterpress on newsprint paper (the term ‘mimeo-revolution’ can be misleading, since these small-press magazines often used a variety of methods). Zen Buddhism jostled with scrambled advertisements and tawdry scraps of popular magazines. It’s a sort of mischievous culture-jamming and parody of Western capitalist excess. And yet, with its repeated motifs from Eastern mysticism, it hankers after some unifying spiritual meaning.

These two strains play out in the visual poetry featured in *After Words*. The visionary work of Timothy C. Ely imagines communication through pure sensation, trying to access an ur-language through a notation system he calls ‘cribriform’, while in *Kaldron* magazine in 1977, Charles Doria traces the roots of visual poetry back to the earliest writing systems, which functioned as a kind of ‘sorcery’. At other times, the written word seems a very unmagical and wonky instrument, as in John Furnival’s byzantine and precarious structure of words, a teetering Tower of Babel which threatens to collapse under its own weight. The printmakers Emily McVarish and Johanna Drucker emphasise typography’s implicit ‘agenda’ (typefaces are always a kind of surreptitious visual persuasion), while the German printer Hansjörg Mayer’s decision to produce a whole series of experiments with Futura in 1965 is significant. It’s a typeface with a fraught history, initially adopted in the 1930s by the Nazi Party for its communications, but dropped in favour of the more gothic-looking Fraktur.

Levy’s *Tibetan Stroboscope* is emblematic in other ways, too. The title invoked an imaging technology which, by the mid-1960s, was employed to confuse the senses. The stroboscopic lamp, adopted for use in music concerts, induced hallucinogenic states through its rapid succession of freeze frames. The magazine’s effects aimed at equivalent sensory disorientation, with its rows of text overprinted and blotted out, both inviting reading and refusing it. At more or less the same time, Tom Phillips was beginning to experiment with a similar technique in what would become probably the most famous work of erasure writing, *A Humument*, and the Argentinian Mirtha Dermisache published her early ‘asemic’ poetry, delicate scribbles that look from a distance like text. The *Tibetan Stroboscope* is in dialogue with these developments, but its blotted-out lines, appearing more like sound waves than text, also call to mind a term coined by the Fluxus artist and poet Dick Higgins, ‘intermedia’. The poetry of the 1960s was crossing boundaries, Higgins argued, joining up not just with painting but with other art forms, including music and performance. This ‘spirit of the in-between’ was what animated Henri Chopin’s *OU*, a multimedia magazine that came with a vinyl record on which sound poets such as Bernard Heidsieck performed not just words but noises. Chopin, from a French Jewish background, belonged to an older generation that had its own motivations for rejecting the authoritarianism of the ‘true word’ in favour of the liberation of linguistic anarchy. He had survived a death march during the war while his two brothers had both been shot.

Poetry could also be a prompt or ‘score’ for a performance. La Monte Young’s *An Anthology of Change Operations* (1963) contains a poem by Jackson Mac Low, which ‘may be used in any way as a score for solo or group readings, musical or dramatic performances, looking, smelling, anything else &/or nothing at all’. At this point, a world of possibilities opens up beyond simply reading a poem, and the question becomes what you do with it. Herman Deman’s ‘Braille-Pouim’ (1968) invites touch, while Hannah Weiner’s *Signal Flag Poems* (also 1968) are in the form of an interactive wheel-like volvelle that readers rotate. Steve McCaffery’s *Carnival* (1973) consisted of a series of large-scale ‘panels’ that readers were instructed to tear out and arrange on the wall. *A: An Envelope Magazine of Visual Poetry* (1971-72) needs to be unsealed and unpacked, as does the magazine-in-a-box *SMS* (for ‘Shit Must Stop’). These, too, are ‘assemblings’ of loose-leaf elements which you sift through, in no particular order, bringing us closer to the origin of the term ‘magazine’, deriving from the Arabic word *makhzan*, meaning container or storehouse. There’s even a poem you can put your beer on: Philip Gallo’s ‘bar napkin doodle poem’ (1988), a disposable drinks mat that Gallo printed with a squiggle and some minimal cryptic text.

With these more disparate shapes, the ‘experimental’ aspect increasingly concerns not poetry so much as its material container. You might assume that mimeo magazines of the 1960s and 1970s would morph into such DIY methods as photocopying, dot matrix printers or digital publishing, and *After Words* includes a chapter on the ‘xerography’ magazines of the 1980s and 1990s. But what emerges more strongly is physical diversity – boxes, beermats and quipus – as if, following the big bang of the mimeo revolution, the trajectory is ever outwards into uncharted reaches and new formats.

This is the territory that Granary Books, publisher of *After Words*, has occupied since the 1980s. Founded by Steve Clay with the mission of exploring ‘the intersection of word, image and page’, it produced some of the works represented in this volume, including Bervin’s stitched book, Vicuña’s quipu and Buzz Spector’s enigmatic book-cum-sculpture *A Passage* (1994). This contains an anecdote about a rabbinical scholar who can point to a single letter on any page of the books of the Talmud and recall each letter that appears in that exact position on every subsequent page. The rabbi wouldn’t have much trouble with Spector’s book, since its 181 recto pages are all identical. Each has also been ripped out vertically at tiny increments to form a wedge shape. The effect is that every page is simultaneously visible and yet the text is virtually illegible, since all we can see are torn edges. It’s a well-known piece, but here it appears in a new light. Placing this profusion of small-press material in proximity means unexpected connections emerge and old boundaries dissolve. *A Passage* might seem a long way from the inky provocations of the mimeo mag, but we can see many of the same ideas at play. You just can’t put it under a windscreen wiper.
