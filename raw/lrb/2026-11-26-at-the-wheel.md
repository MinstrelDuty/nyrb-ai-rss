# 正文

Last year​ I was in Los Angeles for a book launch – my own book launch, in fact – and, as I’d never learned to drive, I called an Uber to take me from the hotel to the bookshop. I was met by a teenager in a souped-up Honda Civic with scratches down the side, which even I know is the budget-conscious drag racer’s stock car of choice. He proceeded to drive Gran Turismo-style, switching lanes at top speed without signalling, alternating between hitting his weed pen and flicking through Jelly Roll songs. Arriving at the launch drenched in sweat and on the verge of throwing up, I thought: wouldn’t it be better, if I were to die in a car accident, to have my own hand on the wheel? At least I could choose the music.

On returning to New York a few days later, I looked up driving schools. The first one that came up offered a $5 trial lesson and immediate booking. I made the appointment that afternoon and received an automated text telling me to meet my driving instructor, Zayd, on 72nd Street, outside Juice Generation. I wasn’t sure how to prepare for the lesson so I spent longer than usual picking out the right pair of shoes – penny loafers seemed the best approximation of those nice Italian driving shoes with rubber soles – before cycling down to the rendezvous point.

‘Hi! I’m here,’ I texted.

Zayd texted that he was across the street.

‘In a car?’ I asked.

‘Yes,’ he replied. Obviously he was in a car. ‘In a green car.’

I looked around. There was a forest green Porsche Cayenne SUV double-parked across from Juice Generation. I didn’t see any other green cars.

‘What type?’ I replied.

‘Corolla.’

I approached a white Toyota with a lime green gradient painted on its side. The back of the car bore the name of the driving school in an ugly pixellated font.

‘I was worried you were going to be in that Porsche,’ I said as Zayd got out of the car. He was about my height and about my age, with a dark beard and a bashful Winnie-the-Pooh expression.

‘Why worried?’ he asked, gesturing at me to get in the driver’s seat. ‘That would be awesome.’

‘Because what if I crashed the Porsche?’

‘Are you planning on crashing this?’

I couldn’t be sure.

For a long time I didn’t mind being a non-driver. We didn’t have a car when I was growing up. There was no reason to. We lived in Manhattan, and the subway conveyed my brother and me to our grandparents in the Bronx, the New Jersey Transit to our grandparents in the suburbs of Philadelphia, and the Long Island Railroad to the beach. I could walk to school, to my clarinet lessons, to the park. Sometimes we took the crosstown bus, double length and connected by a chamber we called the ‘accordion’ because it concertinaed slowly as it lumbered through turns. The only person I knew who drove to school was my best friend, Alexa, who lived in Midtown. If I slept over, her father, an Iranian jeweller, would drive us to school in their silver Range Rover. We always stopped to get ‘chai’ from Dunkin’ Donuts, which was just black tea.

My mother got her driver’s licence as a teenager, but let it lapse when she moved to New York. Riding the subway, ordering steak tartare, wearing all black and taking us to see foreign films were central to her self-conception as a woman delivered from suburbia to the promised land of urbanity. There was something vaguely European about not driving. I loved the subway, which was still plastered in rainbow graffiti. When a friend’s dad asked me what car my parents drove, I replied: ‘It’s silver, it’s very long and it goes underground!’ He stared at me, confused, until his wife said: ‘She means the subway, sweetie.’ He told me he drove a black Lexus.

My father, a product of Coney Island and the Bronx, does drive. Or at least, he has a car: my grandfather’s 2002 Toyota Camry, which he tends as if it were a vintage model. He says he can never get rid of it because it contains his father’s soul – he uses the Hebrew word *neshama*. My dad has a fetish for the yellow taxis his parents never took. He hails them with the kind of stentorian whistle only fathers are capable of, followed by the scream of *tax-ayy* in his Brooklyn drawl.

My first memory of an automobile is falling out of one. Deposited in the back of a cab, I scooched along the bench seat until I reached the far door, fiddled with the black plastic handle and tumbled out into Columbus Avenue. I can see it now, my toddler self flattened like Wile E. Coyote, a pizzetta of blonde ringlets. Why had I opened the door? Probably because I thought taxis worked the same way as trains, and the doors only opened when it was time to exit. ‘The inert but living body abandons itself to the moving deadweight that is transporting it,’ Paul Valéry wrote about taking the train to Paris in *Monsieur Teste*. ‘The express has an idée fixe, which is the city. You are captive to its ideal, the puppet of its monotonous fury.’

‘You don’t drive, do you?’ my friend Willa once asked me as her friend, someone I now realise is a terrible driver, was driving us up the West Side Highway to Arthur Avenue in the Bronx for Italian subs.

‘How’d you know?’ I’d asked, resting my head on the window.

‘You’re a very comfortable passenger,’ she replied.

Zayd told me to drive east towards the park, turn left and then continue uptown. It didn’t seem wise that I should start driving immediately. Shouldn’t there be a tutorial first, a video simulator, something involving toy trucks? You had to undergo more stringent training to take out a canoe at summer camp. But I tried to remain composed, adjusted the driver’s seat and asked Zayd for a hint as to how to start the car. He pointed to the green ignition button.

I had driven before but mostly in parking lots and on the empty back roads of Ohio, where the only other vehicle you are likely to meet is an Amish horse and buggy. In New York people were jaywalking and cars kept doing things without signalling. Suddenly they were merging! They were double-parked in the middle of the street! Driving was harder than I’d expected, not the mechanics exactly but the feeling of zooming along while staying in place. It was a bit like coming up on MDMA, your body hurtling ahead of you. I was having intrusive thoughts. What if I lost control of the car? What if I suddenly slammed on the gas pedal? What if I passed out at the steering wheel and crashed into a bus? I wanted to take a Xanax.

‘In my experience,’ Zayd said, ‘the best driving students are extremely stupid teenagers. You’re one of these big thinkers. That’s not good. You need to try to channel an extremely stupid teenager. Stop thinking. Just drive.’

For the next lesson, I booked a two-hour block. ‘Why the hell would you book a two-hour lesson?’ Zayd asked me. ‘You know that means you’re going to be driving for *two hours straight*? We could go on a road trip to Philadelphia.’ Instead we drove up Riverside Drive, to a section I’d never been on before because there was no sidewalk: it’s accessible only by car, something I didn’t know was possible in Manhattan.

During the Covid lockdown I escaped my roommates in Queens (one of whom, a male model and chef, had turned the living room into a gym for his model-chef friends to use) to hide out in an apartment on the Upper West Side that my aunt uses as her law office. This was the neighbourhood of my childhood and it quickly started to seem monotonous. After my millionth walk around Central Park I became annoyed at its engineered landscape, as I imagine an iguana grows angry with the artificial rocks and miniature castles in its terrarium. But now, with Zayd riding shotgun, bubbling on a watermelon Elf Bar, Riverside Drive felt brand new. I was *driving* down Riverside Drive. I’d never even registered that it was called Riverside *Drive*, but my god was it nice to manoeuvre the lime green Corolla around its serpentine curves, past Grant’s Tomb and down to the giant Corinthian temple commemorating the Union soldiers, or as we called it as teenagers when we met to smoke pot there, the ‘Big Monument’. That asshole Robert Moses really knew what he was doing erecting this grand boulevard under a canopy of oak trees right along the river. Why had I spent so many years not knowing how to drive in the United States of America? This was a civilisation built on the economy, the infrastructure, the libido of the automobile. I should have been born a car!

‘What the fuck are you doing?’ Zayd asked, as I felt the acceleration begin to drop against my will. ‘You’re doing double the speed limit!’ He had engaged the instructor emergency brake pedal.

‘Right, right, sorry,’ I said.

‘It’s OK. You’re getting comfortable. Just pay attention.’

At my next lesson, a guy in a Range Rover tried to manoeuvre around me while I was stopped at a light. I asked Zayd what I’d done wrong. ‘This guy is just an aggressive idiot driver,’ he told me. I looked at the car. It had New Jersey plates. ‘Wait,’ Zayd said suddenly. ‘This is a learning opportunity. Don’t do this in the test but I’m not going to let you be disrespected like this. You’re a New Yorker. Get up in front of him and box him out.’ I rolled forward slowly, blocking the larger car from the right. ‘OK, now honk at him.’ My first honk was fabulous. Honking and boxing out the Range Rover, I understood why men hung plastic testicles from the backs of their trucks. For the next few blocks, I drove with one hand on the wheel, until Zayd told me not to get too cocky.

On the days when I didn’t have driving lessons, I missed Zayd. In our next lesson, he told me about being sent back to Yemen to find a wife. ‘My family all wanted me to marry one of my cousins. But then I met my aunt’s friend’s daughter, and she was beautiful and funny and amazing. I had to marry her. Now my aunts are all mad at my mom because I didn’t marry my distant cousins. I guess they’re my first cousins, but they live far away.’ I told Zayd this was what had happened to my great-grandmother. She’d been sent back to Poland to marry one of her cousins, but instead she fell in love with my great-grandfather. We only learned this recently, when my aunt hired a rabbi to translate a box of my great-grandmother’s correspondence. One of the letters was from the cousin. You may not want to read this one, the rabbi told my aunt. He calls your grandma a bitch.

Our next lesson was all about parking. For some reason I kept reversing the wrong way. Zayd had to use the emergency brake five times. But he also said I should think about booking my test soon. In my excitement, I began to think of all the trips I could make. Would I go canoeing in the Adirondacks? Become the sort of person who spends their weekends at antique sales upstate? Would I do my shopping at that supermarket with the animatronic animals?

Zayd told me I should try to book my test as far from the city as possible. I thought of a high-school friend who failed his test multiple times. Eventually he had passed in Scarsdale, where the streets were empty. It made sense. Zayd recommended a testing site in Poughkeepsie, which was said to be especially easy. I called the Italian deli in Poughkeepsie, where I’d once eaten an incredible muffaletta sandwich as my friend broke up with his girlfriend on the side of the road, and asked if they had any halal options for Zayd. They said they could make anything with turkey. Heaven. I called the testing site – they told me they had a three-month waiting list but there was a spot at Yonkers the next afternoon. I asked Zayd. It wasn’t perfect, he said, but it was doable.

That evening I baked fifteen brown butter chocolate-chip cookies to give to the examiner. I’d given the same cookies to my neighbour, along with a note asking him to stop chain-smoking indoors, and it had worked. His adult son still thanks me for the cookies. He says they saved his father’s life. I make them for him every time the hallway starts to smell like a speakeasy.

‘You can’t bribe the examiner,’ Zayd said, when he came to pick me up.

‘Not even a cookie?’ He rolled his eyes and dragged on his vape.

On the drive up we ate some of the cookies, which Zayd conceded were a good bribe, and discussed his plan to become a state park officer: you got a cop’s salary and a gun but only had to protect birds and raccoons. ‘My friend is a regular cop,’ he said. ‘They’re disgusting. The whole precinct WhatsApp chat is just links to porn.’

In the line for the test, we watched a man get out of a BMW and switch places with his passenger, a gorgeous brunette, and give her a lingering kiss. We then watched the examiner, a man in his fifties, stare at the woman’s bottom before getting into the passenger’s seat to administer the test. ‘How is that allowed,’ I asked Zayd, ‘but I can’t give them a cookie?’ ‘Some people are lucky,’ he replied.

He explained that I wouldn’t know if I’d passed the test until 6 p.m. that evening because during Covid a number of badly behaved aspiring drivers had assaulted their examiners after failing. Others had returned to the testing site to ambush the examiners after work. One man kidnapped his examiner and refused to let him out of the car until he had given him a pass. Now the Department of Motor Vehicles makes examiners work across different test sites to avoid reprisals.

It was our turn. Zayd got out of the passenger seat and a woman got in. I complimented her blue top and she said she liked my sweater. Once we started driving, however, all friendliness fell away. I started bombarding her with pleasantries – this must be an interesting job? Has it rained this week? What happens if a student’s car smells? – but she just looked ahead, expressionless, and issued directions. I started sweating profusely. I looked over and saw she was reading her emails. Had I failed already or – maybe, possibly – had I already demonstrated my competence? Or was the mind game part of the test?

The examiner told me to do a three-point turn, so I began to pull over.

‘You don’t have room,’ she said.

I’d made much tighter turns in Manhattan, so I knew I did have room, but I deferred to her. She told me to put the car into reverse, which I did, but knowing I had room ahead of me, I decided to attempt the turn anyway. I accelerated, forgetting the car was in reverse. She pressed the emergency brake. ‘What the hell are you doing?’

Shit. I completed the turn – vindicated because I did in fact have room – and then parked reasonably well. She went back to her emails. Was this a definite fail? It was impossible to know, since she had to behave as though I might kidnap her. She told me to drive back to the start and exited the car without a word.

Zayd was waiting on the side of the road. I got out and he sat in the driver’s seat. As we drove away, I burst into tears. He handed me a cookie. ‘I knew you’d overthink it,’ he said. ‘It’s OK. We’ll book again for next week.’ Later that night, I logged into the DMV portal and discovered I’d passed with the maximum number of violations, including one Zayd had never seen before: ‘misuse of gears’ while driving an automatic vehicle. When I emailed the driving school the next month to book an appointment for my mother to regain her licence, Zayd was gone. I wondered if he’d been accepted as a state park officer, walking around Riverside Park, protecting the squirrels and turtles with a gun.
