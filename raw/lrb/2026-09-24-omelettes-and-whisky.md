# 正文

The notion​ that novelists don’t invent might be recent, yet George Eliot got tired of people pointing to the ‘original’ of Mr Casaubon, who was sometimes said to be Robert William Mackay, a rather pedantic associate of hers at the *Westminster Review*, or Dr Brabant, a pretentious family man of 62 who had wooed her decades earlier. Others said he was Mark Pattison, the rector of Lincoln College, Oxford, who was busy suffocating the young woman to whom he was married. The Pattisons corresponded warmly with Eliot and never admitted to the likeness. When a friend asked her directly whom she had drawn on for her desiccated, middle-aged scholar, Eliot, as her biographer Gordon Haight writes, ‘with a humorous solemnity … pointed to her own heart’.

A good biographer can quote the friends, visit the archives, animate the themes, lift the lid on the family romance and cross-examine the demons. But if the subject is a novelist, it is the manufacturing of the self that really matters. We hope the biographer will discover illicit correspondences and unknown sources, identifying patterns and materials that might explain what the novelist does and how she does it. In popular biography, we are well used to the ticker-tape parade of encomia, the dance of praise and awards, the flutter of newspaper cuttings and reviews, but the best literary biographers have a vision of their own that lights up the writer’s mind as well as her period. In such cases, it’s as if the subject is powered into coherence by a single source, the biographer’s imagination. Such perfect sublimation is hard to find. ‘Our social personality is created by the thoughts of other people,’ Proust wrote, and that is a crucial secret for the serious biographer, who wants to deliver a portrait of the whole life, inside and out – something the subject may have spent a lifetime scheming to avoid.

Before this biography, the name ‘Brookner’ probably brought to mind a succession of solitary women pining for love while waiting for death, an image that critics were quick to attach to the author of these women. Yet in Hermione Lee’s book we enter the life and work of someone else, a woman who was ‘dandyish, witty, controlled’, and who often sought in the shade what other writers may seek in the sunny uplands of a social group. ‘She is the *inventor* of those lonely women, not their alter ego,’ Lee writes, and yet, over the course of 24 novels and 87 years, Anita Brookner achieved many dislocations of selfhood, and may have become more and more like the people she dreamed into life. It’s a novelist’s fate, not so much that their characters are like them but that they become their characters. ‘Brookner’ was a thing to live up to, even for Anita Brookner, a formal elegance, a field of intelligence, which conditioned both her life and her art. Her affinity, as Lee tells us, was not with her Polish heritage but with France, and we feel it in her sentences, in the pleasure she affords in the patterns of discretion and the inner life of a culture. Brookner became so good at writing her own sentences that it hardly matters when one of her novels doesn’t entirely work, as many of them don’t. She continues to be in charge of her style, filled with the longing that all stylists have for a perfection beyond perfection, for a life that can’t be lived and a past that can’t be reached, while she tots up the fictions that keep us going.

In *Incidents in the Rue Laugier* (1995), Maud Gonthier, according to her daughter Maffy, had ‘elected to be a stranger’. Maud didn’t achieve the life she wanted in France and married an evasive Englishman, a kind and unsexy man who was always destined to do the right thing because he couldn’t do the essential thing, which was to make Maud love him. Maffy will never understand, but her mother married the wrong man, and all her life was unable to think of herself as an honourable person, not because she cheated on her husband, but because the man she really loved, a rake called David Tyler, did not love her back, and so she failed in exactly the same way as the man she settled for. These are the subtleties Brookner deals in, painful, tender and life-shaping, and at her best she choreographs them with the power of implication that we admire in Henry James. It is the essence of Brookner to show the kinds of discernment, the sort of noticing, that can cause life to be both wonderful and agonising at the same time. ‘On the way to Bryanston Square,’ we are told in *Falling Slowly* (1998), ‘she had picked up a bunch of anemones, as an adjunct to the asparagus quiche she had bought earlier.’ (Nobody else in British fiction would have used the word ‘adjunct’ in that way.) Brookner’s women, especially, know a thing or two about the effort to make life nicer than it might have been, and so we have Ruth, in Brookner’s first novel, *A Start in Life* (1981), wandering round Harrods buying leeks. It’s good that it’s Harrods, and good that she’s called Ruth, and good that she thinks her life has been destroyed by literature, but the real achievement lies in Brookner’s sense of the minuteness of living. In the midst of all this shopping and longing and putting emotional things off, Brookner’s characters begin to see who they are or, even worse, who they might never be. There’s often a carry-over from one generation to the next, as when Edith, in *Hotel du Lac* (1984), notices that ‘my mother’s fantasies, which remained unchanged all her life, taught me about reality.’

Some of Brookner’s fiction bulges with the art historian’s need to expatiate on form and philosophy, but it is in domestic detail that she best expresses her genius. In this way, she’s a cousin of Elizabeth Bowen, Rosamond Lehmann and Elizabeth Taylor, female writers who observe things that male writers almost never do, such as the surprisingly different valency of some emotions – the way a woman might feel, say, about a dead ex-husband as opposed to a dead father. Or a woman’s sense of whether she is wearing well, and whether it’s wise to wear brown in the autumn and navy in the spring. With Brookner, there’s also a modernist edge, which allows her stranded people to voice their moods and their frustrations by repetition, almost by rote. In *Brief Lives* (1990), Julia Wilberforce, an actress who lives chiefly on omelettes and whisky, is ‘a stickler for amusement, although she had absolutely no sense of humour: she wanted to be amused all the time.’ Brookner’s people have unnecessary habits of concealment, which fan out to become domestic routines and ways of living. Julia has a ‘habit of placing her underclothes on a chair in her bedroom and covering them with a square of silk reserved for this purpose’. Her friend Fay, who used to be a singer on the wireless, is almost dutifully wistful, behaving as if everyday disappointments were really the point of life, to be solved with a cup of tea and an aspirin. But we detect in Fay some of the loneliness that Brookner often spoke about in interviews: ‘There is a murky area in women which deepens once old alliances are invaded by a man.’ And later Fay says: ‘I was astonished at my own sadness. It was a sadness devoid of envy, or, if envy there was, it was only momentary. What I felt was my own vulnerability, which had done me such disservice in the past.’

It often occurs to me while reading Brookner’s novels that the thing most needed by her unhappy heroines is a novel by Anita Brookner. If only they had something relatable and preoccupying, the day might brighten. Fay in *Brief Lives*, who is having an affair with her frenemy’s husband, takes herself off for a disastrous week in Saint Paul de Vence, where she is bored, hot and bitten by mosquitoes. She is terribly lonely, of course, and (spiritually speaking) not so very unlike Winnie in Samuel Beckett’s *Happy Days*, up to her neck in sand and unfulfilled by all the men. What she needs to make her holiday nice is a procession of gin and tonics and a comprehending novel.

Brookner’s understanding of human frailty, as people sometimes call it, is not limited to women, and she wrote about male vulnerability better than any Englishman of her generation. Her men exude the delicacy that comes with a restricted sense of life, and they are subject to the terrors of time and disappointment every bit as much as their wives. Sofka’s children in *Family and Friends* (1985) are variously marked for difficulty, but the men are as delible in their own way as the women. They don’t get to be more vivid or more permanent just because they are men. ‘Her sons’, Brookner writes, who had ‘the legendary but short-lived handsomeness of those men who die young, are to establish themselves on the ruins of their father’s fortune’. And again: ‘Golden, indulgent fathers’, who awaken in their daughters ‘secret dreams of hard, fierce, brutal men. Fathers of infinite kindliness, easily moved to tears by their children’s beauty.’

Lee tells us that Brookner threw away her childhood photographs, ‘wishing to silence the “clamorous voices” they evoked’. But Lee notices the way these voices pop up everywhere in Brookner’s fiction and she investigates the sources of that clamour throughout the book. It seems that none of us can entirely escape the person we were meant to be. Brookner’s mother didn’t love her, that much is obvious, and in Lee’s construction – which is both more plausible and clearer than Brookner was able to state – she wished her daughter to be someone else. Brookner’s escape was France, or Francophile attitudes in general. After unmemorable times at school and college, she ended up on the staff of the Courtauld Institute. Though she would be edged out in her sixties, when someone asked Brookner what the place had given her, she replied: ‘A whole life, really.’ Some people don’t merely require a job, they require a design for living, and though she only began writing novels in her fifties, in the Courtauld she had already found the mise-en-scène of her self-consciousness. Halls full of gossip and weekend plans and bold comparisons and academic ambitions don’t suit everyone, but the artist in Brookner feasted there.

She was a connoisseur of confined spaces and secret affinities, so the Courtauld proved useful to her in several ways. It was, according to Lee, ‘a privileged, elitist, competitive and male-dominated environment’, filled, it seemed to others, with the sound of posh girls on the telephone and the echo of secrets. (It could hardly not be, with Anthony Blunt upstairs.) Brookner learned about the ‘struggle between reason and passion’, as Lee puts it, from the paintings of Jean-Baptiste Greuze and Jacques-Louis David. You get the impression that the sensualities and privations of Paris, where she lived in the 1950s while pursuing her PhD on Greuze, served as an apprenticeship for her as a creative writer, a matter she evoked nicely in [a piece about her Paris landladies](/the-paper/v19/n12/anita-brookner/mme-de-blazac-and-i), published in the *LRB* of 19 June 1997. The central landlady in the piece, Mme de Blazac, could easily be one of the over-extended Kensington ladies in a Brookner novel, and the speaker, Brookner herself, appears as someone lingering at the margins of her own fate. ‘In Mme de Blazac’s heartbroken company I was allowed to be sturdy, resilient and much older than I was accustomed to feeling … I was assured that [she] thought of me as a daughter.’

Brookner’s own love affairs could seem quiescent and unrequited – even when they were requited – such as that with John White, alias Wingco, a Spitfire instructor and professor of Renaissance art history she slept with in Paris. There were several affairs, possibly flings, but the names don’t really matter, because the headline news for Brookner as the 1960s approached was that she was needed at home to look after her parents. Lee argues that the relationship Brookner’s characters have with Paris is complicated. ‘All these characters,’ she writes, ‘are “rapt” with the sense of Paris as a place of escape, possibilities and self-transformation. There they can be “anonymous”, “accountable to no one”, “weightless, impalpable and suddenly free”. The city answers to their “secret desire to escape all forms of control”.’ But in the way of fiction-making and fiction-surviving, Brookner’s people can also be blighted by the things they wished for and the illusions they gave in to. ‘For Maud, in *Incidents in the Rue Laugier*, who has lost her chance of happiness there, “Paris retreated from her memory as though it had never been. It had betrayed her: that was her impression.”’ Later on, Lee reads into Brookner’s novels a regret at ever having fallen for France. ‘Perhaps it’s better to stay at home in the dark of London, accepting, as one elderly woman does, that “I shall never see Paris again.”’

Nothing much happens. And that can become a life. For thirty years, she wrote about her painters, she worked at the Courtauld and she did her teaching, but you might argue that the self that mattered to Brookner, and now matters to us, was held in abeyance. ‘As the era of missed chances became more distant,’ Lee writes, ‘her tone became less poignant and more trenchant.’ She said she was too absent-minded to get married and too anxious to have children. Such determinations can be crucial for certain novelists. Lee resists saying so, but there was an element in Brookner’s aloneness that was not only perfect for her prose, but a kindred expression of something deep in her characters. ‘For the happy few,’ Brookner writes at the conclusion of her book on David, ‘art and life are indistinguishable.’ A novelist does not become less fallible by writing about fallibility, but she might be able to make it golden nonetheless, despite a lack of glitter.

No matter how much she drew from her past, Brookner’s real genius is for foreshadowing what a person in her shoes could become, and that is really the sense in which she is a biographical novelist. When it comes to Mrs Pusey, in *Hotel du Lac*, we feel the pressure of futurity. ‘With some people (I know them well),’ Brookner writes in that book,

> the shadow of their death precedes them; they lose hope, appetite, viability. They feel the meaning of their lives draining away, and they recognise that they have lost, or never attained, their heart’s desire, and they give up. In the eyes of such people one reads dreadful recognition, the ultimate self-knowledge: I have not lived enough and it is too late to redeem myself.

The great engine of Lee’s empathetic power as a biographer is that she can make you see, from one end of her subject’s life to the other, the absolute value of that romantic impulse. In Brookner she finds, as much as she found in Virginia Woolf or Edith Wharton, a font of ‘desire and longing, ideal standards and painful disappointment’, such as can make a lifelong narrative.

There are parts of the story, of course, that only the novels can take up, the business of daily life being a process of deliberate omission for someone like Brookner. Even friends, who are fairly celebrated in this book, could be omitted when it suited her, and it caused some of them more pain than Lee finds herself willing to record. Shyness, embarrassment, reticence: they can be a form of aggression with some people, and, whatever regrets she had, Brookner preferred the world her way, which is the reason that the terrifying self-encirclement of her best characters is so completely authentic. She had an almost anorectic understanding of her own desire and the powers involved in containing it. In *Providence* (1982), Kitty Maule loves a charming English lecturer, Maurice, and at one moment she quietly expresses that love by giving up her own appetite for his.

> ‘Are you going to eat your potatoes, Kitty?’ She handed over her plate. His physical presence so bemused her that her own awakened appetite seemed subsumed into his. He was eating for them both, and that was how she would have it. To feed him, at that moment, was all she wanted to do; the food was enhanced by his enjoyment of it and she speared a potato from his plate because it looked so much more appetising than when it had previously featured on her own. A man at the next table, eating alone, smiled faintly at her absorption; she caught his eye, and smiled back. Across Maurice’s bent head they looked at each other gravely and with complete understanding. I love him, you see, she said, without uttering a word.

This is a key moment, in which desire and personhood are subsumed via consumption in ways that might not have seemed plausible for the lonely women in Victorian novels. Brookner’s women, whether feminists or not, are shaped by the changing values of mid-century Europe and America, by developments in family planning and housing, money and employment, such that the women in her books (as much as in the novels of Muriel Spark and Edna O’Brien) are not only thinking and working but shopping and fucking, with the added element, in Brookner’s case, of living in an England where there is no such thing as society. In many ways, Brookner’s characters are stranded with the problem of themselves. It wasn’t Martin Amis or Iris Murdoch who was the great existentialist of the Thatcher period, but Brookner, a writer much closer to the dark edge of Europe.

Miriam Sharpe, a translator who features in *Falling Slowly*, tries to figure out how to live after a failed marriage. In ‘a delirium of ennui’, she is looking for transcendence. The English obsession with keeping up appearances is saving her and killing her at the same time, and Brookner, perhaps more in this novel than ever before, feels like a new kind of exemplary voice, British and female, alone and compelled by a love that cannot be found and a life that can scarcely be lived. ‘How had she,’ Brookner writes of Miriam,

> a not unintelligent woman in the late 20th century, when women were supposed to know everything, come to this? But her part was clear; she was committed. She would manage, somehow; there was no sense in which she would be found wanting. She would go home, have a hot bath, wash her hair. And then she would go to bed, and begin her period of waiting. And no one but herself would know what it cost.

As a reader, you feel that bath is something she knows, as well as that period of waiting. ‘Brookner is the opposite of a polemical writer,’ Lee observes. ‘The women of her novels don’t have an ideology or a support system to resort to. They are alone with their dilemmas … they don’t belong to any group, though they are placed inside social situations or institutions that involve watching other people … They all want more than love: they want trust, tenderness, full knowledge and acceptance.’

Brookner’s​ depression should not be underestimated. And neither should her strategies for surviving it and making her ideas legible. Next to a writer such as Woolf, she left almost no materials behind – no diaries, no abundance of letters, no *Hyde Park Gate News*, no Bloomsbury – and Lee has to apply all her skills to identify the spark that could still be struck in the gloom. In her biography of Woolf, Lee records a visit Woolf made in May 1929 to have her eyes tested. The oculist told her that she wasn’t as young as she used to be, which gave Woolf an acute sense of ageing, and her thoughts turned to suicide. ‘How best to grow old, how best to die?’ Lee asks, before quoting Woolf writing in her diary, where, despite her mood and her disappointment, ‘she became again, not an elderly woman, but simply “a woman … as I always am when I write”.’ Lee has always been brilliant on the ways that dark materials can shape and define a writer. In her biography of Wharton, we find the novelist beset, at the close of 1913, with anxieties, difficulties, loneliness. ‘Henry James was finding her troubling and alarming,’ Lee reports. And then she quotes from Wharton’s account of a dream she had, about black demons dragging a trunk, which opened to reveal ‘a succession of limp black squirming things … that seemed to writhe about as they fell at my feet’. Wharton knew what they were: ‘the hideous, the incredible things that had happened to me in this dreadful year, or were to happen to me before its close’. Once the demons have dragged everything out, Wharton, in the dream, looks into the empty trunk and says, ‘Are you sure it hasn’t a false bottom?’

Brookner wrote novels about people living through the worst, hoping for the best, and surviving both things across a lifetime. ‘A chronic dreamer herself,’ Lee writes, ‘[she] loves to use dreams in novels as symptoms of inner trouble. Reviewing an anthology of dreams in 1986, she says that writers don’t dream about their fictional characters, but may “dream their dreams for them”. She knows all too well how “the oppression of dreams … can cast a shadow of illness on to the following day.”’ In a rather moving way, each of Brookner’s later novels could be titled ‘The Following Day’; they are small, sometimes perfect treatises on unfathomable longing and the pressure of imminence. Each novel is a lesson in all of them; each book is a lost map of the backwoods of the others. Brookner could repeat herself: the phrase ‘eternal vigilance was the price of liberty,’ said of Miriam in *Falling Slowly*, appears again in the mind of another character haunted by dreams, Julius Herz in *The Next Big Thing* (2002), a 73-year-old man who thinks that ‘perhaps eternal restlessness was the answer, just as eternal vigilance was the price of liberty.’ By this stage, the clue is in the title: Brookner had made her way to naming the experience that is sadder than death – life. What happens in a minute, the next big thing, is a cousin to James’s ‘distinguished thing’ – death itself – but in Brookner’s imagination the next big thing can’t be worse. ‘It was their lives rather than their deaths that were regrettable,’ Herz says, thinking of his parents, ‘and all the frustrated love that had failed to sweeten their end.’ Meanwhile, he has to fill his day. And he does so with caution but not without humour, a ripe mix for a novel.

‘Women disappear all the time,’ Brookner writes in *Fraud* (1992), ‘sometimes because they want to.’ One imagines that such truths caught up with her, defining her old age in ways that even she wasn’t quite equipped to deal with. As with all romantics, time didn’t agree with her, except on the canvas, where passion and reason could be gloriously present, making the sort of sense to her that her own life didn’t. To me, and as Hermione Lee proves, it is the stuff of pure biography. No multiple marriages or plunges from high windows, no madness at the Savoy or high jinks at the Plaza, but the material of everyday survival and true sentences. Lunch with Anita would last for 75 minutes, involving a small piece of fish and two cigarettes – the good old days – before a single coffee and a taxi home. Some would consider it a poor life, but in many ways it was the richest, and British in ways she might never have considered. She woke every day at 5 a.m. and walked to the newsagent for papers and cigarettes. She would go back out for provisions at Waitrose or Marks & Spencer. Late on, she did some voluntary work at the Royal Brompton Hospital in Sydney Street, pushing the tea trolley between the beds and offering books from her stack. By this point, Brookner had become her own creation to the extent that nothing could touch her. In her sensible shoes, with her lacquered hair, cashmere sweater and prim skirt, Miss Brookner was unknown to everyone but herself, helping the patients before going home to silence.
