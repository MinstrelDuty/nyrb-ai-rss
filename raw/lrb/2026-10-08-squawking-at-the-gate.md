# 正文

The modern zoo​ is nearly two centuries old. The zoological gardens in Regent’s Park were established in 1828 by the inventor Humphry Davy and the colonial adventurer Stamford Raffles, founder of modern Singapore. It was a fitting collaboration: encompassing the globe and studying its minutiae were different aspects of the same imperial ambition. Chartered by George IV, London Zoo started out as a private research establishment; so, a decade later, did Artis, its equivalent in Amsterdam, the motto of which remains *Natura Artis Magistra*, ‘Nature is the Teacher of Art’. Berlin Zoo followed in 1844, thanks to Friedrich Wilhelm IV of Prussia, who donated animals from his father’s collection on the Pfaueninsel at Wannsee. The old king had been inspired by the Ménagerie du Jardin des Plantes in Paris. The French model had its origins in Louis XIV’s menagerie at Versailles, as well as the arena at the Château de Vincennes, where wild animals provided aristocratic entertainment, but it emerged from the turmoil of the French Revolution and became a site of serious scientific inquiry as well as popular amusement.

For centuries, European monarchs had kept exotic creatures as emblems of their wealth and power. French royal menageries dated back to the 13th century, and some of London Zoo’s first residents came from the Tower of London, where King John employed lion-keepers as early as 1210. John moved the royal menagerie from Woodstock Palace in Oxford; it had been formed a century earlier by Henry I, probably as a hunting reserve. The London menagerie began to flourish during the reign of Henry III, when the Holy Roman Emperor sent a gift of three more big cats. Further diplomatic offerings followed. In the 1250s, the lions were joined by a polar bear, courtesy of the king of Norway, and an elephant from the king of France. The elephant was dead within a couple of years and the bear didn’t last long either, though according to legend, it did get to swim in the Thames on a chain. Between the 14th and 16th centuries, scores of doomed specimens arrived, including leopards, lynxes, wildcats, jackals, bears and birds of prey. James I was given an elephant by Philip IV of Spain with instructions that it was to drink only wine between autumn and spring. James, who was partial to majestic spectacle, ordered the renovation of the Tower to enhance the menagerie, including the installation of a viewing platform from which visitors could watch animals being tormented. An excavation in 1937 revealed their scattered bones.

Visitors to the Tower in the 18th century could either pay a few pence or stick with the Elizabethan token of admission: a cat or dog to feed to the lions. By 1800 the menagerie boasted bears, monkeys, wolves, an elephant, a tiger and a baboon, although a generation later most of the cages were empty. As in all menageries, the mortality rate necessitated a vigorous acquisition policy; the idea of breeding in captivity lay far in the future. At the time of the transfer to Regent’s Park, the inventory was expanding under the direction of Alfred Cops, a showman as well as head keeper, whose passion for animals survived his near asphyxiation by a boa constrictor.

In June 1793, the Jardin des Plantes was renamed the Muséum national d’histoire naturelle by the National Convention, the constituent assembly of the new republic. Richard Burkhardt’s *Leopard in the Garden* is a detailed account of the first 45 years of this furiously modernising institution, charting its successes and setbacks, noble ideals and unintended outcomes. At more than seven hundred pages long, it’s hard not to feel that a slimmer, more dynamic book is trying to get out. The copious records in the Archives Nationales are a monument to the way the museum made a zoo out of a menagerie, and Burkhardt has uncovered many forgotten stories, including some focused on specific species (the gnu, the beaver, the seal, the electric eel), but, however interesting, these inevitably impede the narrative momentum.

His account begins on 16 October 1793, with Marie Antoinette’s tumbril trundling across the Place de la Révolution. Nearby, the showman Dominique Marchini was displaying his small circus – polar bear, leopard, monkey, civet – to the crowds. Marchini was already struggling to compete with the draw of the guillotine; three weeks later, the Paris Commune confiscated his animals as a threat to public safety and gave them to the new museum. The professors who had been installed there were good with animals – so long as they were dissected and bottled, or stuffed and mounted in glass cases. In the name of liberty and fraternity, however, they were encouraged to broaden their aims and, as Burkhardt puts it, ‘to civilise specimens and citizens at the same time’.

This was a tall order, and Marchini’s quartet was not a promising beginning. The addition of a tiger, two mandrills, three eagles and another polar bear widened the taxonomic scope. It wasn’t unusual for the curators to arrive each morning to find crates of growling, squawking animals dumped at the gate. The opportunities were obvious, but with them came the worry of where to put the beasts and how to keep them alive. Marchini’s leopard ate six pounds of meat a day, at considerable expense, yet still died a year after its relocation. By this time, the museum’s holdings included a raccoon, a baboon, a panther, a mountain goose and a cockatoo. The mammals in particular seem not to have been happy in their strange new home. Visitors warned that the replacement leopard would ‘launch its urine towards those who stare at it’.

As the sans-culottes and men of the National Guard stomped through the palaces of the mighty, yet more exotic animals were declared state property and dispatched to the museum. Pickings were especially rich at Versailles and at the castle of the former prince de Condé in Chantilly. The curators were instructed to free the museum from its decadent past, uniting, in the words of the naturalist Bernard Germain de Lacépède, ‘the pride of the dominator, the curiosity of the scientist and the commitment of the citizen’. Like the republic itself, the new museum marked the start of the next stage of France’s destiny.

Shifting the menagerie from plaything of the rich to locus of scientific inquiry went beyond political grandstanding. In Jacobean England, Francis Bacon had attempted something similar; the Royal Society, founded in 1660, badgered the colonist John Winthrop to report on the exploitable fauna of the New World. But research was piecemeal, contested and narrowly focused. Even after 1700, there remained more academic interest in anatomy than in behaviour, which, to be useful, the botanist Jacques-Henri Bernardin de Saint-Pierre argued, required animals to be studied in their natural habitats. Only then could mankind appreciate their place in God’s creation. The religious aims of Renaissance natural philosophers continued to colour the humanist landscape of the Enlightenment, but if these were Ancien Régime notions, they did not offend the National Convention. The work of Rousseau and others had convinced the deputies that the contemplation of nature would improve moral stature and shape productive citizens.

The increase in pace and volume of work at the museum called for systems of transportation, registration, classification, accommodation and nutrition, plus – given poor life expectancy – taxidermy and pathology. Curators had to lobby for money, of which there was never enough, recruit competent staff, sequester libraries from dissolved religious houses and, critically, extend the cramped premises. In September 1794 the Commission of Public Works approved plans for a compound to house monkeys, birds, bears and big cats, although it took some time for the scheme to be completed. Costs were kept down by refurbishing old buildings and even utilising fixtures from the Versailles menagerie. The space also needed to be made fit for visitors. An architect was commissioned to design signage, including a warning not to throw brickbats or bottles at the animals. To further promote civility, the curators were obliged to deliver public lectures on everything from agriculture and mineralogy to animal husbandry and insect reproduction. There were classes in drawing creatures great and small, even the traditionally detested toads and snakes.

Much of the drama of Burkhardt’s story concerns men driven by curiosity but overwhelmed by duties, making the best of a fraught, sometimes ridiculous situation. Yet they made great advances. The history of science is rarely more satisfying than when a maverick impulse or happy accident knocks a fixed paradigm into a new way of seeing. After Philippe Égalité, duc d’Orléans, was beheaded in November 1793, Jean-Baptiste Lamarck and Étienne Geoffroy Saint-Hilaire visited his estate at Raincy, east of the capital. They found not only deer, sheep, goats, swans and partridges, but parrots, a water buffalo, an English bull and a camel. This was the easy part; more difficult was arranging for the creatures to be carted to Paris, quartered, fed and watered, examined and catalogued. What sustained Lamarck and his colleagues was the conviction that behind the chaos lay a scientific, republican ideal. ‘Nothing would be spared to produce this microcosm of the whole of nature,’ Burkhardt writes, ‘just as a regenerated France would be the microcosm of a new moral world.’ The shelf life of some utopian aspirations was short, but there were lasting scientific gains. Lamarck’s service at the museum convinced him that organisms flourish by inheriting biological traits of benefit to their ancestors. His *Philosophie zoologique*, which questioned the biblical account of creation, was published in 1809, the year Darwin was born.

This spirit of modernity would influence zoos for generations. Antoine Claire Thibaudeau, an official reporting to several revolutionary committees, likened the menageries of old to corrupt prisons such as the Bastille, whose inmates displayed ‘the physiognomy of sadness’. Menageries ought instead ‘to be constructed so that the animals, of whatever species, enjoy all the liberty consistent with the safety of the spectators’. Nature was the foundation not only of good government and justice but of human happiness. Dialogue between reformed conceptions of society and the zoo energised a mutually sustaining ideology. It was no longer acceptable for the lion to be king of the beasts because, as the museum’s director, Louis Jean-Marie Daubenton, commented, ‘there are no kings in nature.’ Lectures riffing on this aphorism earned rapturous applause – a reception that, so the story goes, perplexed the elderly Daubenton. Burkhardt is sceptical, and argues that the professor knew exactly what he was doing: aligning his museum’s ethos with republican doctrine in order to secure its survival and independence, even against the backdrop of the Terror.

The proletarianised lion was the only carnivore admitted in the museum’s first six months to survive until 1795. Yet lions kept coming, sourced in North Africa and shipped to Paris, as did war booty in the form of bears and elephants. Inadequate conditions and diets were to blame for the alarming death rate. One visitor complained about the ‘boxes of stone’ in which the animals were kept. The keepers did what they could, but wages and morale were pitiful. A constant supply of cadavers to dissect in the anatomy classes – including, in 1794-95, a dromedary, a panther, two eagles, two apes and a baboon – was little consolation.

Daubenton’s death on 1 January 1800 marked the end of an era, but the museum’s financial woes persisted. By then, Napoleon had seized power and appointed his brother Lucien Bonaparte as minister of the interior. Lucien instructed the museum to charge entrance fees, but the curators refused. The museum’s only leverage with the government was a veiled threat about public relations: for animal avatars of patriotic victory to die from neglect was not a good look. The animals died anyway, with the plight of the polar bears especially striking. They acquired muscular tics from confinement, overheated in the Parisian climate and, fed on bread rather than fish, became severely obese. Some went blind. In 1802 a distressed elephant, who had embarrassed keepers by what seems to have been habitual masturbation, possibly due to anxiety, dropped dead. The museum officials did not admit liability, though they did publish the results of a postmortem and sought to comfort his grieving mate by introducing her to a camel.

A subsequent ‘programme for a national menagerie’ proposed a unified layout of enclosures and passageways tailored to the needs of herbivores, carnivores, primates, birds, reptiles and lizards. Punctuated by gardens and connected by elegant footpaths, this zoological paradise was as appealing as it was unaffordable. The construction of a classical rotunda for *les animaux féroces* was postponed in 1805; it was finally completed in 1814, and Parkie the elephant was encouraged to see it as her new home. Parkie, set in her ways, wasn’t convinced. Lions, tigers and bears, languishing in poky cages, did not receive new quarters until 1821.

As Burkhardt shows, the museum was a barometer of France’s difficulties as well as a flagship for its ideals. The invasion of Russia in 1812, and the subsequent economic crisis, pushed the programme for a national zoo far down the First Empire’s list of priorities. The restored Bourbon monarchy retained many of Napoleon’s reforms – a centralised bureaucracy, an equitable legal code and an efficient tax system – but failed to deliver political stability. Demographic and industrial growth was insignificant until later in the century. Yet the museum survived, and remained committed to the pursuit of knowledge.

That the animals could be viewed by all citizens, at least from 1804, was itself progressive. London Zoo opened its gates to the public only in 1847 when running costs forced its hand, almost twenty years after its foundation. The same was true of Amsterdam Zoo in 1851. Both were instantly popular. Dublin Zoo welcomed visitors from its inception in 1831, at a shilling a pop and a penny on Sundays. When the fee was waived to mark Queen Victoria’s coronation, twenty thousand people flocked to Phoenix Park. Bristol Zoo, established by Dr Henry Riley and the local zoological society in 1836 for the purpose of ‘rational amusement’, delighted many generations with rides on camels and elephants. By 1900 there were public zoos all over Europe, from Leeds (1840) to Leipzig (1876), Brussels (1851) to Budapest (1866). The engine of proliferation was competition, between nations and between cities vying for the status that a zoo conferred. Zoos sprang up in the US soon after the Civil War, often incorporated into programmes of urban rationalisation and renewal guided by bourgeois ideals of space, variety, education and access to nature. At every zoo, though, the enthusiasm of visitors was the sustaining factor.

Public attitudes to animals are indicative of wider mentalities. For centuries our ancestors revelled in showmen parading wretched creatures from one market square to another, usually a muzzled bear, taught by the whip to dance. Marchini in Paris no doubt had a routine for his captive cast, and audiences gathered to grin and jeer and throw a few sous into his hat. Britain’s best-known circus dates to 1683, when James Chipperfield’s performing animals delighted Londoners at the ‘great frost fair’. In the early 19th century, an arcade in the Strand housed a menagerie where for two shillings you could see creatures including a lynx, a kangaroo, emus, sea lions and snakes. In 1826 an elephant, maddened by musth, ran amok and was shot. Elsewhere, gamblers bet on contests between brutalised dogs and prize cockerels fitted with razor-sharp spurs. Bulls and rats were baited, and games in which geese and foxes and badgers were tortured to death were commonplace. Cruelty was acceptable in a world where felons were publicly hanged, burned and mutilated. People predisposed to relish man’s dominion over nature – ‘over every creeping thing that creepeth upon the earth’ (Genesis 1:26) – were both inured to the mistreatment of animals and secure in its divine justification.

Animals were characterised according to the human scheme of values. They might be noble or generous, or wantonly cruel, even murderous. Bears in particular excited extreme emotions and were often mistreated by visitors, who tortured them with food dangled on fishhooks or tried to choke them with nooses. In 1813, a European brown bear called Martin killed a museum guard who had entered his enclosure to retrieve a five-franc piece. Seven years later, a visitor ‘swung his legs over the parapet and dropped into the pit’, in what was taken to be a suicide attempt. It’s not clear whether he died from the fall or from Martin’s attack. Parisians were fascinated because Martin was intelligent and dextrous, yet unflinchingly violent and remorseless – a sort of ursine Hannibal Lecter. In a published fable of the day, Martin stands before a tribunal of animal peers, who fear they will all be made to atone for his crimes.

The museum’s curators conferred human attributes on animals as part of their search for correspondences in nature. As Burkhardt puts it, ‘anthropomorphism and anthropocentrism intertwined.’ The question of liberty, its contradictions and glories, exercised minds at the museum. The ‘stigma of slavery’ that Thibaudeau condemned in royal menageries was a nagging concern: the animals were kept against their will, often in degrading conditions. In the 1790s, the head librarian, Georges Toscan, and his colleagues, feeling guilty about a despondent lion, confected a romantic melodrama about the lion’s friendship with a dog. Like other pets, dogs were much loved, but were perhaps also unjustly subjected to lives of servitude. Frédéric Cuvier, superintendent from 1803 (and a pioneering student of the interplay between instinct and habituation, cited by Darwin), proposed in a study of seals that social animals were best suited to domestication, yet fretted about their ‘enslavement’.

Cuvier was most interested in orangutans and, unlike Geoffroy Saint-Hilaire, focused on behaviour rather than physiology. ‘Orangutan’ was a Malay term meaning ‘man of the woods’, befitting a clever being that native peoples, according to the Dutch naturalist Jacobus Bontius, believed could speak but chose to keep silent in case humans enslaved it. Cuvier didn’t meet an orangutan until 1808, when one was given to Joséphine Bonaparte; he concluded that although it wasn’t human, it came close. The implication was that orangutans were some kind of biological cousin to man rather than an entirely separate species – not that Cuvier had any truck with the concept of evolution. He noted the orangutan’s capacity to learn, abide by generalising rules and solve practical problems, and was impressed by its displays of amusement, sadness, anger and frustration, although he stopped short of attributing to it self-awareness or aesthetic sense. Like all savages, he wrote, orangutans were unmoved by music. Another specimen, purchased at enormous cost in 1836 (3500 francs, almost twice Cuvier’s salary) and called Jack, was capable of playing games, using a spoon, wearing hats and wiping dressing from salad leaves with a napkin.

Jack’s demeanour divided opinion: some visitors loved him; others were offended by his ‘repulsive bestiality’ and the suggestion that he was ‘a degenerated child of Adam’. When he fell sick during his first winter, he was bled and purged with enemas, but to no avail. Such losses were supposed to be mitigated by the construction of a ‘palace of the monkeys’ in 1837, a vast cage complete with a fountain and stoves to provide heat. The new building came too late for Jacqueline the chimpanzee – part-acrobat, part-ballerina, coquettish, patient with children, and according to the journalist Samuel-Henri Berthoud, ‘nearly speaking’. In fact, like the rest of the museum, the monkey palace was sustained through replacement rather than preservation. Between July and December 1838, no fewer than fifteen primates died. That autumn, a bereaved kangaroo became depressed, a lion succumbed to a mystery illness and war broke out between the Australian and Asiatic cassowary birds. The most successful species remained the brown rat, which thrived throughout the zoo, immune to extermination and truly at liberty.

The abundance of the natural world meant that conservation formed no part of a zoo’s raison d’être in the 18th and 19th centuries. There were plenty of animals to be found in the wild; the problem was keeping them alive in captivity for long enough to study them. Over the last century that dichotomy has inverted, with the purpose of zoos now largely the preservation of species endangered or extinct outside their walls.
