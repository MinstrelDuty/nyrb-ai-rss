# 正文

For the second time​ in five years, Britain is experiencing an energy crisis. The political debate has coalesced around two proposed solutions. The first, advocated by the ‘Drill, baby, drill’ contingent, favours fast-tracking new oil and gas licences and reducing taxes on fossil fuel companies. Its proponents point to the Norwegian energy system as a model, but don’t suggest setting up a majority state-owned company like Equinor or bringing in export controls on oil and gas. That the UK’s remaining fossil fuel reserves in the North Sea are inadequate to demand is often ignored.

Supporters of the second solution believe that Britain should focus on scaling up renewable forms of electricity generation, such as wind and solar power. This overlooks the fact that electricity and total energy use are not the same thing. Even if Britain were to produce all its electricity from renewables, the country won’t be able to escape the influence of fossil fuels as long as 85 per cent of UK homes continue to rely on gas central heating. This camp, with a quixotic commitment to globalised energy capitalism, also claims that any new domestic production won’t affect our energy prices because oil and gas are traded on a global market. But many countries do control exports and prices. Renewables boosters want to see increased subsidies to private sector electricity generators (especially wind and solar farms), but have no enthusiasm for public ownership. Britain’s political class remains unified in its belief that energy infrastructure must be owned by the private sector. Since privatisation in the 1980s, successive leaders have rejected any suggestion of nationalisation, despite the scale of the crisis facing the British energy industry. The Burnham government may mark a turning point. It has promised to set out plans for ‘stronger public control’ of essential services, though the relationship between control and ownership has yet to be defined.

In January 2009, a border dispute between Ukraine and Russia led to a rapid rise in European gas prices. Ed Miliband, then energy secretary under Gordon Brown, went on the *Today* programme to reassure listeners that Britain had a ‘diverse range of sources where our gas comes from, which is the most important thing this dispute teaches us’. By this he meant gas entered Britain from the North Sea fields and on tankers carrying liquefied natural gas, mostly from Qatar and Algeria. A few months later, Miliband approved a National Strategy for Climate and Energy, which pitched renewables as a way to ‘improve the security of Britain’s energy supplies’ and claimed that the ‘diversity of our gas supplies helped the UK to remain largely unaffected by the Russia-Ukraine dispute’.

Others were concerned about the potential impact on prices of future shocks. In 2010, the energy industry regulator, Ofgem, concluded a major study into the resilience of the system. One of the scenarios it tested was a Russia-Ukraine conflict. The resulting report, Project Discovery, warned that ‘consumer bills rise in all scenarios due to the levels of new investment required … and especially so if oil and gas … prices spike sharply.’ Project Discovery was swept under the carpet then and is almost never mentioned today. To remember it is to admit that the British state lacked the capacity to do anything about the crisis it correctly predicted would happen.

The energy crisis of 2021-23 was much more serious than that of 2009, although in its early stages politicians were keen to tell the public that there was nothing to be worried about. In September 2021, the business secretary, Kwasi Kwarteng, dismissed concerns as ‘alarmist, unhelpful and completely misguided’, assuring Parliament that Britain had ‘a diverse range of gas supply sources’. As the scale of price hikes became clear, the government desperately tried to pull every lever, even contemplating buying gas itself. This proved too complex an undertaking; the civil service had no experience of buying gas in bulk. As Britain’s privately-owned gas system operator put it: ‘The underlying market arrangements in the UK are predicated on the basis that the market will provide.’ Trying to get financial support to the households that needed it most was particularly difficult, because the IT systems holding data on welfare beneficiaries weren’t linked to those of the energy companies.

In the end, the government threw money at the problem. Energy suppliers were told to pay whatever was necessary, all consumer bills were capped and the state picked up the bill – at least £40 billion. In the aftermath there were recriminations and calls for reform. But most of these initiatives failed, scuppered by the complexity of the task and lobbying by energy companies. The IT systems still aren’t linked up. The way electricity and gas markets operate remains the same.

Britain has some of the highest electricity prices in the world. It is convenient for the industry to blame this on Russia’s invasion of Ukraine, but electricity prices have been rising above the rate of inflation since 2003. Between 2000 and 2019, prices for residential customers increased by 80 per cent. Only a handful of countries had greater price increases than the UK in this period. The energy industry and political commentators debate the reasons for this ad nauseam. Some blame it on renewables, claiming that even if wind farms are cheaper to run than gas-fired power stations, they require lots of electricity network in remote places and lots of battery storage. Others blame fossil fuels, claiming that the real issue is Britain’s reliance on gas. According to the rules of the wholesale market, the price of electricity is set by the most expensive source needed to meet demand. In Britain that source is gas around 60 per cent of the time, down from around 90 per cent five years ago. Many other countries have the same approach to electricity pricing. What differs is the kind of power that sets the price. In France it is usually nuclear, and in Norway, hydro. Britain scrapped its coal-fired power stations and has little flexible hydro or nuclear, so the last power station called on is almost always gas.

There are elements of truth to both positions. A third of the increase in the average electricity bill between 2016 and 2026 was due to the increase in gas prices. But another third was due to increased subsidies for generators (resulting in part from higher interest rates and the growing cost of equipment and labour). A further 20 per cent was due to rising electricity network charges following a wave of construction after decades of underinvestment. Whatever the reasons for rising bills, the bigger problem for renewables is trust. For at least a decade, the British public has been told that renewables are cheap and will bring down bills. Between 2000 and 2025, the share of electricity generated by renewable and clean sources (wind, solar, hydroelectric, nuclear) rose from 24 per cent to 50 per cent. But this has failed to translate into stable or reduced electricity prices.

The shift to renewable electricity generation won’t reduce electricity prices in the short term. When Labour was elected in 2024, one of its top priorities was to reach ‘clean power by 2030’. This means the electricity industry has to build a huge amount of infrastructure very quickly. Privatisation in the 1980s established the principle that all costs must be recovered from the consumer. Until very recently, even the subsidies given to generators of renewable energy came entirely from consumers rather than from the Treasury (the 2025 budget moved roughly a fifth of total renewable subsidies onto general taxation for the next three years, but the rest still falls on bills). In other countries, including France, Norway and China, the state picks up more of the tab – energy infrastructure is publicly owned and subsidies are paid from general taxation.

Britain’s energy strategy over the last two decades has been predicated on the assumption that generating electricity from renewables is cheaper than generating it from fossil fuels. ‘Decarbonisation’ was supposed to drive down prices and encourage consumers to switch from using fossil fuels for driving and heating. This hasn’t happened. One important metric is the share of final energy demand serviced by electricity: it has barely increased over the last two decades, going from 18.4 per cent in to 21 per cent in 2023. Between 2000 and 2019 electricity consumption per capita actually fell by 22 per cent. Only Yemen, Zimbabwe, Jamaica, Tajikistan and Syria have had greater drops.

Rising electricity prices have undermined electrification. Electricity is harder to deliver than gas and thus more expensive, but how much more expensive – what is known as the ‘spark gap’ – is an important factor when households or companies decide whether or not to electrify. The UK has one of the widest spark gaps in Europe. In the three years after the last energy crisis, the gap grew so the incentive for consumers to switch to electric vehicles and heat pumps weakened. Domestic central heating is where Britain is most exposed to fossil fuel shocks, and consumer demand for gas in winter could be exploited by a hostile state. Despite this, the use of heat pumps remains low. The Climate Change Committee has said heat pump installations need to rise from around 60,000 in 2023 to nearly 450,000 by 2030 and 1.5 million by 2035. The government recently reduced its 2028 target. It is impossible to reconcile this with its claim that Britain needs to ‘get off the fossil fuel rollercoaster’.

If electricity demand does not rise significantly soon, the price of electricity could go up even more than expected. As Britain shifts to renewables, an ever increasing share of the system costs is fixed, remaining the same however much electricity is consumed. If demand undershoots government projections, these fixed costs will be divided over fewer units of electricity. Britain could enter a spiral of falling demand and rising prices.

Today’s​ industry has its roots in Thatcherism. Between 1986 and 1995 the state-owned enterprises the oversaw the energy system, such as the Central Electricity Generating Board and the British Gas Corporation, were broken up and sold off. ‘I do not see the government’s task as being to try to plan the future shape of energy production and consumption,’ the energy secretary, Nigel Lawson, said in 1982. ‘It is not even primarily to try to balance UK demand and supply for energy. Our task is rather to set a framework which will ensure that the market operates in the energy sector with a minimum of distortion, and energy is produced and consumed efficiently.’

Energy policy stuck closely to this philosophy in the 1990s and 2000s, under John Major and Tony Blair, but subsequent governments abandoned it. Major reforms in the mid-2010s under David Cameron introduced subsidies for renewables and for gas-fired power stations. The measures were supposed to be temporary, even before Cameron promised to cut ‘the green crap’, but renewables companies have opposed their removal, claiming that without financial support their power stations wouldn’t be profitable enough, and therefore wouldn’t get built.

The principal form of subsidy for new renewables generators is the contract for difference, which guarantees a fixed ‘strike price’ for fifteen to twenty years. It is sold as protection for the consumer against volatile prices, and because the generator repays the difference whenever the market price runs above the strike price, it can be presented that way. But its real purpose is to protect the generator. Renewables have no fuel costs, so they bid into the market at close to zero, and because they generate at the same time – when the wind blows, for example – they drive down the wholesale price at the very moment they are most productive. Wind cannibalises wind: each new wind farm erodes the price the others earn. The contract for difference takes that risk off the investor and puts it on the bill-payer. A second protection is built into the market design. Britain runs a single national wholesale price, the same across the country, so a generator is never penalised for its location. If the electricity network becomes too congested to move its power to a demand centre, the generator is paid to do nothing, at the national price.

Britain’s energy markets are now a byzantine mess distorted by state subsidies. Despite repeated attempts at reform, the subsidies remain, and because they are added to electricity bills, they undermine the other half of Britain’s energy strategy: electrification.

Privatisation involved separating the bits of the system that could be competitive (generation and retail) from those that could not (networks). Britain went further than any other country in splitting up its industry, in the belief that this would improve efficiency by creating specialised firms that would compete. The downside was that it created a highly fragmented industry, which made co-ordination difficult when it tried to build new infrastructure.

The construction programme of the 2010s was disjointed. Wind farms were built in remote parts of Scotland before the electricity network was ready to handle the flows of power they brought south. In response, between 2022 and 2024 the Conservative and then the Labour government established the National Energy System Operator, known as the NESO, ‘an independent, public corporation responsible for planning Britain’s electricity and gas networks and operating the electricity system’. It was tasked with making a series of grand plans: Future Energy Scenarios, a Strategic Spatial Energy Plan, a Centralised Strategic Network Plan and multiple Regional Energy Strategic Plans. There is no definition of what makes these plans ‘strategic’. In 2024, another central planner was added, Clean Power Mission Control in Whitehall.

Central planning has been placed on top of the fragmented privatised industry, but there has been no attempt at reintegration. In many ways the institutional architecture of the energy system has become even more complex over the last five years. Whereas the privatisers envisaged a single independent regulator – Ofgem – in between the government and the energy companies, there is instead a triumvirate of institutions: Ofgem, the NESO and the Department for Energy Security and Net Zero (DESNZ). There are also dozens of quangos, providing employment to a new class of economic regulators and corporate affairs professionals. On any given matter companies must speak to, and lobby, multiple organisations. Responsibilities are distributed across different bodies: for example, the Department for Energy Security and Net Zero is responsible for subsidising power stations in line with a plan written by the National Energy System Operator, but the NESO is regulated by Ofgem, which reports back to DESNZ. The scope for confusion is considerable.

The central planners of today are unlike their predecessors in one crucial respect: they do not build, own or operate infrastructure. They carry out PowerPoint planning, something perfectly suited to Britain’s service sector economy. State intervention is being employed to protect private sector returns; target setting is used to justify subsidies. In summer 2025, the government decided to abandon a set of major reforms to the electricity markets, after facing bitter opposition from the renewables industry. These reforms were called ‘zonal pricing’, and would have replaced the single national price with regional prices. A wind farm in a remote corner of Scotland would have earned a lower price if its output depressed prices and would no longer be paid the national price to switch off when the network was congested. Having built such assets on the promise of national pricing, the industry fought hard against the reform. The government claimed it was giving ‘certainty to investors’. This contradicts the notion that under capitalism investors earn their returns by taking risks. It also makes clear that the role of the British state is now to ensure ‘investability’ by ‘de-risking’ projects. In this new world, critical infrastructure is perpetually financially reconfigured so that it will generate dependable returns for investors.

Much of Britain’s vital national infrastructure is in the hands of private companies and asset managers. In 2024, the water companies got into a dispute with the industry regulator, Ofwat, over bill increases. According to *Utility Weekly*, a major infrastructure investor warned that failing to keep the ‘investment community on side’ would ‘put wider infrastructure investment at risk, beyond just water’. In the postwar period, the energy industry was owned by the British state and its citizens (apart from a very small share of debt owed to foreign investors); today it is mostly owned by foreign companies and investors. The industry has accumulated vast amounts of debt: the ratio of debt to revenue is about ten times greater than it was under public ownership. This is why British politicians are so keen to keep on the good side of investors such as BlackRock; Britain is a distressed borrower that has to find a way of paying for a massive construction programme. The equipment used to turn wind and sun into electricity is overwhelmingly manufactured outside Britain. Until privatisation, the UK was a significant net exporter of the pipes, pylons, turbines, transformers and other elements that make up the energy system. By the late 1990s, it had become a net importer.

None of this means that renewables are the wrong bet. Britain must wean itself off imported fossil fuels, and the North Sea is all but depleted. The real disaster of British energy policy is trying to deliver an energy transition through a form of privatisation that has lost its original logic. If Britain’s political classes genuinely believed in markets, they would have pursued something like the Texan model, with limited subsidies and markets relatively free of government interference. If they genuinely believed in the power of central planning and statism, they would have pursued the French, Norwegian or Chinese model, in which the state owns power stations and electricity networks, and funds their construction through a combination of taxation and bills. Britain has private ownership while making the state responsible for planning via subsidies. The result is a zombie system, a muddled and dysfunctional hybrid of state and market, with the benefits of neither and the costs of both. The regulatory architecture behind this is the most complex in the world. The energy transition is more expensive than it needs to be because Britain is almost entirely dependent on foreign capital to finance it and overwhelmingly dependent on imported kit to build it, and has chosen to recover the subsidies from bills rather than taxation.

Last summer​ , Chris Stark, the head of the government’s Clean Power by 2030 Mission, wrote a piece in the *Telegraph* arguing that Britain should copy China and become an ‘electrostate’. The term had been concocted earlier that year by energy industry analysts on the model of the more familiar ‘petrostate’. Over the last fifteen years, China’s economy has rapidly electrified. Today 30 per cent of its final energy consumption is serviced by electricity, up from 18 per cent in 2010, whereas in Europe and the US this figure has stalled at 22 per cent. To achieve this, China has had to build electricity infrastructure at breathtaking scale and breakneck speed. In 2003, it opened its first high-speed electric railway, a 400 km line from Qinhuangdao to Shenyang. By 2024, its electric rail network was 45,000 km long. Nearly two-thirds of all the new solar farms in the world that year were in China. It also manufactures the vast majority of solar, wind and battery equipment.

Last December, Stark was in the *Times* again arguing that Britain was ‘now an electrostate’, rolling out ‘clean wind and solar’. The energy industry is prone to technological boosterism, and the term ‘electrostate’ is only the latest example. Does it simply describe a country that consumes a lot of electricity? On this measure Norway would fit the bill, with electricity servicing 50 per cent of energy demand, but it could also be considered a petrostate, since oil and gas make up 60 per cent of its exports. Based on electricity consumption, Britain is not an electrostate. But if ‘electrostate’ means a country that generates a high share of its electricity from renewables, then Britain could claim to be one, though China could not. In 2024, it generated 58 per cent of its electricity from coal.

The many articles and reports on China’s energy miracle almost all fail to acknowledge that its electricity system is overwhelmingly owned and controlled by the state. Gigantic state-owned enterprises, the so-called Big Five, dominate generation. The electricity network is run by two state-owned companies, China Southern Power Grid Company and the State Grid Corporation of China. By contrast, Britain has nine network operators (all in private ownership) even though its electricity system is far smaller. Since the early 2000s, China has set up electricity markets and encouraged the private sector to become involved in electricity generation, but prices are still tightly controlled by the state, especially for domestic consumers. The oil and gas sector is dominated by three state-owned enterprises and there are strict controls on the export of crude oil and liquefied natural gas. Domestic production has increased in recent years, and last year the state approved more new licences for exploration than ever before.

Britain and China are now on very different paths. China is using a combination of public ownership and markets; Britain is de-marketising its energy system with subsidies and central planning to preserve ubiquitous private ownership. Many in the UK energy industry seem to believe that we can somehow become an electrostate like China while avoiding the other features of its system, without bringing energy infrastructure into state ownership, restructuring the industry, controlling exports, and reforming a planning regime that is lucrative for consultants and lobbyists.

There is a consensus across the political spectrum that Britain’s terrible economic productivity is a result of low levels of investment since 1990, especially in fixed assets such as housing, rail, road, water and energy infrastructure. Historical examples of infrastructure projects are used to highlight Britain’s decline while also giving hope that it can be reversed – we used to build things, after all. Take the recent flurry of interest in nuclear power. An online alliance made up of groups with names such as Anglo-Futurists, YIMBYs and Abundantists has mounted a campaign in favour of building more nuclear power stations. They often claim that Britain ‘once led the world in nuclear innovation’ – a reference to Calder Hall, the world’s first nuclear power station, which opened in 1956. They rarely mention the role state ownership played then, or that the nuclear programmes in France, Finland, South Korea and China were (and are still) under public ownership. That privatisation killed off the British nuclear programme in the 1990s is also ignored. Pro-nuclear groups want to see the private sector build, own and operate nuclear power plants independent of the state. Due to the costs involved, however, many have (reluctantly) accepted that government support is inevitable.

Britain’s​ privatised energy industry is built on a series of myths about the ‘bad old days’ of nationalisation. The first myth is that state ownership led to underinvestment, as politicians prioritised expenditure on healthcare and schools. In fact, the British electricity system mobilised vast quantities of capital to build a huge amount of infrastructure. Between 1950 and 1979, the British energy industry invested, on average, the equivalent of 1.5 per cent of the nation’s gross domestic product, peaking at 2.4 per cent in 1966 and 1967. That figure has hovered around 0.5 per cent since 1990. Between 1947 and 1979, power station capacity grew by 12.5 per cent a year. The coal-fired power stations that were the backbone of the system became bigger and more efficient: those built in the 1960s were nine times the size of pre-war equivalents. The Central Electricity Generating Board also built a ‘supergrid’, which visiting engineers from the US described as ‘unnecessarily ambitious’. It was 1150 miles in length and approved, designed and constructed between 1952 and 1960. It cost £1.9 billion in today’s prices and was delivered on schedule and under budget. Today’s private electricity transmission companies hope to construct 620 miles of high voltage onshore grid at a cost of nearly £20 billion over a decade.

The second myth is that state ownership is the enemy of technological innovation. The hydroelectric power stations built in Scotland were impressive feats of engineering. Between 1948 and 1958, hydroelectric capacity increased eightfold. The pumped-storage hydroelectric facility inside Ben Cruachan, which opened in 1965, is a labyrinth of tunnels and chambers, with a machine hall a kilometre inside the mountain. The ‘hollow mountain’, as it is called, took six years to build and was first properly put to the test on the night of the 1966 World Cup final, when its fast response time kept the UK grid going despite the spike in demand. It is still operational today.

In the 1960s, Britain’s gas industry also made a series of breakthroughs – most important, it developed two ways of deriving gas from oil and opened the first terminal in the world for the import of liquefied natural gas, which at that time came mostly from Algeria. In 1958, the British Gas Council had developed the world’s first liquefied natural gas tanker, the Methane Pioneer, from a repurposed American cargo ship. Liquefied natural gas became less important for the UK after the discovery of natural gas in the North Sea in 1965, which also led to one of the most impressive mega-projects in British history: the conversion of the whole system from town gas (made from coal) to natural gas. Natural gas had twice the calorific value of town gas – meaning it produced more heat energy when burned – and so had the potential to be much cheaper. But making the switch would be very expensive. Natural gas has a different chemical composition from town gas and so the two couldn’t be mixed. The entire infrastructure – from pipelines to appliances in homes – had to be replaced or adapted.

Between 1966 and 1976, 35 million appliances were converted in 13.4 million homes and more than 3000 miles of pipeline constructed. Gas conversion was an immensely complex undertaking, involving thousands of different kinds of cooker, heater and stove. Households were given a ‘C-Day’ when a fitter would arrive and replace or adapt all their appliances. Within ten hours the gas would be switched back on. Hospitals and hotels only had their supply interrupted for a few hours. Information flowed back and forth between the engineers and a central technical advisory board. Vast storerooms were set up around the country, all employing the latest techniques in inventory management. The programme cost £10 billion in today’s prices, and was delivered on time and under budget. As was the case after the construction of the electricity supergrid, gas prices tumbled.

By the late 1970s, Britain’s nationalised gas industry had set up an international consulting service and was licensing technologies to the US and Japan. Nearly 85 per cent of British homes still use natural gas for heating, a dependence that is in large part due to the successes of the nationalised industry. State backing allowed the industry to place bets that would take a long time to pay off and to explore many technologies simultaneously, rapidly concentrating efforts when it became clear which technology was most likely to succeed.

Many on the centre and right argue that Britain struggles to build infrastructure today because of self-imposed ‘barriers’, usually identified as the planning system and environmental rules. It is true that there are more constraints. But under the Town and Country Planning Act of 1947, engineers had to apply to ministers for permission to build anything. Most projects were the subject of public inquiries. In 1952, a parliamentary committee complained that these ‘tedious procedures’ were delaying the completion of power stations. The 1957 Electricity Act included an ‘amenity’ clause that meant the new Central Electricity Generating Board had to ‘have regard’ for ‘preserving natural beauty, conserving flora, fauna and … features of special interest’. The board’s annual reports were littered with complaints about this clause. It ran a massive PR campaign, with one advert, in *Country Life* in December 1960, showing a tiny pylon being placed in the landscape by a giant hand alongside the question ‘Where would you run the pylons?’ The text beneath focused on the benefits electrification would bring to rural communities.

Planning reform is necessary, but is it sufficient? It is in the interests of private companies to focus on stories of ‘planning regulations gone mad’. These divert attention from the bigger question: how should Britain pay for a once in a generation infrastructure expansion? The state is fiscally constrained and energy companies are at the limit of their capacity to borrow. The default option today is to put the cost of infrastructure onto consumer bills. This relies on the fiction that taxpayers don’t have the money to fund infrastructure but consumers do, even though these people are one and the same. We are told that electricity bills will increase in the near term on the promise that they might fall at some point in the future. As a policy, this is regressive; rich and poor pay the same price for electricity and gas. Moreover, it is hard to believe in jam tomorrow when bills have been going up since the early 2000s.

The postwar generation​ had an elegant solution: a combination of borrowing, bills and state funding. In the first decade after the war the gas and electricity industries issued bonds, without giving holders any voting rights. After 1955, all capital was raised by the Treasury. The Gas Council was permitted to borrow over £20 billion in today’s prices with a Treasury guarantee. Investments were also funded by consumer bills, which was known as ‘self-financing’, and by the Treasury, through transfers, loans or fixed interest stock issues (also guaranteed by the state). A key feature of this arrangement was the low cost of capital, because the state could borrow money at a lower rate than private firms. The approach was simple, transparent and flexible. In the 1950s and early 1960s, when the electricity industry was building infrastructure, only between 20 and 40 per cent of investment came from bills. When this phase ended, self-financing levels were raised. This mechanism meant that bills were held down during the most expensive phase, the build-out, so as to stimulate consumption.

The nationalised model was highly suitable for asset building and electrification; privatisation, by contrast, was made for asset sweating. In the early 1990s, the newly privatised energy companies built a number of gas-fired power stations, a major reason for the drop in electricity prices during that decade. But this was only possible because the nationalised industries had done much of the groundwork. Most of the new power stations were built on the site of coal-fired stations already connected to the grid. These stations could gain access to gas through a transmission network built in the 1970s, supplied from the North Sea fields that the Gas Council had helped to open.

Britain’s position in 2026 is historically precarious. It has become common to compare the current decade to the 1970s, a time of stagflation, division and strife. However, in the case of energy the comparison is inappropriate. By the end of the 1970s, Britain had built most of the power stations, electricity networks, gas facilities and pipelines it would need for decades to come. That endowment has now been spent. Becoming an electrostate means building the system back up again and bringing parts of the energy industry back into public ownership.
