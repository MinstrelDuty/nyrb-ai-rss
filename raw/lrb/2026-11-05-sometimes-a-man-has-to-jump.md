# 正文

Early​ in July 1942, people on Japan’s northernmost main island of Hokkaidō became aware that a man was rifling through their rubbish bins. This would have been peculiar behaviour under any circumstances, but the man was their prime minister. Tōjō Hideki was a little over half a year into an all-out war with the United States and Britain – to add to an older conflict still raging in China – and it was taking its toll. Apparently, worried that food supplies on the island weren’t adequate, he was carrying out his own spot checks. The night before the attack on Pearl Harbor, in December the previous year, his wife had found him sitting up in bed, crying.

Could this really be the warmongering fanatic plastered across the Western media? The evil mastermind of mass slaughter in the Asia-Pacific? It is telling that caricaturists could never quite decide whether Tōjō was a cold-hearted murderer or the buffoonish runt evoked by Jack Campbell’s ‘Tokio Kid’ poster series: round glasses, grotesquely exaggerated teeth, pointy ears and a bloody dagger in his hand. In mangled English, he expressed his appreciation for American industrial workers who failed to perform their wartime tasks effectively, thus aiding Japan’s cause. ‘Much waste of material make so-o-o-o happy! Thank you.’

Tōjō was hard to caricature because he was hard to grasp. He was no Hitler or Stalin, capable of imposing his will and vision on the vast machinery of a modern nation-state. As Peter Mauch reveals in his new biography, Tōjō was a product of that machinery. He was what you get when a constitution distributes power and responsibility across a range of competing interests: army, navy, civilian cabinet, diplomats, talking-shop parliament, big business and an emperor whose divine aloofness meant that no one could agree on where he should sit in the decision-making process.

All this makes Tōjō at once a challenge and an opportunity for a biographer. He is a challenge because his inner life was opaque. Tōjō was not a visionary like Lenin, an orator like Churchill, or an inspiring human being in the mould of Gandhi or Martin Luther King. Anyone hoping to find in him explanations for the disasters that befell Japan in the 1940s, and the great misery it inflicted on its Asian neighbours, will be disappointed. The opportunity here lies in seeing the world from Tōjō’s point of view. He was the man who had the near impossible task of serving as a decisive wartime leader for a country whose constitution had been designed, in the 1880s, with consensus in mind, by the small clique of men who laid the foundations for modern Japan. They had now gone, and consensus had gone with them.

Born in December 1884, into a family with samurai ancestry and a history of military service, Tōjō attended the Peers’ School in Tokyo, established to educate members of the imperial family and the nobility. He was neither of those things, and seems to have found aristocratic disdain – either for the military specifically or for commoners in general – too much to bear. His classmates arrived at school in rickshaws and had maids deliver them freshly prepared meals at lunchtime; Tōjō made the 45-minute journey on foot, carrying his own lunch. He left the Peers’ School after a year and eventually settled at the Tokyo regional Military Preparatory School. There, amid early morning bugle calls and lessons in horse riding, reconnaissance, marksmanship and the Chinese classics, he learned the lesson that would shape his life: the role of the military is to execute the emperor’s will. When he graduated in May 1904, the Meiji Emperor himself attended.

The great cliché of failed military planning is that generals always fight the last war. In Japan, it was worse: the generals didn’t even have an accurate account of that war. The version of the Russo-Japanese War of 1904-5 on the curriculum at the War College, which Tōjō joined as a young army officer in 1912, was a selective one. Little was said about how narrow Japan’s victory had been and how flawed the decision-making of its top military commanders. A naive faith was thus retained in the country’s late 19th-century fusion of two military cultures: Prussian and samurai, the latter itself a romanticised memory. The upshot was an emphasis on attack over defence, and on the fighting spirit of the infantry.

Soon enough, there was another ‘last war’ from which to learn. Japan entered the First World War on the side of the Allies, though its participation in the European theatre was mostly limited to providing battlefield medical care and assisting Allied shipping in the Mediterranean. Tōjō and others of his generation concluded that war was now a matter of ‘national mobilisation’: people, resources, entire industries. The implications were perilous: young officers in the Imperial Japanese Army concluded that they could not just sit back and hope that the industry, infrastructure and national co-ordination required for the next war would be furnished by what they regarded as grasping politicians and their allies in big business and the diplomatic service (members of this last group were seen as suspicious because of their very choice of career, which implied a fondness for foreigners).

A generational shift was taking place, one for which the authors of Japan’s 1889 constitution hadn’t planned. That constitution had placed a semi-divine emperor at the heart of everything. He was the ultimate source of his people’s rights – and his ‘gifts’ could be withdrawn. He oversaw appointments to the cabinet, the judiciary and the upper house of the Imperial Diet. He had the power to dissolve the elected lower house, which in any case had been designed as a talking shop and a conduit – alongside schools and newspapers – for the wishes of Japan’s ruling elite to reach the people. Different services within the armed forces reported, separately, to the emperor as commander in chief.

For all that this might sound like the ideal underpinning for an imperial dictatorship, in practice the emperor’s role in decision-making was more modest, and less straightforward. Real power in Japan, in the late 19th and early 20th century, was wielded by a small group of elder statesmen. They dominated early cabinets, and even once those cabinets came to draw their members more from Japan’s fledgling political parties, they continued to guide the country’s affairs via back channels. Democrats might not have liked it, but a combination of custom, decorum and personal relationships held the system together. Tōjō came along just as it was falling apart.

Mauch draws our attention to a milestone in this process: a clandestine meeting that took place in the Black Forest spa town of Baden-Baden in October 1921. Three senior Japanese military figures met there to discuss how Japan might prepare for total war, and to plot and scheme how to get rid of their army rivals. One of them, Okamura Yasuji, later invited Tōjō to join the group. He quickly agreed. A dominant theme in discussions among officers in the years that followed was the need for Japan to turn its modest foothold in Manchuria – a legacy of the Russo-Japanese War – into a strategic base for an inevitable future conflict with the Soviet Union.

It is difficult to detect, during the years of Tōjō’s young adulthood, much evidence of any interest in big political or philosophical ideas. One of his associates in the army, Ishiwara Kanji, was a convert to a Buddhist sect rooted in the teachings of the medieval monk Nichiren. He believed that Nichiren’s prediction of an apocalyptic future war destined to renew the world after first drenching it in blood would soon be fulfilled in a conflict between Japan and the US. For Tōjō, by contrast, loyalty to the imperial house seemed to answer all questions. Communism and socialism were obviously wrong, democratic politics were fine as long as they were subordinated to the imperial will, and regular visits to pray at Shintō shrines took care of the rest. Tōjō focused his attention, instead, on using his increasingly senior position within the army bureaucracy to address the managerial challenges of readying the armed forces for war. This included rigging the entrance exams for the War College, to ensure that the right sort of people would fill senior army roles in the years to come.

Tōjō was a stickler for the constitution. He condemned political violence at home, including the assassination of Japan’s prime minister by young military officers in May 1932 (they had hoped to murder Charlie Chaplin too, but at the last minute he postponed meeting the PM to go and watch sumo). But he did not condemn the army saboteurs who attacked a railway line in Manchuria in 1931 – a pretext for conquest. In Tōjō’s view, the puppet state of Manchukuo was a way of keeping valuable territory out of Chinese and Soviet hands. The importance of this was underlined in 1937, when worsening relations between Japan and Chiang Kai-shek’s Nationalist government deteriorated into undeclared war. Tōjō was appointed army vice minister the next year, gaining responsibility for managing the China Incident, which marked the actual start of the Second Sino-Japanese War. Two years later came the outbreak of war in Europe in 1939, an exhilarating yet daunting opportunity. As a slogan of the time put it: ‘Don’t miss the bus!’ Europe’s colonies and their resources – including oil, tin, iron and rubber – were potentially up for grabs. Gaining control of French Indochina would allow Japan to cut off a key route used to funnel Anglo-American aid to Chiang Kai-shek.

Japan’s prime minister, Mitsumasa Yonai, a naval officer, wanted to remain neutral in the European war. This was unacceptable to the army, which brought down his cabinet in July 1940 by having the army minister resign and then refusing to nominate an alternative. When a replacement cabinet was formed, Tōjō became army minister, although he seems not to have wanted the job, protesting that the requirement for ministers to keep other people happy reduced them to the level of a barmaid. Once in post, he faced a huge challenge. His country seemed to be encircled by hostile or potentially hostile powers, from the US to the European colonies in South-East Asia, and on to China and the Soviet Union. The key question in the summer of 1940 was whether resources that were badly needed for the conflict with China could be seized in South-East Asia without drawing the US into war – or at least without starting that war before Japan was ready.

The dominant answer in the army was ‘yes’. But the navy thought this naive. It was confident Japan could defeat the US in a quick and decisive war, but worried about its chances in a more protracted conflict. Meanwhile, Japan’s foreign minister, Matsuoka Yōsuke, insisted that the Tripartite Pact with Germany and Italy, signed in September 1940, would persuade the US to stay out of European and Asian affairs alike. This revealed a disastrous lack of unified thinking across Japan’s centres of power, made worse by personal ambition and a tendency to freelance. On a visit to Europe in the spring of 1941, Matsuoka didn’t bother to inform his colleagues in Tokyo about the substance of his meetings with Hitler and Mussolini – leaving them to read about what had happened in the newspapers. The prime minister, Konoe Fumimarō, hoped for a summit meeting in the US, during which he planned to cable the emperor for approval rather than consult his cabinet colleagues or the military.

Tōjō did not want war with America or Britain. He hoped to enlist the US in finding a diplomatic solution in China, and had been told by senior figures in the navy of their fear that Japan could not win a long war in the Pacific. But they refused to make such an admission at cabinet meetings, or during conferences with the emperor. Tōjō felt that there was little that, as army minister, he could do. As he told Konoe during a heated exchange about the looming threat of war: ‘Sometimes a man has to jump, with his eyes closed, from the veranda of Kiyomizu Temple.’

The leap was made​ in December 1941. It is a testament to the ambiguities of Japan’s constitution that Tōjō didn’t want the job of prime minister because it came with too little influence, while the emperor didn’t want him to have it because he feared the rise of a modern-day shogun: a military man, and a hawkish one at that, who would usurp his power. But, in the end, Tōjō seemed the candidate most likely to be able to control a restive army, so he got the job. To him fell the task of persuading a sceptical Japanese public that their country’s ‘self-existence’ was threatened by the encroachment of Anglo-American and European powers into Asia and by the willingness of those powers to use China as a means of weakening Japan. The desired solution was for Japan to dominate South-East Asia, contribute to the downfall of Britain – though this was regarded as being ultimately in Hitler’s hands – and strengthen isolationist sentiment in America, thus undermining its will to fight.

It was only once a decision for war had finally been reached, at the beginning of December, that Tōjō became aware that a plan existed to open hostilities with the US by attacking Pearl Harbor. This was days before the attack was launched. Even then, Mauch tells us, Tōjō found out only because he was still also army minister and thus able to participate in meetings of the Imperial General Headquarters. Had he merely been prime minister, Tōjō would not have found out about Pearl Harbor.

Having taken his country to war less out of conviction than because he thought it was the only course of action, Tōjō prayed that American resolve would falter, and worked to ensure that the same didn’t happen at home. Taking on the additional role of home secretary, he was determined to apply lessons from the First World War about the risks of allowing domestic morale to be undermined by nervy citizens, quisling politicians and left-wing radicals. He introduced legislation to curtail freedom of speech and assembly. Anglo-American music was censored. Moves were made to pack the Diet with friendly faces.

The importance of controlling popular opinion and representation was brought home to Tōjō during the months after the Battle of Midway in June 1942. It turned into a major defeat for the imperial navy, in retrospect marking the point at which Japan shifted from rapid advance to steady retreat. From this point onwards, Tōjō appears, in Mauch’s account, as a competent middle manager charged with an impossible task and made to take the blame for failure. As the war dragged on, thoughts turned to who would bear responsibility if and when Japan were defeated. By the middle of 1944, Tōjō was ready to step down as prime minister, but only if the order came from the Imperial Palace. Some in the imperial family didn’t want to be seen to have instigated such a dramatic turn of events, while others wanted Tōjō to remain in post – ready to serve as a scapegoat after the war, in place of the emperor.

The emperor was eventually persuaded that Tōjō had to go. His cabinet resigned on 18 July 1944 and a little over a year later Tōjō was receiving hate-mail from a newly liberated population. Why, his correspondents demanded to know, when their family members had been called up to die on distant battlefields, had Tōjō’s own sons been allowed to stay safe? He was an ‘ogre’ and a ‘brute’. He should just get on with it and kill himself. Tōjō had already resolved to do this, taking responsibility for his failures while avoiding the ignominious fate of Mussolini: summary execution, body dangled upside down next to a petrol station. He shot himself before American agents could arrest him. But emergency medical care, including six or seven blood transfusions, saved his life and set the stage for a final act.

There was one thing Tōjō and the victorious US could agree on: the emperor was not to be held responsible for the war. The US needed Hirohito as an ally, capable of winning the Japanese people round to the sweeping reforms they were planning as part of the Allied occupation. Tōjō, for his part, remained true to that prep school principle: the job of the army is to serve the emperor. That meant giving an account of the war at the International Military Tribunal for the Far East – convened in April 1946 and modelled on the Nuremberg Trials – which completely exonerated His Imperial Majesty.

It did not mean giving the Allies everything they wanted. Tōjō rejected the idea of a ‘war criminal’, on the basis that armed conflict was not to be conflated with criminality. And he used the trials to argue that the events of the 1930s and 1940s should be understood not in terms of Japanese aggression but of Anglo-American imperialism and hypocrisy – which he attempted to contrast with Japan’s ‘righteousness’ in dealing respectfully with its fellow Asians (a claim bitterly received by the country’s Chinese, Korean and South-East Asian victims). General Douglas MacArthur, the supreme commander for the Allies, didn’t want Tōjō to take the witness stand. Unfortunately for MacArthur, the chief prosecutor, Joseph Keenan, had other ideas. The prospect of becoming a legal legend overnight was too tempting to turn down. But Keenan’s preening and under-informed cross-examination went so badly that at times it seemed Tōjō was upbraiding him for his poor performance. At one point, Tōjō questioned Keenan about the meaning of one of his questions, to which Keenan was forced to reply that he didn’t know. As Gary J. Bass reports in his history of the trials, *Judgment at Tokyo* (2023), a woman working in the British embassy commented that ‘Tōjō had a good morning hanging Keenan.’

Whatever enjoyment Tōjō may have taken in baiting the American prosecutor, he knew how the process would end. Having concluded his defence in January 1948, he spent much of the ten months that it took the tribunal judges to consider their verdict thinking about his own mortality. He had been a regular visitor to Shintō shrines, especially the Meiji Shrine and Yasukuni Shrine – the latter dedicated to Japan’s modern war dead – but he now regarded these visits as narrowly transactional. In any case, he thought that a man in his humbled position no longer had anything to offer the gods. Tōjō found himself drawn instead to Jōdo Shinshū, a Buddhist sect founded by a medieval monk called Shinran, which stressed human frailty, radical humility and the possibility of salvation at the hands of Amida Buddha. Military men in past centuries had preferred the discipline and austerity of Zen, but perhaps something about the scale and intensity of total war made people feel the futility of what Jōdo Shinshū called ‘self-power’ and left them hungry for ‘other-power’: the saving grace of Amida Buddha. It is striking that one of the great Japanese philosophers of this era, Tanabe Hajime, who had advocated for total war, also embraced Jōdo Shinshū, with its call for deep and unqualified repentance.

Was Tōjō sincere in his religious conversion, desperate, or hoping for one last go at shaping the way history might remember him? He began reading Shinran and saying the Nembutsu: a simple prayer – ‘I take refuge in Amida Buddha’ – whose recitation is said to be all that humans can or need do towards their own salvation. Tōjō chanted those words, over and over again, when he went to the gallows on the morning of 23 December 1948. Lest he become a martyr, the Allied authorities prevented publication of his trial affidavit and had his ashes scattered at sea.

They needn’t have worried. The Japanese public had been through too much to make a hero of someone like Tōjō: a man who had regarded them as fodder for a ‘total war’ fought despite the absence of any pressing need, goal or ideology. For all its flaws, the Allied occupation, which ran from 1945 to 1952, sought to prevent a repeat of such a conflict. A new constitution placed sovereignty in the hands of the people, with the emperor now a mere ‘symbol’ of the state. Women were given the vote. War was forsworn as a means of settling international disagreements. And what later became the Self-Defence Forces were subordinated to elected politicians.

Mauch’s biography wisely steers clear of counterfactuals: we cannot possibly say how that crucial period in 1940-41 might have played out without Tōjō. There are too many moving parts: Hitler’s shifting strategy in Europe, Soviet strength, American resolve, divided opinions in the Japanese armed forces about how limited resources ought to be used – perhaps above all a system of decision-making that made it almost impossible for a leader to manoeuvre Japan away from the brink. A product of that failed system, Tōjō was not the man for the job.
