# 正文

At the end​ of September, Google Maps updated its satellite images of Gaza from the set taken in the summer of 2023. Entire cities have disappeared, wiped from the surface of the Earth. Some names still hover over the indistinct dunes of rubble. Pins marking the Green Farm pharmacy, Abu Khalid bakery, Jalal grocery, Al Qarara Museum, Algerian Hospital, Lama coffee shop for women, Abuahmad supermarket, Ashour furniture store, Dr Ehab Salah Dentistry and the Red Dragon School for boxing and martial arts are digital ghosts of a world that no longer exists, with Google optimistically marking them ‘temporarily closed’. More than 74,000 people have been killed in Gaza according to the Palestinian Ministry of Health, and up to ten thousand are missing under the rubble. More than a hundred thousand Palestinians have managed to leave. The survivors are corralled into a concentration area along the coast, living in dense tent encampments among ruined buildings, mountains of rubbish and rivers of sewage, under the constant hum of drones. Assassinations continue: in October more than a hundred people were killed, including an eight-year-old girl called Shahd Abdul-Razzaq al-Najjar.

Gaza has been experiencing an old-fashioned scorched-earth campaign reminiscent of the Nakba of 1948 and no different from the settler-colonial genocides of previous centuries. Much of it was carried out by artillery, tanks, sappers and bulldozers. The aerial campaign was so intense – the most intense on record – that its effects could equally have been achieved by carpet-bombing. Yet much of the outrage generated by Yuval Abraham and Rachel Szor’s film *NAZA*, recently released in the UK, concerns Israel’s use of artificial intelligence and other computational technologies to select targets and calculate how many people will be killed. The outrage is connected to the growing panic about AI: the rubble of Gaza seems like an image of a world where AI is out of control, the terminal state of the ‘end times fascism’ described by Naomi Klein and Astra Taylor. In the film, anonymous interviewees, their faces obscured, describe the automated targeting system and the unprecedented extent of ‘collateral damage’ (the Hebrew acronym for this is NZA): that is, the number of civilians – men, women and children – the system foresees will be killed by each strike. This system isn’t the main means by which the genocide has been carried out, but it tells us something about the way the Israeli military and Israeli society seek to obscure their atrocities behind seemingly dispassionate tech-speak about calculation, datasets, interfaces and machine learning.

Calculations of how much the lives of the colonised are worth have always been part of colonialism. The origins of the calculations that *NAZA* deals with go back to late 2000, when the Oslo Accords collapsed into the al-Aqsa intifada. The Israeli prime minister, Ehud Barak, who developed an expertise in the assassination of Palestinian political and cultural leaders as a special forces officer in the 1970s, sought to quell the uprising by eliminating the Palestinian political and military leadership. He asked Daniel Reisner, then chief legal adviser in the Israeli army (he died in July), to write a protocol that would make such assassinations comply with Israel’s interpretation of the laws of armed conflict. Reisner spent six weeks on the question. Assassinations, he concluded, were ‘legal’ if a number of conditions were met: the target must pose an imminent danger, there should be no plausible means of arrest and the operation must strictly adhere to the principle of ‘proportionality’, minimising the killing of civilians. Proportionality is a cornerstone of the laws of armed conflict, but was only formally codified in the protocols added in 1977 to the Geneva Conventions, following a debate that began in response to the devastation caused by the US bombing of Vietnam, where half a million civilians were killed.

The 1977 protocol prohibits ‘an attack which may be expected to cause incidental loss of civilian life, injury to civilians, damage to civilian objects, or a combination thereof, which would be excessive in relation to the concrete and direct military advantage anticipated’. A lot hangs on the word ‘excessive’. The macabre aspect of the protocol is that it doesn’t prohibit the killing of civilians. Proportionality is about degree, about deciding what is ‘too much’. But how much is too much? On this, the protocol is deliberately vague: it doesn’t give a number, since the drafters believed that this would become a licence to kill that number of civilians.

Reisner was frustrated with this vagueness. Proportionality doesn’t ‘tell us what to include in the calculation, what is the equation and what is the exchange rate’. He thought armies needed simple formulas and exact numbers to be able to act quickly and efficiently ‘in real-time situations’. He was trying to develop what he described to me around fifteen years ago as ‘mathematical tools to tackle the problems of proportionality’. He wanted a system that would take into account the importance of the target, the likelihood of a civilian presence and the expected harm to civilians. He believed that letting computers make the calculations was more ethical than leaving it to humans, because machines aren’t subject to human bias and aren’t corrupted by the desire for revenge, cruelty or excess. One of *NAZA*’s interviewees shares this position, telling Abraham that ‘it’s better that the AI takes the decision rather than a person whose friend was just killed.’

In a seminar on proportionality that Reisner convened for Israeli army officers in 2002, he asked the participants to take part in an experiment. He told them to jot down on a piece of paper the number of civilians they thought it would be legal to kill in an attack on a particular target. The officers folded up their notes and put them in a military cap, which was passed round the table. Reisner tallied the numbers and divided the total by the number of participants. The average was 3.14, or π. The participants may have found the process entertaining, but Reisner used it to make his point: ‘As a lawyer I need numbers to work with. I need thresholds in order to instruct the soldiers. Any number could become a useful benchmark.’ The Israeli army began treating collateral damage as an accounting problem in necro-economics. This shift was accelerated by advances in computational technology. Signals intelligence and precision-guided munitions evolved, and drones, which emerged in the early years of the millennium, made round-the-clock surveillance possible. When, like everyone else, the targets of strikes started carrying smartphones, intelligence agencies gained an unparalleled means of tracking location and movement. Software modelling of the probable result of explosions was also developed.

Some bombings were still considered disproportionate. Around midnight on 22 July 2002 an Israeli jet dropped a one-tonne bomb, its largest munition, on a building in the al-Daraj area of Gaza City, killing Salah Shehadeh, the head of the al-Qassam Brigades, Hamas’s military wing. The building he was in was swallowed into a massive crater. Sixteen other houses around it were destroyed or damaged and fourteen other Palestinians were killed, including Shehadeh’s wife, Layla, and his daughter Iman, along with seven members of the Matar family, who lived next door. There was an international outcry, with objections even from Israeli pilots. Dan Halutz, then the commander of the Israeli air force, defended the pilot who dropped the bomb – ‘You did exactly what you were instructed to do’ – and demanded that the operation’s Israeli critics be put on trial for treason. Asked what he felt when dropping bombs that killed civilians, he replied: ‘I feel a light bump on the wing of the plane.’ But even the Bush administration condemned Israel for its ‘heavy-handed action’.

When Israel began its targeted assassination programme the US declared it illegal. But after 9/11, with neo-colonial wars spreading from Palestine to Iraq and Afghanistan, the US adopted Israel’s tactics and its underlying interpretation of the laws of war. In 2003, the US prepared for its invasion of Iraq with a mass bombardment of Baghdad intended to cause ‘shock and awe’. The Pentagon’s proportionality analysis set collateral damage thresholds. Marc Garlasco was an intelligence analyst at the Pentagon involved in planning aerial strikes on ‘high-value targets’, with Saddam Hussein at the top of the list. He was ordered to keep the number of civilians killed in each strike under thirty. The estimate of deaths was produced by a piece of software originally called BugSplat (later – when the name was found to be politically ‘unhelpful’ – renamed Fast Collateral Damage). The software, variations of which are still in military use, took into account architectural factors such as the size of a building and the relative amount of steel, concrete and glass, along with factors such as the number of people expected to be inside and around the building at various times of day, the size and type of the bomb and the angle of the attack. ‘If the number [provided by the software] was above 29,’ Garlasco told me, ‘we had the air force play with the bomb angles, fusing, bomb tonnage etc, and got that number down to about 25.’ If there was no way to keep the number under thirty, the strike had to go to the secretary of defence, Donald Rumsfeld, for sign-off.

Those carrying out proportionality calculations often claim that their job is to minimise civilian harm. While they take some lives (fewer than thirty), they save others (presumably above that number). Such logic makes for an uncomfortable proximity between military action and human rights law, which can become a blueprint for military efficiency: it regulates how the military can best achieve its objectives without the unnecessary use of force. After he resigned from the Pentagon, Garlasco was hired by Human Rights Watch, and asked to undertake similar analysis but in inverse fashion. He was sent to war zones to study the shape of destroyed buildings (one of his destinations was Gaza after its devastation by the Israeli attack of 2008-9), deduce what kind of bomb had been used and whether the attack was proportional.

The number settled on as proportional depends on the aims of the occupying army. At one end of the spectrum, militaries try to reduce the number when they are seeking to win ‘the hearts and minds’ of the colonised population; at the other, they seek to maximise it when they want to terrorise people into submission, expel or exterminate them. In 2009, during the early Obama era, General Stanley McChrystal, who was leading the US military in Afghanistan, claimed that the anger caused by civilian casualties is ‘one of the most dangerous things we face’. He cautioned against the ‘insurgent math’ by which each civilian killed would create ten new enemies. When the Obama administration was criticised for its drone assassinations, John Brennan, the White House adviser on counterterrorism, retorted that it was ‘hard to imagine a tool that can better minimise the risk to civilians’. Others, incredibly, called drones a ‘humanitarian technology’.

According to the Bureau of Investigative Journalism, Obama ordered ten times as many drone strikes as Bush. Those strikes most often occurred in densely inhabited civilian areas – markets, homes, mosques and schools, in Pakistan, Somalia and Yemen – and killed hundreds of civilians. Lethal drone strikes targeted not only known individuals but suspects whose patterns of life suggested they might be members of al-Qaida or the Taliban. Strikes were ordered on the basis of movement along particular roads, phone calls to specific numbers or attendance at certain mosques.

The killing of civilians and the destruction of civilian property can also be used as an act of terror and revenge. During the 2006 Lebanon War, Israel destroyed fifteen thousand buildings and a large part of the Beirut suburb of Dahiya, a stronghold of Hizbullah. In 2008, it announced that the destruction of cities would be the cornerstone of its deterrence. Gadi Eisenkot – then commander of Israel’s northern front, and, as I write, the man challenging Netanyahu in the Israeli elections on 27 October – explained that the ‘Dahiya doctrine’ would see Israel retaliate with ‘disproportionate power … and cause immense damage and destruction’. Along with the explicit threat to target civilians, the use of the term ‘disproportionate’ is crucial here. It was meant both literally – Israel is using its overwhelming firepower without restraint to flatten urban areas – and legally: Israel is flouting the laws of war, in which the principle of proportionality is key. If the use of proportionality is taken to suggest support for the ethical, measured, rational waging of war, disproportionate action publicly demonstrates an unwillingness to be restrained by any legal or normative threshold and a genocidal willingness to kill civilians en masse. The Dahiya doctrine was applied in the massive rounds of terror bombing that Israel unleashed on Gaza in the winter of 2008-9, the summer of 2014 and again in the spring of 2021, in which thousands of Palestinian civilians were killed and tens of thousands of homes destroyed.

In between those assaults, another form of calculation was imposed on Gaza, with a relentless siege meant to keep Palestinians on the threshold of life. A military document called ‘Red Lines’ set the minimum number of calories required to sustain Gaza’s population at a level just above the UN definition of hunger (2100 calories a day for men, 1700 for women and a varying amount for children depending on gender and age). The total number of calories was translated into the tonnage required to feed Gaza’s then population of 1.5 million and thus the number of aid trucks allowed into the territory. The same went for megawatts of electricity and litres of fuel and water. What was seen as good behaviour was rewarded by an increase in the amounts coming in, any acts of resistance by the tightening of the siege, like a population-scale version of a Milgram experiment.

After the 2021 aerial bombing of Gaza, Israel’s then chief of staff, Aviv Kochavi (the philosophy-loving general I [discussed in the *LRB* of 16 December](/the-paper/v43/n24/eyal-weizman/tunnel-vision) that year), reported that many targets were being chosen by an AI system, which was later revealed to be named Habsora, Hebrew for ‘the Gospel’. One army officer described the 2021 bombardment as the ‘first AI war’. When he became chief of staff, Kochavi promised to make the army ‘more lethal’, and the Gospel, which he described as a tool for ‘the industrialisation of precise annihilation’, was central to this promise. In previous rounds of bombardment, buildings were destroyed at such a rate that military intelligence couldn’t supply new targets quickly enough and the air force ran out of things to bomb. The Gospel was designed to synthesise data from a multitude of sources – intercepted phone conversations, location data, aerial and satellite images and human agents – and to churn out co-ordinates (without them, guided munitions don’t work). Yet again, the army claimed that the Gospel helped to reduce collateral damage.

Gaza’s armed resistance retreated underground, seeking to make itself invisible and incalculable. Just as Netanyahu’s government thought it had satisfied Hamas with a certain easing of the siege, persuading Qatar to funnel nearly $2 billion into Gaza and increasing the amount of electricity, fuel and food supplied, the event that it hadn’t computed for erupted on 7 October 2023.

Israel’s​ genocide in Gaza began with its minister of defence, Yoav Gallant, declaring that ‘there will be no electricity, no food, no water, no fuel,’ shutting off the tap that had allowed through sometimes more, sometimes less. He also maximised the level of ‘collateral damage’. The first three weeks saw a massive aerial bombardment in which the army dropped more than 25,000 tonnes of munitions on Gaza’s cities and camps, the majority targeting Gaza City itself. The Palestinian Ministry of Health recorded the first ten thousand deaths. The scenes that end *NAZA* show attempts to rescue people from under the rubble, a father desperately trying to remove debris by hand to find his children.

This time the air force didn’t run out of targets, but it did start to run low on munitions. The US replenished them in one of its largest airlifts ever. Bombs landed on homes, commercial tower blocks, shopping centres, schools, mosques, bakeries, banks, the promenade, hospitals, the legislative council, bookshops, publishing houses, libraries, universities, food warehouses, restaurants, the supreme, administrative and constitutional courts. Hundreds of archaeological and heritage sites were also destroyed or damaged. The bombing unhoused more than a million Palestinians: the aim was to send them south, towards the border with Egypt and then out of Palestine.

The devastating application of AI after 7 October was exposed by Abraham, *NAZA*’s co-director, in a series of groundbreaking articles for the online magazine +972, *Local Call* and the *Guardian*. In *NAZA* we hear some of his sources talk for the first time. They describe a system called Lavender, which was intended to establish – through phone location data, calls made and contacts saved – whether the user of a phone was a Hamas operative, if so how senior they were and what their home address might be. The system’s degree of certainty was only 85 per cent – and as one of *NAZA*’s interviewees admits, a baker near a Hamas base will have a phone full of contacts who are Hamas operatives – but this was enough to authorise their killing, along with their entire families. Another of Abraham’s exposés had to do with a system called ‘Where’s Daddy?’ It enables intelligence officers to listen to, among other things, conversations between family members, including a targeted person’s children, happy that their father is home. This technology makes remote killing intimately close. The killers listen to the everyday conversations of the people they are about to kill, and later to the moans and screams of the injured.

The commander of Unit 8200, the intelligence corps that designed the system, referred to ‘the synergy between humans and machines’ as ‘supercognition’. Intelligence officers and legal advisers rarely dispute the results it produces. One of the interviewees says: ‘You have to trust the system.’ And they do trust it, as is made clear by the fact that it takes, on average, just twenty seconds for a target to be authorised. The system allows for the responsibility for ‘industrialised annihilation’ to be shared between coders, operators, legal officers who pre-set the level of collateral damage, officers who approve the strike, officers who choose the bomb and the pilots or drone operators who launch it. ‘I am just a small screw in the system,’ one of the interviewees says, quoting Eichmann’s defence during his trial in Jerusalem. The system that begins with a phone number being put into a computer system ends with an entire family crushed under the rubble.

The aim of the aerial bombardment was more crass than the sophisticated technology may suggest. When Herzi Halevi, chief of the general staff, presented the war cabinet with a summary of the army’s actions in the first 48 hours after 7 October, he celebrated the fact that the air force had already attacked 1500 targets, based on ‘exceptional intelligence and operational capacities’. ‘Why not five thousand?’ Netanyahu reportedly responded. Halevi told him that ‘the army doesn’t have five thousand authorised targets.’ ‘Authorised’ meant people identified as military targets, with an associated level of permitted collateral damage. Netanyahu said he was ‘not interested in targets. Flatten buildings, bomb with whatever you have.’ Some parameters must have been adjusted – to permit less certainty over the target and more collateral damage – because the rate of bombing increased. In the summer of 2025, audio was leaked in which a former head of army intelligence who oversaw the development and deployment of the AI system said that for every Israeli killed on 7 October, fifty Palestinians must die. ‘The fact that there are already fifty thousand dead in Gaza is necessary and required for future generations,’ he said. ‘It doesn’t matter now if they are children.’

Palestinians were murdered by bombs, drones, snipers, tanks, shot as they went to find food, starved because of the destruction of agriculture and the blocking of aid convoys, and killed by preventable diseases. If mass indiscriminate bombing was what was wanted, why bother with an AI system? Part of the answer offered by *NAZA* is that advanced technological tools give murder an air of scientific legitimacy. They allow the Israeli army to claim that it goes to great lengths to distinguish between combatants and civilians and to reduce the number of civilians killed. Israel’s defenders claim that the fact that it calculates ‘collateral damage’ means its assault can’t possibly be classed as genocide. Genocide requires that the killing of civilians is the intent, and the computer system is programmed to regard them as incidental.

Yet as one of *NAZA*’s interviewees confirms, with seventy thousand Palestinians designated as military targets and default collateral damage set at twenty civilians for every low-level operative (and more than a hundred for military and political leaders), ‘in this way you could kill the entirety of Gaza.’ As another interviewee explains, ‘the aim was quantity not quality,’ but – as Stalin said – ‘quantity has a quality all its own.’ Genocide is the sum total of individually targeted strikes. This is what the technology researcher Júlia Nueno Guitart, writing about the use of AI in Gaza, has labelled ‘individuated mass killing’. The killing of civilians and the destruction of homes and infrastructure was the intent, not the ‘collateral effect’, of the bombing; as technologists often say, ‘it was a feature not a bug.’

In Israel the filmmakers were described as traitors following the film’s enthusiastic reception at the Venice Film Festival in September. Mobs hunted for them and their families. Without having seen the film, Eyal Zamir, the present chief of the general staff, called its revelations false, a ‘blood libel’, and then, without noticing the contradiction, launched an investigation into who had leaked secret information. Netanyahu announced that the government would work to strip the two directors of their citizenship. The same Israeli officials who declared that there were no innocents in Gaza are now outraged by a film showing that this is exactly the principle the military has acted on.

The film is quieter and more contemplative than I expected. It is hauntingly shot on the rooftops of Tel Aviv’s boom-town buildings, high rises that have sprung up in recent decades thanks to investment in the Israeli tech industry, much of which trades in ‘security products’ tested on Palestinians. The people who develop and operate the mass targeting apparatus are mostly members of Unit 8200, whose graduates often found security start-ups for the Israeli tech bubble. Although the film doesn’t show it, from the rooftops of the southern suburbs of Tel Aviv one can see the flash of bombs exploding in Gaza at night. The interviewees belong to a cohort of liberal Israelis where, as one of them says, ‘no one hates Arabs.’ They are not settlers or far-right extremists but secular, educated, technologically sophisticated ‘liberals’ – the class who end up, as the same interviewee admits, ‘killing most Arabs’. The film is a story about two cities, one of steel and glass towers and another of refugee camps. The growth of the first is the result of the destruction of the second. The AI system presents the coloniser as rational, in a mirror image of the presentation of the indigenous resistance as irrational, emphasising the distance between the supposedly post-human coloniser and the barely human colonised.

In 1755 an earthquake in the Atlantic led to a tsunami that devastated Lisbon. Thirty thousand died. The destruction of Lisbon shocked Europe and brought up moral and political questions not unlike the ones the rubble of Gaza now raises. In 1758, in the wake of the disaster, Voltaire wrote *Candide*. The character Pangloss is a parody of Leibniz, who reconciled the existence of disasters with the presence of God by conceiving the latter as a perfect mathematician solving a maximum/minimum problem in the calculus of optimisation. The destruction and pain God inflicts on the world are always perfectly calibrated as the very minimum they can be. The world, for Pangloss, is always the ‘best of all possible worlds’, or the least of all possible evils. The total ruination of 18th-century Lisbon, like that of 21st-century Gaza, makes a mockery of such calculations.
