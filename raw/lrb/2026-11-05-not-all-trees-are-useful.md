# 正文

In​ 2021, a refugee crisis unfolded on the Poland-Belarus border. Having been lured to Minsk with tourist visas, hotel packages and rumours of obliging smugglers, desperate people – many fleeing war zones in the Middle East – hoped to cross into EU territory through the ancient Białowieża woodlands. On reaching the militarised zone, most were turned back by Polish forces. Writing at the time, Sophie Pinkham argued that Alexander Lukashenko had engineered this ‘spectacle of confrontation’ between vulnerable migrants and unyielding border guards as a vengeful response to Europe’s criticism of Belarus’s human rights record. Some refugees escaped into the trees. Still hoping to make it over the border, many spent weeks as outlaws in the forest, wet, hungry, thirsty. The forest was both their refuge and a new terror. ‘Damp pervaded everything,’ Pinkham wrote. ‘The foetid autumn leaves swallowed up any sound. Who knew what creatures were lurking in there.’

Białowieża – a rich ecosystem of oaks, limes, ash, hornbeams, fungi, birds, bison, insects, rare types of moss, bees – was for many centuries a playground for the elite (hunting appealed to medieval monarchs and Soviet Politburo members alike) as well as a shelter for religious radicals and for partisans and fugitives in war. It stands at one end of Moscow’s sphere of influence; at another is the Siberian taiga, with pines, spruces and larch trees. Today about a fifth of the world’s forest cover is in Russia. In *The Oak and the Larch*, Pinkham asks how these landscapes have shaped Russian history, as an economic and cultural resource underpinning the imperial project, but also, sometimes, a site for its subversion.

She begins at the eastern extreme, where ‘brown, squelching swamp’ cedes to Arctic tundra. Here, eight thousand years ago, humans developed technologies to extract the forest’s wealth. Hunter-gatherers constructed weirs to trap the fish of the Ob River and built a fortified settlement (at Amnya) to store and protect their catch from raiders. In the medieval era, Indigenous hunters – Khanty, Mansi, Selkup, Nenets – traded sable, squirrel and stoat pelts. As Europeans began to cut down their own woods to make way for farming, Siberian furs became an ever more coveted commodity. Invaders came from the east, though Siberia or ‘shibir’ – a Mongol word meaning ‘forest on a marsh’ – was tough terrain for armies on horseback. Eastern Slavs also desired the furs, and once they had freed themselves from Mongol rule, set about making the region their own. They sent in Cossack troops to crush resistance and build fortifications, followed by tribute collectors who brought sugar, tobacco, alcohol and weapons to seduce the Indigenous people. But by the late 1600s, the most important commodity from Russia’s forests was no longer fur or fish; it was the trees themselves. The expanding Russian Empire required a naval fleet to defend itself: oaks, larch and pine were chopped down to build ships. Forests became a resource to be managed. Peter I issued scores of decrees designating forests as state reserves; later Catherine the Great, influenced by European practices of forest management, sought to make the extraction of timber rational and sustainable.

But even as the tsarist regime sought to tame its woodlands, another vision of the forest – as wild, romantic, sublime – served the imperial project rather differently. On the southern rim of the empire, the forested mountains of the northern Caucasus thwarted Russian advances into Georgia. Here the colonisers faced fierce resistance from Muslim fighters who, under Imam Shamil’s leadership in the 1830s, adopted guerrilla tactics, using the woods to ambush the invaders; in response, deforestation became one of the Russian army’s most important tactics. Yet the threatening beauty of the woods also made them a place where Russian national identity could be forged. ‘The staggering mountains, the thick forests and their fierce inhabitants,’ Pinkham writes, ‘would inspire generations of Russian writers, becoming central to Russia’s imperial imagination.’ In the works of Pushkin and Lermontov, young, disillusioned Russian aristocrats find freedom in the Caucasus, falling in love with its landscape and its women, who must be sacrificed for the Russian nation to thrive. Other writers located the nation’s identity in the gentle birch woods of central Russia. As a young man, Tolstoy also adventured in the Caucasus, but he settled on his estate outside Moscow, conceiving of it as a place where peasants and gentry might come together. He planted trees, though – with regret – also sold off parcels of woodland when his debts were high.

Following revolution and victory in the civil wars of 1918-22, the Bolsheviks inherited almost all the tsars’ territory. At first glance, trees might seem to have little future in an age of relentless industrial advance. Chekhov had already predicted that, once emancipated, former serfs would set about chopping down the cherry orchards. The revolution promised mastery over nature and the building of new mechanised landscapes. ‘The current arrangement of mountains and rivers, fields and meadows, steppes, forests and seashores can in no way be called final,’ Trotsky wrote in 1923. ‘Man has already made some changes, and not small ones, to the picture of nature; but these are only student experiments in comparison with what will be … In the end he will rebuild the earth, if not in his own image, then in his own taste.’ In an early propaganda poster, an iconic red chimney stack becomes the lungs of the nation. The slogan reads: ‘Smoke from the chimney stack is Soviet Russia breathing.’

Incentives failed to attract sufficient migrant labour to do the hard work of felling trees, so new residents were brought to the forests: prisoners. The White Sea-Baltic Canal – a forced labour project celebrated in state media in the early 1930s – saw great swathes of the Karelian forests uprooted for prisoners to dig a passage from the interior to the Baltic Sea. Tens of thousands died. The forested expanses of Siberia were also targeted. ‘As Stalin’s purges escalated in the 1930s,’ Pinkham writes, ‘more and more citizens found themselves forcibly returned to the inhuman music of the taiga, conscripted in the project of transforming nature to serve Soviet needs.’ For a time, forestry was one of the Gulag’s largest industries. Survivors describe learning to saw down trees in freezing temperatures, pushing themselves to extremes to meet impossible quotas and avoid further punishment (denial of food rations, solitary confinement).

Yet even during the 20th century, the story of Russia’s forests isn’t only one of destruction. Some early Bolsheviks worried about the effect of their loss on the environment. After the Second World War, Stalin too came to recognise – in his own way – the potential benefits of tree planting. In 1948 he announced his Great Plan for the Transformation of Nature, promising to plant six million hectares of forest to protect collective farms from harsh winds and drought. A propaganda poster shows him shoulder to shoulder with two young rural workers, and behind them tidy squares of yellow crop fields hemmed with the green of new trees. Trofim Lysenko, soon to be discredited, claimed that the newly planted trees would become active agents in the struggle for a new agricultural order in the flatlands of the steppe, joining ranks to defeat the enemy weeds.

Alongside the Promethean, even ecocidal, treatment of the USSR’s natural resources, there were complex and contradictory approaches to nature under state socialism. Not only did environmental movements, however marginal, survive even in the worst years of terror, but the officials tasked with managing the forests realised the imperative to preserve them in a scientifically defensible manner. Leonid Leonov, a stalwart of Stalinist literature, wrote a novel called *The Russian Forest*, in which two forestry professors embody the ‘right’ (preservation) and the ‘wrong’ (destruction) way to treat the country’s trees. The official response to his work shows the regime’s continued ambivalence. Leonov was awarded the Lenin Prize in 1957, but Khrushchev cautioned: ‘Not all trees are useful. From time to time the forest must be thinned.’

For Leonov and writers who followed in his stead, these were Russia’s forests. But this claim did not always go uncontested. Just as Imam Shamil’s rebels had used the woods to defend their lands against tsarist incursions in the previous century, so did nationalist partisans in the Soviet era. In western Ukraine and the Baltic states, wartime partisans turned against the Soviet occupiers, with ‘forest brothers’ holding out in some places until the mid-1950s. For pacifists, the forest offered the possibility for quiet resistance. Religious dissidents had for centuries retreated into the woods to escape persecution, and they continued to do so under state socialism. In 1978, geologists travelling by helicopter over the seemingly unpeopled forested mountains of southern Siberia spotted a dwelling; it turned out to be the home of an Old Believer family who had fled there in the 1930s to escape Stalinist violence. The youngest of the family had never seen a wheel.

Later in the book, Pinkham turns to Ukraine, and a different forest emerges. Following the Chernobyl nuclear disaster in 1986, the town of Pripyat was abandoned and all its markers of Soviet modernity – apartment blocks, factories, funfairs – were reclaimed by the trees. Three years ago, in the war-torn eastern provinces, the immense Kakhovka dam (a symbol of Soviet progress in the 1950s) was blown up, either by Russian forces or by a Ukrainian missile, depending on which side’s media you believe. A hundred people died and eighty villages were flooded. Within a year, willows and poplar were growing in the reservoir; now it is Europe’s largest flood-plain forest. ‘A new, ecologically grounded form of Ukrainian nationalism,’ Pinkham writes, ‘embraces the return of landscapes not seen for many decades.’

Yet this isn’t quite the book’s end. Pinkham closes with an excursion to the ‘ancient, forested heartland of Muscovy’. In the 1990s, a group of artists and architects resettled from Moscow to a hamlet in Kaluga region and began an artistic collaboration with locals, using snow and wood as materials for sculpture. In 2006, the site hosted its first annual festival, Archstoyanie, and its landscape installations became a major tourist attraction. Soon ultra-nationalists began to circle. Writing about the project, Putin’s spin doctor Vladislav Surkov claimed: ‘Two Romes have fallen, but the third still stands, made not of iron or stone but, impossibly, of wood.’

While acknowledging Surkov’s attempt to appropriate the project, Pinkham concludes: ‘Amid the enchantments of Archstoyanie and the woods, river and meadows of Ugra Park, it is possible to imagine an alternative reality: one in which there is no nation only landscape.’ Possible, certainly, and appealing. Yet the prospect of a landscape without national affiliation runs very much against the grain of her book, which shows just how hard it is to liberate ‘nature’ from the political. The rich forests stretching from Białowieża to Baikal and beyond have long been bound up with the material and cultural needs of a national project, and the Russian state – though not unchallenged – has almost always come out on top.
