# 正文

In the aftermath​ of a devastating assault, Jordan Brown, one of the narrators of Marlon James’s new novel, returns to the scene of the crime.

> For real I’ve been daring to come back to this place but couldn’t find the right reason. Not true, I have a hundred reasons, a new one for each day. Sometimes I thought that if I just go back and take my own damn time to leave that I regain something that they took away, no, I don’t fucking know what. Maybe I regain freedom to leave. To choose. I don’t know. Other times I thought if I went back I would learn something, but I don’t know what that is. Maybe if I knew what it was then I wouldn’t need to learn it. These are the thoughts that start wars in my head whenever my mind lands on this place. Other times I picture going in and the first thing I smell is blood, or flesh, or iron, something that scream violence. Or maybe I would smell me. Or maybe all the screaming became its own thing and never stopped. But I was not smelling or hearing anything. I don’t fucking know why I wanted to come back here, but now that I was here I wanted to both stay and leave.

The place referred to is a real location, a house on Lady Musgrave Road in Kingston, but it stands in for the book’s emotional territory, profoundly oppressive and impossible to dismiss.

Homosexuality played a part in James’s *A Brief History of Seven Killings*, winner of the Booker Prize in 2015, but here it is the whole. The major characters are all gay men, with ages ranging from around twenty (in the case of Jordan and the narrator of the opening section, Eddy Porteous) to their fifties. The senior figure is Havelock Titus, who has the idea of rehearsing a play in his home (the house on Lady Musgrave Road) and recruits cast members by leaving flyers in places both likely and unlikely – Eddy picks one up at the J.R. Wong supermarket. In this cultural context the phrase ‘drama audition’ is enough, as one potential actor remarks, to ‘weed out normal man’. In the book’s Jamaica the slightest action or accusation can trigger violence. Two boys can be pulled out of the toilets at the Tropical Plaza mall and beaten because someone said they went into the same stall. When they take refuge in a pharmacy a mob starts throwing rocks at the windows, since by offering protection the owner has shown that he is a battyman too. The police don’t arrive for an hour, although the station is across the street and the mall is constantly patrolled, and when they do they beat the boys in front of the crowd before taking them away. This horror story is a rumour, but it’s a plausible one. Eddy sees with his own eyes the smoke from the burning of all the mattresses in the student dorms of the university he attends. ‘That is how you stop it,’ he is told. ‘Stop what?’ ‘The Aids.’ The book begins in May 1988, not the best time to be a gay man anywhere, let alone here.

The play being cast and rehearsed is an odd choice: John Herbert’s *Fortune and Men’s Eyes*, first staged in 1967 and adapted for film in 1971. It’s set in a prison and shows the power structure that establishes itself in a world without women, and the degradation that follows. To choose this text to be workshopped in a private setting – no public performance is proposed – indicates something closer to an encounter group than a theatre production, though a stage manager puts in an appearance at rehearsals.

Why not a marginally more seductive drama, such as Mart Crowley’s *The Boys in the Band*? This suggestion comes from Jordan (‘I want some real food, man, not a bag o’chips’) but is immediately rejected by Havelock, who went to New York with a group of friends to see the play in its first production in 1968 – ‘Fruit life? On a stage? Talking for real?’ – but regards it as toxic. The stage manager, Clive, backs him up, saying that after he watched the movie version he burned the VHS in his backyard, said two Hail Marys and got himself a wife. Crowley’s harsh comedy suggests that gay men’s difficulties are largely internal, and so might with effort be overcome. That’s the more likely reason for the rejection of the play in favour of Herbert’s, which is every bit as negative. A play about the distortions of life behind bars is closer to Jamaican reality than one about residual guilt and shame but without active persecution.

Homophobia isn’t the boiling oil poured down from the ramparts of this culture so much as the foundation stone without which it would collapse. A father beats his son for allowing his wrist to droop. Eventually the son will be strong enough to take his revenge. Instead of regretting the violence that will be turned back on him, the father congratulates himself on a job well done. He has made his son a man.

There can be no desire without fear – under other circumstances this might be no more than an aperçu about ambivalence. Here it simply describes the vulnerability of sexual minorities. The first section of the book recounts not just the process of rehearsing the play but Eddy’s explorations of the world around him, as recorded in his journal. The ambient threats, such as a stop sign doctored with the words ‘AIDS. KILL A FAGGOT’, never lose their intimidating force, reminding him that behind any wall there may be a mob ‘watching for me to catch fright so they will know to ambush me, beat me the fuck up, rape my ass with a baton or mopstick, flagpole, whatever, and then do to me what they do to black people in the American South’. The wording makes it clear that the intimidation is designed to provoke the flinch that invites violence.

It’s hardly surprising that Eddy doesn’t dare pick up Prince’s new album, *Lovesexy*, from the rack at RadioShack, asking a female friend to do it for him. ‘Maybe there is a sort of man who can walk up to a record with a naked man with a perm on the cover, a singer that half of Jamaica already thinks is a faggot, and buy it like he buying a bag of mints.’ Eddy is not that man. It’s surprising, though, that in this atmosphere of dread he should risk having sex in public, on the slender assurance that the botanical gardens are safe. The last government cut the lighting budget, the new one hasn’t restored it and – relax! – there’s no moon. When he has sex with a stranger in a toilet in the New Kingston Shopping Centre he is reproducing the situation that supposedly conjured a bloodthirsty mob in another mall. All that protects him is the ruse of stepping into the shopping bag the stranger lays in front of him, so that a spying eye at floor level will see two legs, not four.

Eddy relentlessly polices his body language, wondering how much it testifies against him, and works hard to crack the coded signals of others:

> A look away means he’s not interested or he’s straight and don’t understand. Or he knows when a battyman checking for him and will either keep quiet, demand money, or scream *that batty bwoi deh ’pon him*. How long is too long and how long is not long enough? There’s a look that says *I don’t want men* and there’s a look that says *I don’t want you*, and fuck if I know the diff.

At home, with his family away, Eddy stares at the mirror and takes stock of what he has to offer, from the Grace Jones cheekbones that don’t stop his face from looking like a rectangle (or so he thinks) to the lips that represent the only perfect blending in his body of his parents’ genetic input – they’re ‘thick like a black boy’s but pink like my mother’s’. He describes the birthmark on his back that he needs two mirrors to see, his chest, his arms – the triceps are the only body part he can show off, thanks to the swimming he used to do – and his penis, which is darker than his palms, lighter than his face. It’s a strong passage, but at nearly ten pages long stretches the conventions of the diary entry to breaking point. When exactly in this mildly narcissistic reverie is he taking his eyes off the mirror and reaching for pen and paper? It’s just as obvious that taking mental notes at rehearsals so as to transcribe the group’s interactions afterwards would require the memory Truman Capote had, or said he had.

If someone happened to read Eddy’s journal, it would be dramatically relevant that this is in theory a written document rather than sophisticated literary narration. It doesn’t happen. In fact, for a gay Jamaican teenager he isn’t particularly vulnerable to being spied on – his Belgian mother insists on calling herself a modern woman, though what this means in practice is that she doesn’t want to hear anything that would require her to live up to that description. If he caused a scandal his prosperous family would send him abroad rather than kick him out. And even at risky moments he has the advantage of looking more white than not:

> She looks at me too deep. My hair is straight, but it’s black, my lips are pink, but they are thick, my skin is light, too light to just be brown and any country boy can be brown, but not light enough to be the kind of white you don’t fuck with even when they are wrong. Meaning she is judging whether she can cuss me out or not.

When the cast members exchange their memories of growing up gay it’s clear that Eddy’s sufferings have been mild. His brother pretended not to know him at school after hearing him referred to on his second day as Edwina. In this context an absence of physical violence counts as a victory. One of the cast members, Orrin, grew up in the country, and for a while it seemed as if there was room there for someone who didn’t fit into gender roles: ‘You know like how every place have them madman but is fi them madman, you see me? From ever since me could remember, me was them Miss Whorina. Yes, them tease me and sometime trip me or threaten to beat me, but mostly them leave me alone since me never bother nobody.’ This can’t last. Orrin is beaten and buried alive in the cemetery, surviving only because a guard dog makes his attackers disperse. The preacher’s verdict? ‘Boys will be boys.’

The shadows in the novel are already so deep that it comes as a shock to realise that the month of May, as recorded in Eddy’s journal, is the book’s closest approach to springtime, a period almost of innocence. In June a play rehearsal is interrupted by a brutal attack that leaves one person dead and another blinded. The only one who isn’t hurt is Eddy, who wasn’t there – his parents were breaking up at the time.

The assault is described, over 25 pages, from the point of view of the perpetrators. There is a terrible virtuosity in the writing of these polyphonic monologues: ‘We thump him in the mouth and he flash a white smile. We thump him in the mouth again, he flash a pink smile. We thump him in the mouth again, he flash a red smile.’ These Hemingway cadences collide with sinister bureaucratic boilerplate. Some of the attackers are policemen out of uniform, who have prepared for the raid by spying on the house: ‘Several persons of interest were seen entering and leaving the property on a daily basis.’

Hatred draws nourishment from interpretations of scripture, from politics either racial (‘according to Black Israel it’s sexual sin that birthed the white race in the first place’) or national: ‘Unnatural forces set a course for this land ever since this damn blasted 70s government tried to establish socialism.’ Peer pressure plays a part, as does the refusal of self: ‘Kill it in them, kill it in me.’ A well-meaning television programme intended to give Aids a human face by interviewing a woman infected by blood transfusions can be disregarded, her refusal to blame others swept aside: ‘And none of it is her fault but that of them who go down to Kingston Harbour to buy and sell batty and don’t care how much like shit round back there smells, then after that take a stop to defile glorious vagina.’ It’s refreshing that offence is being taken on behalf of a body part rather than a gender, since this is a subject about which women feel so much less strongly than men. If female enthusiasm were required for its survival, homophobia would have died out long ago.

Some participants in the violence get carried away. Some find the proceedings too intense and need to take a break, as if they were watching a horror film rather than acting one out. Others feel cheated that they were told not to bring guns and have to make do with clubs. Hatred can reach the point of losing all sense of an object: ‘Let the dark and the heat boil you into a stew, let the heat go from fire in your skin to fire in your mind and blame that on them too blame them for even the dark.’ Or it can aspire to sadistic connoisseurship: ‘You think fear is just one thing, but shit’s got layers, bro. The eyes get wet with tears, but dark, too, because nigga’s angry, and wide because he still can’t stop being scared out of his fucking shit. It’s wild, yo, fuck drugs, the look in that bitch’s eye is better than sniffing coke off Real Roxanne’s tits.’ This righteous vigilante seems to be experiencing something close to arousal.

Despite the cruel gloating, the group’s resistance takes their assailants by surprise. Eventually the emergency services are called, presumably by one of the attackers. The casualties are sent to a public hospital, making them vulnerable all over again once the headlines start. There’s an attempt to charge them with manslaughter, since the assault is being characterised as an orgy that turned violent (‘HOMO KILLS HOMO AT SEX PARTY’). This threat is made to go away once Jordan practises some discreet blackmail on a closeted local plutocrat, and thanks to the same reluctant benefactor the injured are transferred to the relative safety of a private hospital. In the aftermath two of the men, the ‘disappearers’ of the title, drop out of sight. Only Eddy and Jordan seem concerned, and try to find out what has become of them.

So much for the characters, but what of the reader? We are left, a third of the way through a long book, with a feeling of absolute outrage, when outrage is no more than anger in the key of helplessness. Outrage leads nowhere; violence freezes time for the victim; self-hatred paralyses every other emotion. These are all problems for a long narrative that needs to find a dynamism distinct from local intensity. *The Disappearers* seems to be constructed along the same large lines as its acclaimed predecessor, though *A Brief History of Seven Killings* included swathes of Jamaican political history and needed the space, as the new book does not.

Eddy and Jordan’s search functions mainly to bring new suburbs of hell into view – including, memorably, a rescue from an abruptly materialising mob, engineered by a group of trans kids living in a filthy gully below a highway. Eddy has a certain amount of survivor’s guilt about not being there on the night of the attack, while Jordan’s experience of atrocity takes him on a long journey towards revenge. Even before that night, their relationship was 90 per cent intellectual competition, 10 per cent sexual tension. Jordan has been living in his car since he left home after his father had struck him, by his count, for the 5760th time. He makes ends meet by selling himself. This gives him the edge in terms of worldly knowledge, as well as the leverage for that emergency blackmail.

Eddy prefers Auden to Eliot and knows the difference in atmosphere between Len Deighton and John le Carré. Jordan’s reading is more tightly focused, but their joint specialist subject would be 20th-century gay culture:

> ‘What you have to say ’bout Gide?’
>
> ‘*If It Die* or *The Counterfeiters*?’
>
> ‘Shit, man. *Corydon*.’

One of the many things that make this book painful to read is that its characters are so well versed in a literature that has only the dimmest relevance to their lives. The very different worlds of *Finistère* and *Dancer from the Dance* are equally remote from their experience and prospects – in gay fiction the worst danger is rejection, while in their lives it’s being burned alive.

Second-hand knowledge (C.A. Tripp for psychology, John Boswell for pre-modern history) has done nothing to raise their self-esteem. Early in the book Eddy sneers that ‘homos must be the only men who will send a fist all the way up your bottom and a tongue past your tonsils before they tell you what their parents call them.’ Why would that be, I wonder? Perhaps because exchanging names with a casual contact in 1980s Jamaica makes both of you vulnerable, as it did in 1950s London. Trustworthiness can crumble under a threat, a beating, an interrogation. In a repressive environment casual contacts are simply less risky, because less visible, than established relationships. Lives that have been forbidden to unfold inevitably seem two-dimensional.

The strangest item on Jordan’s reading list is John Rechy’s *The Sexual Outlaw*, published in 1977, which he takes more or less as a guide to life. When I read it around that time it seemed impossible to take this hypermasculine caricature at face value, as Jordan seems to, living by what he takes to be a samurai code while actually running scared from any reciprocation. It’s possible that James is signalling Jordan’s emotional paralysis by the various admiring references to *The Sexual Outlaw*, though that’s quite a risk to take with a book so little read. ‘*Promiscuity, like the priesthood, requires total commitment and sacrifice.*’ The only problem Jordan has with this ludicrous dictum is that he doesn’t want to commit to anything.

If fear has layers then so does self-hatred. The answer to the question on page 218 – ‘Where are we supposed to put all this hate?’ – has always been: inside ourselves. This isn’t masochism but osmosis. The obsessive policing of body language has an equivalent in the realm of moral behaviour, where any sign of the supposed ‘faggot impulse for drama’ is pounced on. Eddy, admitting to Jordan that he has been dropping off clothes, food and money to the ‘gully girls’, denigrates his own impulse by saying: ‘The gesture more symbolic than anything. But you know how B-boys love a symbol.’ As if by giving real help to these outcasts he was doing no more than striking a pose.

There seems to be no limit to the resourcefulness of self-hatred. Jordan brings back from Miami a book by ‘this homo called Paul Monette’ – presumably the memoir *Borrowed Time*. He wants to learn about Aids, though he knows all he will learn ‘is what white man will show you because they seem to be the only ones brave enough to teach’. Is this just bravery, or is there some sense of entitlement even within catastrophe? (Though that doesn’t make the testimony any less valuable.) One way or another, there doesn’t seem to be any logic driving the next sentence’s turn to racial scapegoating: ‘Or maybe the black man who would write is instead going home to the wife he is about to take to bed and kill.’ All the cultural icons in the book are imported white goods, Prince a rare exception. Eddy worships Bruce Springsteen, particularly his mid-section (‘if he turns around to show his backside, right above the crack will be a patch of sweat in a perfect V’), as if fetishistically programmed by the cover design of *Born in the USA*, while Jordan has tender feelings, not at all his usual style, for Kurt Cobain.

At the far end​ of the book, in a strong concluding section, there’s a partial explanation of the disappearances. But long before this point readers have been cued to favour the symbolic aspect of the situation. It’s hard to miss someone who was hardly there. Violence traps the victim in the present, but no battyboy thinks about the future anyway, ‘because it might not include us.’ Even Aids is just more of the same, according to Jordan, constituting a series of disappearing acts:

> First is watching the friends and family disappear. Second act is when people try to disappear you. Third act is watching your body vanish from itself … until all of you gone. I swear to God, I used to think people with Aids just evaporate. If there is a fourth act it’s when everybody erase your name as if you were never born in the first place.

Jordan tells a journalist who interviews him that gay men can’t even tell lies, which would imply ‘that they have some kinda reality to distort, when the truth is they have nothing’. In private conversation Havelock goes even further:

> when something happen to normal people they leave the space behind. People will fill up a box with funeral programme, or put ashes in a spot a tall man or a big woman used to hold and believe that the person take up the same space. Even when normal man leave him mother yard, she look at him room and the emptiness just beat her. But when battyman leave or die he don’t leave no space. He don’t leave no empty bed with a sink in the mattress. He don’t make a mother talk about he used to not throw out the empty milk carton. He just gone and the world just swallow that empty space up like him never did born.

It’s not easy to square this speech with the younger Havelock, who left the Gay Liberation Front because they wanted equality while he wanted revenge, but people are allowed to change.

Occasionally there’s an attempt made to examine internalised homophobia, as in this rather stilted exchange between sexual partners familiar with each other, though far short of a couple:

> ‘You need fi leggo this.’
>
> ‘Sure, as soon as you leggo shame first,’ I said.
>
> ‘Batty is not pussy.’
>
> ‘Don’t bring up that fucking song with me. Don’t ever bring up that fucking song.’
>
> ‘Is just a song. No song ram the dancehall like that track, tell you that.’
>
> ‘Yeah? That woman you grinding know you have a pussy, too?’
>
> ‘Fuck off.’
>
> ‘Winding your damn waist to a song telling people to kill you.’
>
> ‘Is just a song, brethren.’
>
> ‘And a bullet is just metal.’

The journalist who interviews Jordan and Eddy is called Peta-Gaye Burke. Her questions are edited out, leaving answers that are often, in Jordan’s case, defiantly aggressive: ‘Bitch, trust me. If I was being hostile, you would fucking know.’ Some resistance is understandable – a native like Havelock may maintain that the dead man was killed by Jamaica itself (‘Not one man or two. This whole fucking island kill him’), but outsiders can’t be allowed to generalise. Burke is writing for an American gay magazine called *Out*, though she seems to be Jamaican herself, to judge by one of Jordan’s comments: ‘I don’t care if it’s just another article about how us Jamaicans are brutish and brutal, because we are. No use running from it. I am one and so are you.’

It was made clear early in the book that gay politics is an alien import (‘American business’) irrelevant to the island, when one of the actors, Rainford, shut Eddy down by denouncing the desire to go on marches ‘and wear rainbow panty and all that “we’re here, we’re queer” fuckery’. Still, Jordan eventually admits, despite his own generalisations about Burke (as part of a group – ‘you lesbians’ – who supposedly exploit suffering), that her work is important. He even says: ‘Sorry about last Saturday.’

It feels like a breath of fresh air when a walk-on character, the bar owner Miss Sandy, suggests that gay Jamaicans are ‘so damn frighten to expose ourselves that we doing battyman hater work for them’. He had an epiphany: ‘Me say to myself, *Sandy, you pretty little bitchlette, maybe, just maybe we are whole other way of living as people.*’ Positivity is hard won in this world. Less than ten pages later Sandy is dead, stabbed 72 times with an ice pick. The police leave his body lying in his living room for a whole day, while crowds gather outside to sing Streggeh Don’s ‘Batty Is Not Pussy’. This artist is no friend of either bodily opening, to judge by another of his lyrics: *slice up the pussy and gouge out the meat*. He has hatred to spare.

The time scheme of the book is extended, or perhaps violated, by a fourth section made up of letters written (some of them unsent) by Havelock in the late 1990s to an old friend, Mark Armstrong-Parker, and reaching back to their student days at Oxford around 1950. It’s not clear how this history adds to the Jamaican narrative, and as with the device of the interview, only one side of the correspondence is given. Readers must work to understand a relationship that contains a strong element of exploitation – Mark used to sell Havelock’s sexual favours to his posh friends – as well as lasting goodwill, since Mark pays for the combination therapy that keeps Aids survivors like Havelock alive.

This section is comically slipshod. The university setting is a travesty, with the basic terms of Oxford student life replaced by their American equivalents, such as ‘summer break’. Order in the college, ‘musty old St Cross’, would not be maintained by ‘security’ or ‘night watchmen’ but by the college porters, who would be unlikely to expel a member of college, but certain to be interested in how the four ladies carousing in the college hall late at night had got past them. A sentence like this one is largely composed of blunders: ‘The school year ended and while my grades were high enough to graduate with honours, they weren’t exceptional enough to merit a postgraduate scholarship.’ Students at Oxford were (and are) assessed in final exams, while ‘graduating with honours’ isn’t a distinction like *cum laude* in the US: a third-class degree is still a degree with honours. And if Havelock graduated, how was he ‘kicked out of Oxford’? Any number of solecisms can be forgiven when there is a compelling story to tell, but his involvement with an earl, no less, called Everton Caulfield is hard to care about. Caulfield may be a nasty piece of work but he didn’t necessarily need to be outed and destroyed by having his love letters sent to the *Daily Mail.* The hundred pages of this section would have been better devoted to material that broadened the scope of the book, rather than a past whose relevance is moot.

It’s hard to compare the ordeals of Jamaican gay men with the horrors, including ‘corrective’ rape, suffered by the island’s gay women. At various points in *The Disappearers* the cultural and political continuity of these atrocities is acknowledged – the fact that they are part of the same agenda – but gay women hardly appear. James has contrived a possible way in to this material by having Jordan father a child with a gay woman, but her presence in the text, where she is described as more macho than Hulk Hogan and just as fond of yellow briefs, is hardly fuller than her profile in the list of characters: ‘Gwendolyn, big lesbian mother of Jordan’s child’. What has Gwendolyn’s life been like? If the current thinking is that a gay male novelist shouldn’t appropriate the experiences of gay women, then the alliance-based activism that was strong when the book is set – at least in the US, thanks to ACT UP – has yielded to scruple that lacks any other goals. One way of describing the status of women would be as a virtual minority, numerically equal but systematically deterred from claiming that status. Ally them to gay men and you have an actual majority – perhaps that’s the root of what, etymologically, homophobia characterises as fear and misogyny as hatred, though the two emotions run together like blobs of mercury.

When university students from comfortable backgrounds insist that ‘a man with just one woman must be a battyman,’ homosexuality is being seen as overwhelmingly powerful – heterosexual monogamy isn’t enough to keep it at bay. This strange homage to minority taste is present in any society that sees the need to protect what is supposedly a natural, self-evident and healthy orientation with legislation discriminating against any alternatives, as Britain did until the ages of consent were equalised in 2001 (Northern Ireland held out until 2008), but the obsession is rarely so naked. The hatred of women and that of homosexuals are not just loosely connected, because they tend to be felt by the same people, but tightly bound together in a way that resists examination, not just overlapping but somehow symbolically identical.

The expletive heavy lifting in the book is done by a small handful of related words – ‘bomboclaat’, ‘raasclaat’, ‘bloodclaat’. The few female characters don’t seem to have an aversion to using them, though perhaps they should, since the words express an aversion to them. All of these terms refer to menstrual cloths, to the vagina seen as both contaminated and contaminating. Orifices and hygienic taboos collide in the patois, to the point where it’s hard to tell them apart, hard to be sure what is arousing disgust. Jordan may insist at one point that the bloodclaat is not a body part, but twenty pages later he is drawn into a similar confusion: ‘Tell me how you’re going to cut up my bomboclaat and thump down me pussyclaat.’

Readers will realise sooner or later that Streggeh Don is fictional, though his cultural presence is integral to the mood of the book, a grotesque apotheosis of the tradition that preaches hate in dancehall music. Writers and performers must bear some responsibility for what happens when others hear lyrics like ‘Look thru the window/Me see two man ah bow/Send fi automatic/And pow pow pow’ and put the sentiment into practice. Are these musicians true haters or opportunists whose thuggery is a lucrative performance? The second possibility is supported by details that emerge late in the book – Streggeh Don comes from a relatively comfortable background, with three A-levels and a maths teacher for a mother.

For James this may be too weak an indictment of those who peddle hate. His remedy is to have Streggeh Don take part in the atrocity on Lady Musgrave Road. In fact, he is responsible for its most appalling element, Shakespearean in the worst way: the plucking out of a man’s eyes. There’s clearly a difference between pandering to the base impulses of the record-buying public and reproducing the most purely sadistic act in Western high culture, but James, though hardly afraid of extremes, seems to hesitate between those alternatives. If ‘Jamaica’s most popular musician’ took part in the assault, would this not become known, perhaps even celebrated? It certainly makes nonsense of the performative-thuggery thesis.

Though the book lists its characters in groups on the opening pages, Streggeh Don’s name appears separately (as ‘the hottest reggae singer’) from those of the Blackjacks – the attackers responsible for ‘two gouged eyes, six broken ribs, two concussions, one collapsed lung, one broken arm, two broken fingers, three fractured arms, one fractured leg, one fractured tibia, two busted noses, two sodomised rectums, one amputated finger, eleven knocked-out teeth, five shattered teeth, one broken shin, one dislocated jaw, three busted lips’ and one corpse. But he was one of them. All the Blackjacks have their say, but not Streggeh Don. He did more than his share of the damage, and one of the other attackers detects a conflicted relish: ‘This singer too raw … Me did want to beat the sinner. Me never say me want to touch the sin. How the singer to write about the sin like so if he didn’t see it. And now he right here in front of me. Is he is the one who beat down the most girly one until he stop move.’

The fact that everyone else provides testimony of that night in 1988 creates a special category for Streggeh Don: sheer evil with no story to tell. Somehow the book’s fierce purpose has been adulterated. Its portrait of a cruel society loses authority by including a made-up bogeyman, a hate figure exempted from any attempt at understanding. This does significant damage to a book whose harshness is only bearable if it is telling the truth.
