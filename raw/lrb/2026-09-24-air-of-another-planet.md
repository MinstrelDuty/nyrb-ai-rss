# 正文

The​ end of a concert performance is sometimes greeted with a rapturous silence, broken only when the players lower their instruments and the audience begins to applaud. What is probably only a few seconds of held breath can feel like an eternity. At Beare’s Chamber Music Festival last December, this honour was bestowed on Schoenberg’s string sextet, *Verklärte Nacht*. Schoenberg would have been surprised. The response, however, owes something to the musical practices and values Schoenberg promoted.

At its premiere in Vienna in 1902, *Verklärte Nacht* was deemed not only dissonant but morally objectionable. The score was prefaced by Richard Dehmel’s poem, in which, on a moonlit walk in the woods, a woman reveals to her lover that she is pregnant by another man. He replies – ‘generously’, in the assessment of Harvey Sachs – that the child will be theirs, transfigured by their love. Over the past century, the moral objections to Dehmel’s scenario have waned and the music’s harmonic swerves are heard as lush rather than lax. The modernity of *Verklärte Nacht* now sounds like one last, glorious gasp of Romanticism, outdone among Schoenberg’s works only by the massive orchestral version of *Gurrelieder*. Robin Holloway has criticised the ‘potential tackiness’ of *Verklärte Nacht*, and music historians tend to place greater emphasis on Schoenberg’s subsequent works despite their being performed less regularly. For Sachs, this ‘thorny character who composed thorny works’ is essential to understanding ‘the past, the present and the future of Western art music’.

Schoenberg himself looked backward and forward. In his commitment to perpetuating the Austro-German canon, he drew on the compositional techniques of his predecessors: the counterpoint of Bach, the classical forms of Mozart, the motivic working of Brahms, the tonal expansion of Wagner. And in a series of pieces composed in the first two decades of the 20th century he self-consciously progressed towards what is now referred to as atonality. Schoenberg disliked the term, proposing instead polytonality or pantonality, which allowed for multiple rather than unrecognisable tonal connections.

He rarely abandoned tonality completely. The Second String Quartet (1908) begins firmly in F sharp minor, before slipping its confines. Schoenberg then flips the hierarchy of consonance and dissonance so that fragments of a familiar tune – ‘Ach, du lieber Augustin’ – seem more alien than its surroundings. The entrance of a soprano voice in the last movement, singing of ‘the air of another planet’, makes a break for unmarked tonal territory; it abandons the safety net of a key signature. But the move was not permanent: in later works, Schoenberg continued to allude to tonality. Even the twelve-note rows of the serial music of the 1920s, in which all notes are declared equal (the emancipation of dissonance, as he put it), sometimes retain tonal associations.

In 1910, Schoenberg explained that he was driven by an ‘inner compulsion’. Forty years later, responding to Adorno’s *Philosophy of New Music*, in which his ‘progressive’ approach was pitted against Stravinsky’s ‘retrogressive’ neoclassicism, Schoenberg attempted to detach that inner compulsion from his compositional technique. ‘*What* I have to say is important to me,’ he remonstrated. ‘But whether I say it with seven, twelve or 21 tones is of very little importance.’ When a young Robert Craft visited Schoenberg he was struck by his humility, ‘plated all the way down with a hubris of stainless steel’. Each of his notes was important.

Schoenberg didn’t take criticism well. The artist should remain independent and unconcerned by the world, he wrote in 1911, so that he can proudly call out: ‘Elemia, Elem-ia’. ‘L-M-I-A’ stands for ‘Leck mich im Arsch’, echoing Götz von Berlichingen’s infamous retort to his would-be captors (which Mozart gleefully made into a canon). Sachs finds a ‘pleasant corrective’ to this vision of the artist as a superior being in Ingmar Bergman’s line, ‘I loathe the whole of this humble attitude towards artists, who really ought to be given a kick in the ass.’ But then, Sachs concedes, Schoenberg received many kicks over the course of his career, one of the most painful being the so-called *Skandalkonzert* of 1913. A programme of music by Schoenberg and his circle (his mentors Zemlinsky and Mahler, and his students Berg and Webern) had to be abandoned when a fight broke out among the audience at Vienna’s Akademische Verband für Literatur und Musik over the ‘nerve-wracking’ music.

Schoenberg’s response to such experiences had a more profound influence on the future reputation of new music than on his own compositions. In 1918 he established the Verein für Musikalische Privataufführungen, a society for private musical performances. Programmes were not announced in advance; there would be no applause (and, just as importantly, no booing or hissing); and critics were not allowed to attend. The repertoire was broad: Debussy, Bartók, Satie and Szymanowski featured alongside the Viennese crowd. The experiment was short-lived. It turned out that even the most exclusive concert series required a paying audience to survive. But the notion that in order to understand new music one had to be in the know has persisted, as has the image of the composer as specialist (which was the original title of Milton Babbitt’s essay of 1958 ‘Who Cares if You Listen?’). At the start of *Schoenberg: Why He Matters*, Sachs declares himself to be Schoenberg-curious rather than a Schoenberg expert; by the end, he admits that although he has been won over by much of the music, some remains difficult because of its ‘*sound*’. The use of italics is telling, indicating that something about this musical experience is untranslatable. The construction of the music can be analysed, but its effect – the *what*, as Schoenberg might have put it – can still defy explanation.

Teaching assumed a significant role in Schoenberg’s career, not simply as a means of financial support but because he came to recognise the need to find people sympathetic to his cause. He acknowledged that the process was reciprocal: ‘I have learned this book from my pupils,’ begins his *Harmonielehre*, published in 1911. According to Eugenie Schwarzwald, who employed Schoenberg alongside Adolf Loos and Oskar Kokoschka at her school in Vienna, he was strict and unpleasant but achieved ‘splendid educational results’. Schoenberg also taught composition privately until, in 1925, he succeeded Busoni as director of a masterclass in composition at the Prussian Academy of Arts in Berlin. Schoenberg was quick to spot the threat of the ‘upstart’ Hitler. Despite his conversion to Protestantism in 1898 he had continued to endure antisemitism. When, on 1 March 1933, the president of the Prussian Academy announced that Jewish influence was to be eliminated, Schoenberg walked out of the meeting. In May, he and his family left for Paris. Schoenberg returned to the Jewish faith – with Marc Chagall as a witness.

The *New York Times* announced the arrival of the Schoenbergs on 31 October 1933, noting that they were accompanied by their terrier, Witz, explained as ‘German for jest’ (a rare public glimpse of the composer’s sense of humour). After a brief stint teaching on the East Coast, Schoenberg, now in his sixties, was drawn to the warmer climate of Southern California. In Los Angeles, his classes attracted Oscar Levant, the film composer Alfred Newman and John Cage. Schoenberg lamented his students’ ignorance but was happy teaching non-musicians. (One of his students was Jackie Robinson, who became the first African American to play in Major League Baseball.) Schoenberg was far from lonely or in the wilderness. He and his second wife, Gertrud, had a young family; he continued to compose; and his earlier works, including the enduringly radical *Pierrot Lunaire*, were beginning to be recorded.

It is only as a teacher that Schoenberg appears in the essay collection *Sonic Circulations: Music, Modernism and the Politics of Knowledge*. Walter Gronostay and Walter Goehr both attended Schoenberg’s masterclass in Berlin in the late 1920s but engaged more enthusiastically than their teacher with new media technologies. The drama of Gronostay’s *Mord*, commissioned for radio, is driven by telephone calls: a factory director catches a conversation between his wife and her lover because a maid accidentally leaves the phone off the hook. The radio drama *Die verhexte Stunde* (‘The Bewitched Hour’), in which a demonic force mysteriously spreads across the world causing catastrophe and death, featured a montage of texts by Ernst Bringolf, combining original material with excerpts from Edgar Allan Poe, Gustav Meyrink and Guy du Maupassant, accompanied by Goehr’s music, which imitated Morse code and used, as John Gabriel explains, ‘dissonant harmonies to project a spooky atmosphere’. Lessons with Schoenberg were being put to good use.

Schoenberg, perhaps hopeful, or deluded, or both, predicted that the man in the street would eventually whistle twelve-tone rows. But he had little time for art ‘for the people’, in the manner of Kurt Weill and Bertolt Brecht. The popularity of Ernst Krenek’s jazz-infused *Jonny spielt auf*, in contrast to Schoenberg’s twelve-tone *Von heute auf morgen*, must have been galling: Krenek’s opera received 421 performances across Europe in its first year and was translated into fourteen languages; Schoenberg’s had its premiere at the Frankfurt Opera in 1930 and was broadcast on the radio that month, but wasn’t performed again during his lifetime. Schoenberg enjoyed the cinema, composing music for an imaginary film that Otto Klemperer suggested László Moholy-Nagy might produce. He was also interested in the radio, but primarily as a means of broadcasting his music and lectures.

Yet Schoenberg’s influence was greater than the index of *Sonic Circulations* might suggest. He played a prominent role in the International Society of Contemporary Music, founded in 1922. Three years later he fell out with the president, Edward Dent – a seemingly trivial spat that Schoenberg attempted to resolve only when his former student Kurt Rankl needed to find work in England in 1940. The ISCM had by then developed from a small annual festival akin to the Verein für Musikalische Privataufführungen into what Giles Masters describes as a powerful tool for shaping modern taste. Entries to participate were determined by nation, with concert programmes functioning much like the pavilions at the Venice Biennale. These internationalist institutions became a way of questioning the assumed superiority of the Austro-German tradition to which Schoenberg subscribed. They also, inevitably, reflected the migration patterns of musicians during the first half of the 20th century.

As​ the academic discipline of musicology came of age, trends in comparative linguistics and sociology were explored, extended, critiqued and – as Peter Asimov points out – applied by composers. The ‘scientific’ search for the ‘primitive’ origins of European art and culture preoccupied Antoine Meillet, a student of Saussure who argued for a single source of Vedic and Greek prosody on the basis of research by the musicologists Pierre Aubry and Maurice Emmanuel. In turn, Emmanuel’s theory of additive rhythm was taken up by his student Olivier Messiaen. Jules Combarieu’s *La Musique et la magie* (1909) argued that secular and religious song had their roots in magical incantation. Although his research has to be read as a response to the work of Henri Hubert and Marcel Mauss, ultimately, according to Alexandra Kieffer, Combarieu’s goal was musical: the only difference between modern music and ‘primitive’ song and incantation was that as ‘art’, modern music no longer served a religious or magical purpose but was an end in itself.

Among the sources Combarieu consulted were Alice Cunningham Fletcher’s observational studies of the musical and ritual practices of the Omaha tribe. Fletcher, who worked at Harvard’s Peabody Museum of Archaeology and Ethnology, wrote as an outsider, and did not attempt to make the connection across cultures on which Combarieu was so keen. She admitted that she found it hard to hear meaning in the sounds she found ‘distressing’ until she ‘began to listen below this noise, much as one must listen to the phonograph, ignoring the sound of the machinery before the registered tones of the voice are caught’. The influence of audio recording on musical experience and understanding recurs throughout *Sonic Circulations*, as authors repeatedly attempted to listen below the noise – though what they hear is sometimes more distressing.

In 1919, Carl Seashore, founder of the psychology department at the University of Iowa, published both *The Psychology of Musical Talent* and *Manual of Instructions and Interpretations for Measures of Musical Talent*, with the latter accompanied by musical aptitude tests on gramophone recordings. *Measures of Musical Talent* was used as part of the admissions process to music programmes, with the result, as Alexander Cowan observes, that ‘a certain status quo ante of the privileged classes in the American musical world [was] perpetuated under the guise of empirical measurement.’ This wasn’t mere cultural gatekeeping: Seashore was committed to the eugenics movement and used screening for innate musical talent to prove the heritability of mental traits.

The political uses of music during the interwar period transformed its reception and its circulation via media technologies. The sense of a deepening gulf between elite and vernacular music cultures was challenged by the use of melodies from Verdi’s operas for workers’ songs in Turin, as discussed by Harriet Boyd-Bennett. Musicologists habitually trace the lineage of songs to understand their transformations in time and place. Andrea Bohlman follows the vicissitudes of ‘Only in Lwów’, a hit song taken from a Polish movie in 1939, with lyrics by Emanuel Szlechter and music by Henryk Wars (later, as Henry Vars, the composer of the theme tune to the American TV show *Flipper*). A fragment of this waltzing paean to the charms of Lviv, as sung by a prisoner of war in Prenzlau, was pored over by Berlin-based linguists studying urban Polish dialects: war created a need for foreign-language speakers, including those subjugated by colonialism and wartime expansion.

Shortwave became an important tool of colonising powers. On 9 May 1938, the second anniversary of the occupation of Addis Ababa and its establishment as the capital city of the Italian empire, the city’s radio station began to supplement transmissions from Rome with its own broadcasts, in all the languages spoken by the native and occupying populations. European classical and military music featured heavily. The mixed programming itself was not unique but, as Danielle Simon notes, in Addis Ababa the radio had a public dimension, as loudspeakers, placed in the main squares, broadcast throughout the city. Speech became unintelligible, but the local soundscape was overwhelmed by Italian music and culture.

The noisiness of the modern city had perturbed George Bernard Shaw at the end of the 19th century. He was less concerned by the mass choirs at Crystal Palace than by the lone musician, hammering away at the iron-framed piano, ‘an almost impossible neighbour’, and recommended as a more appropriate domestic instrument the piano’s smaller, softer predecessor, the clavichord. He regularly attended musical gatherings at the home of the instrument maker Arnold Dolmetsch. In 1917, after living in Massachusetts and Hampstead, the Dolmetsch family relocated to Haslemere in Surrey (not far from Shaw’s home in Hindhead), where they established a chamber music festival that initiated the early music movement in England. Yet, as Jonathan Hicks points out, Dolmetsch, despite his delight that the cheap seats at his concerts were filled with ‘shopkeepers and small local people’, was interested only in the right kind of suburban culture – meaning that which abided by his house rules.

The early music movement has long been recognised as a modernist project: a reflection of present taste more than an authentic recreation of the past. Dolmetsch shared with Schoenberg a determination to keep their musical adventures relatively private, so that they were sheltered from outside currents. Their protectionist attitude towards old and new music could be blamed for some of the challenges faced by the music industry and educational institutions today, as they attempt to engage with both specialist and general audiences.
