# 正文

When​ the former Conservative Party treasurer Alistair McAlpine died in 2014, he was hailed as the most successful fundraiser in modern British political history. His approach was simple: ‘I used to lurk. I lurked all over London where rich people went.’ McAlpine’s courting of prospective Tory donors typically involved ‘a long lunch during which his victim was flattered by being told how highly rated they were by the prime minister before being “stung” for large amounts of cash’, the *Telegraph* wrote. The technique clearly worked: before McAlpine became party treasurer in 1975, the Tories received about £1.5 million a year in donations. By the time he left a decade and a half later, he had raised an estimated £100 million and helped to deliver three consecutive election victories for Margaret Thatcher.

McAlpine operated at a time when there were almost no rules over the disclosure of political donations. But the stories of McAlpine returning to Tory central office with bags of cash and cheques from traders were so well known that he called his autobiography *Once a Jolly Bagman*. The money didn’t just come from City boys and captains of industry, but from ‘Greek shipping magnates, Hong Kong millionaires and Americans’. Mohamed Al-Fayed was coaxed into giving a quarter of a million pounds for the 1987 general election campaign. The Polly Peck fraudster Asil Nadir chipped in with £440,000, which even McAlpine later admitted was ‘tainted money’ – but by then it had long been spent. When McAlpine discovered a black hole in the Tories’ books ‘the solution was easy: we gave up publishing accounts.’

That wouldn’t be allowed today. Political parties have to file accounts and donations or loans of more than £11,180 must be disclosed to the Electoral Commission, founded in 2001. Funding from foreign donors is prohibited. But the fundamentals of political finance have changed little since McAlpine’s day. There still isn’t a cap on donations. Parties can spend unlimited amounts outside of general election campaigns. The wooing of prospective donors still takes place over long lunches in London clubs and restaurants, as shown in Channel 4 News’s recent undercover investigation into Reform UK. The grainy lapel camera footage and mumbled audio is familiar from previous stings: the former Labour minister Stephen Byers describing himself in 2010 as a ‘cab for hire’; a Liberal Democrat fundraiser telling a journalist posing as an Indian businessman that there were ways to give money ahead of the 2015 general election that would remain ‘utterly private’; the former Tory treasurer Peter Cruddas telling undercover reporters from the *Sunday Times* in 2012 that a donation of £200,000 could secure what he called ‘premier league’ access to David Cameron and George Osborne.

But the Channel 4 investigation, carried out by journalists from the not-for-profit outfit Verbatim, felt more significant. Here was the leader of a major party, Nigel Farage, declaring ‘It worked, boom!’ when told that a foreign donor had paid £30,000 for Reform-friendly polling that was splashed across the *Telegraph* and the *Times* without attribution. The footage shows Farage’s long-time consigliere, Dan Jukes, standing outside a Westminster pub and blithely explaining how impermissible donations could be funnelled to the party; Reform’s new head of policy, James Orr, an associate professor of philosophy of religion at Cambridge, suggesting that for a hefty donation a fake donor, who was posing as a MAGA-supporting Wyoming billionaire, could meet Farage. For all the bluff and bravado of Orr’s performance, he also offered unguarded truths. The Electoral Commission was ‘completely useless’, ‘so dumb and ineffective’: ‘The reality is we could easily get away with it.’ One comment was particularly striking: ‘We just don’t have British resident donors or British entities that would support us,’ Orr told the undercover reporter. ‘That’s the grim reality.’

Reform has tried hard to woo donors in the UK. In January 2025 it hosted a fundraiser at Oswald’s, a private members’ club in Mayfair, where guests were treated to jeroboams of white burgundy and Tempo d’Angelus. Farage even sang ‘YMCA’. But Reform has had more success with a different constituency: more than half of the donations to the party, according to the *New York Times*, have come from people with homes in low-tax countries or with offshore business interests. The party’s reliance on the very wealthy reflects a wider trend in British politics. A decade ago, donations of a million pounds or more represented 1 per cent of party funding. Now they account for more than a third. But even by these standards Reform’s reliance on large donors is unusual. Between 2019, when it was set up as the Brexit Party, and the end of last year, three-quarters of the party’s funding came from just three people: the former party leader and current MP Richard Tice; the former Conservative donor Jeremy Hosking; and by far its biggest backer, the cryptocurrency tycoon Christopher Harborne, who is based in Thailand. Harborne’s money has helped Reform to become a political force, but it has also brought unwelcome scrutiny. In April the *Guardian* revealed that shortly before the 2024 general election he had given Farage £5 million, which was never declared. (The gift is being investigated by Parliament’s standards commissioner.)

Reform’s funding has also attracted the attention of the police. By the time the Channel 4 documentary aired last month, the Met had already launched a criminal investigation into three aspects of Reform’s finances: £1 million transferred by Fiona Cottrell, mother of ‘posh’ George, a Farage aide convicted of wire fraud in the US a decade ago, to a company controlled by Tice weeks before the general election; a separate £500,000 in donations to Reform from Fiona Cottrell; and £37,500 that an American businessman called Gary Klopfenstein – who has also been convicted of wire fraud – gave to Reform’s Treasury spokesman, Robert Jenrick, during his 2024 campaign for the Tory leadership. Days after the programme aired, the Met expanded its investigation to look at foreign funding; Orr and Jukes were suspended by Reform (Farage described them as ‘contractors’); and Cambridge University opened an investigation into Orr.

Then, barely 48 hours after the Met’s statement, it was announced that the Hong Kong-based crypto billionaire Ben Delo was giving Reform £36 million – the largest single donation ever made to a British political party. (The previous record was £10 million left to the Conservatives by John Sainsbury in 2022.) Delo, who donated £8 million to Reform earlier this year, wrote in the *Telegraph* that ‘it is the duty of people with broad shoulders and deep pockets to step up and help Reform to win and govern.’ A day later the *Telegraph –* again – announced that Harborne was matching Delo’s donation. The combined £72 million exceeded the amount raised by every British political party put together in 2025. Gabriel Pogrund, editor of the *Sunday Times* ‘Insight’ team, pointed out that the news of the donations only emerged after his paper had gone to Reform with the story and asked for comment. When the *Guardian* approached Farage about the £5 million donation from Harborne, he pleaded for more time to respond and then planted the story in the *Telegraph*.

Reform’s new-found wealth sparked panic inside Labour. ‘This isn’t just the scale of the money, it’s the character of the individuals giving it,’ the Labour MP Phil Brickell said. ‘It’s about where this money comes from.’ Harborne is one of the biggest investors in Tether, a stablecoin that has been accused of facilitating money laundering and sanctions evasion. Although he has lived in Thailand for decades – where he goes by the name Chakrit, meaning ‘he who is awake’ – Harborne has now given more than £60 million to British political parties (he used to donate smaller amounts of money to the Tories, especially under Boris Johnson). Delo was one of the founders of the crypto exchange BitMEX, and in 2022 was convicted in the US for failing to implement adequate anti-money laundering safeguards at the company – prosecutors described him as the ‘leader of a criminal enterprise’ – before being pardoned by Donald Trump last year.

Reform has often been accused of lacking policies, but when it comes to crypto the party has a number of concrete proposals. Farage has said that in his first 24 hours as prime minister he would order the Treasury to abandon work on a state-backed digital pound, which could compete against the barely regulated cryptocurrencies. Reform wants to cut the capital gains tax on crypto profits to 10 per cent and to ‘create and manage a Bitcoin Reserve Fund as part of the UK’s official reserves’. The party even published a prototype Crypto Finance Bill, although it now seems to have been taken offline.

The crypto industry has already seen that big donations can buy political influence in the US. In 2024 Sherrod Brown, the Democratic chair of the Senate Banking Committee and a vocal cryptocurrency sceptic, failed to be re-elected in Ohio after a pro-crypto super PAC spent $40 million supporting his Republican challenger. Trump himself has made hundreds of millions of dollars from meme coins, while his MAGA Inc super PAC has already received more than $56 million from crypto donors in the 2026 election cycle. Susan Hawley, executive director of Spotlight on Corruption, warns that Britain is a soft target. ‘We are looking at the Americanisation of British politics in front of our eyes,’ she told me. ‘We are seeing bigger and bigger amounts of money, from smaller and smaller numbers of people, often with very specific interests.’ In recent general elections, corporate donations increasingly outnumber union contributions.

Labour’s 2024 manifesto pledged to ‘protect democracy by strengthening the rules around donations to political parties’. But having experienced a recent scandal itself around undeclared gifts, and having accepted tens of millions of pounds from hedge fund managers and financiers, it’s hard for the party to make the case that politics shouldn’t be bankrolled by crypto billionaires. The much vaunted Representation of the People Bill, which is currently going through Parliament, should have been an opportunity for root and branch reform of Britain’s outdated electoral laws, but so far its provisions on political finance are timid.

After Nathan Gill, Reform’s former leader in Wales, was sentenced last year to a decade in prison for taking bribes from pro-Russian politicians in Ukraine, the former senior civil servant Philip Rycroft was commissioned by the government to investigate foreign financial influence and interference in British politics. Rycroft quickly realised that the entire system of political donations needed to be examined. ‘I thought it was worth looking at capping donations across the piece and looking at spending limits as well,’ Rycroft told me recently. ‘Clearly the government didn’t take the hint.’ Labour did act, however, on Rycroft’s proposal for a £100,000 annual ceiling on donations from British citizens living abroad. Although the legislation has yet to pass, ministers maintain that the cap – which is clearly aimed at Reform – has applied since the policy was announced in March. But Farage insists that the recent donations from Delo and Harborne were ‘100 per cent compliant with the law today’. Any legal case would probably drag on, by which time much of the money could be gone: Farage has said that it will pay for four hundred campaign managers in target seats, and Reform is already advertising dozens of jobs.

In fixating on the threat of ‘foreign’ money, Labour has implicitly accepted that plutocrats can buy politics, so long as they are on the electoral roll. Some Labour figures see the £72 million donated to Reform as a signal to ramp up their own fund-raising operation. Donors in the party’s Rose Network were recently invited to a private drinks reception at Number Ten. I know of at least one major Labour donor who is discussing a multi-million-pound donation with the party. As if to illustrate the limits of its political imagination, shortly after news of Delo and Harborne’s donations broke, Labour sent out an email asking rank and file supporters to ‘please donate £3, £5, or whatever you can afford.’

Andy Burnham, who as recently as May said he was in favour of a cap on donations, spent his first day in Parliament as prime minister trying to prevent a vote on an amendment to the Representation of the People Bill, proposed by the Labour MP Stella Creasy, that would have introduced a £100,000 a year limit on all donations. Creasy withdrew after the government said it would appoint a ‘task and finish force’ to examine issues around campaign finance. Meanwhile, Farage and his outriders in the media have spent the last few weeks arguing that crypto funding for Reform is no different from the unions supporting Labour. (His ally Arron Banks has even suggested that Reform could set up a crypto union to funnel money to the party.) Labour isn’t as reliant on funding from the unions as it once was: their total contribution last year was £5.5 million, barely a twentieth of the party’s annual budget. But the unions have done their best to block funding reform, and a number of backbenchers removed their name from Creasy’s amendment following warnings that support could cost them union backing.

Some recent developments might change things. A day before the announcement of Delo’s donation, the general secretary of the Trades Union Congress, Paul Nowak, warned that a cap would be a ‘step backwards’. By the following week, he was using his speech at the TUC Congress to denounce ‘out-of-control political spending, funded by stratospheric sums of crypto and corporate cash’. Unite’s Sharon Graham has come out in favour of a cap on individual donors. But other trade unionists are likely to continue to resist reforms to campaign finance, sensing, not entirely wrongly, that any changes could limit their influence on Labour.

We have been here before. After the 2006 ‘cash for honours’ scandal, Tony Blair asked the former civil servant Hayden Phillips to lead a review of party funding. Phillips’s recommendation of a £50,000 cap on donations from individuals and organisations won cross-party support, but foundered on the question of union money: Labour didn’t want affiliation fees to the party to be included in the cap. In 2011 the Committee on Standards in Public Life recommended a £10,000 cap. Under the proposals, the cap would have applied to each individual affiliation fee, provided members ‘opted in’. In practice, this would have preserved a chunk of Labour’s union funding but reduced the power of union leaders to dictate how that funding was used: the fees would no longer have been paid by the unions on behalf of their members. Again, Labour baulked. The report was quietly shelved, largely because it had also called for the state funding of parties.

The irony is that by introducing the £100,000 cap on British citizens overseas, Labour may have imperilled its own funding from the unions. ‘Any time a right-leaning government comes into power they will legislate to bring unions into the £100,000,’ Sam Power, an expert on political finance at Bristol University, told me. Power says that if the government only caps donations from those based overseas, ‘it will have wasted an astonishing amount of political capital on not achieving very much. There are plenty of rich individuals in the UK.’

In July the government asked the Electoral Commission to review campaign spending. This is overdue: such spending is only capped in the year before a general election, and the limit of more than £34 million is too high (the Tories raised it from £19.5 million in 2023). But the commission has been given until next July to make its recommendations on spending limits, although it’s possible that a general election will already have been called by then, and it will not address the broader question of the way British politics is funded.

British politics has long been complacent about the power of big money. The relatively low price of access to politicians here – admission to the Leader’s Group of top Tory donors costs just £50,000 – has often been interpreted, wrongly, as a sign of probity. The current Representation of the People Bill reflects this view. The legislation contains plenty of welcome tweaks. Opaque ‘unincorporated associations’ that have been used to funnel dark money into political campaigns will be required to register with the Electoral Commission and carry out permissibility checks on political gifts. Companies will be barred from donating more than they have made in profits. But the bill leaves untouched other ways to buy political influence, many of them unregulated and fast-growing. Rupert Lowe, who set up the far-right party Restore Britain after falling out with Farage, earns about as much from posting about mass deportations and rape gangs on Elon Musk’s X as he does from being an MP. Lowe claims to have turned down money from Musk, who has called Restore Britain the ‘last and only hope’ for the UK. The party has been given free office space in the Sanctuary, the Westminster club funded by Delo. It had more than £2.5 million in the bank when it was officially founded in February, but thanks to a loophole that allows new parties to avoid declaring the source of their funds on formation, it has never said where the money came from.

That trick could be repeated. Lowe has set up an organisation called Great Yarmouth First in his constituency. Reform has a number of ‘affiliated’ micro-parties that could be used to raise and spend money separately from the main party. Think tanks have long been used as a way to fund politically influential organisations anonymously. The connections between Westminster think tanks and political parties run deep, especially on the right: trustees and executives of the Institute for Economic Affairs have given more than £35 million to the Conservatives, and the Centre for a Better Britain, which Orr once chaired and which shares offices with Reform, has proposed raising £25 million to develop a policy platform for the party. Paul Marshall and Christopher Chandler seem happy enough to have lost £130 million in five years through backing Reform’s in-house TV channel, GB News.

Money isn’t everything. Kamala Harris raised almost half a billion dollars more than Trump in 2024. But money matters in politics, especially if you have a lot of it. Alistair McAlpine understood this all too well. ‘If you’ve got £1 million or £2 million to spend on political advertising, forget it,’ he told a journalist three decades ago. ‘If you’ve got £8 million, you can make people believe anything you want.’
