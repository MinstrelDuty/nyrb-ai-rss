# 正文

One​ of the more recondite claims made by Kanye West during a notorious December 2022 interview with the far-right radio show host and conspiracy theorist Alex Jones was that Hitler ‘invented the very microphone I use as a musician’. Well, no, obviously. But it is true both that Hitler’s reliance on the distinctively tubular Neumann CMV3 when speaking at rallies and other public events earned it the nickname the *Hitlerflasche*, or Hitler Bottle, and that the quality of the CMV3’s audio signal played as significant a part in postwar developments in sound engineering as it had in the dissemination of Nazi propaganda. ‘The microphone,’ Ralph Jones observes at the outset of his short history of the cultural and political uses to which this now ubiquitous device has been put, ‘is an object completely inseparable from power and influence.’ In its unobtrusive way, he adds, it ‘has changed the world around us. But more than that, whether we like it or not, it has changed us too.’

Microphones convert sound waves into an electrical signal. They both receive and transmit. Alexander Graham Bell’s unveiling of the telephone at the Centennial Exhibition in Philadelphia in June 1876 created an absolute need for such a mechanism. The telephone’s primary weakness lay in its transmitter. The problem was not so much how to capture human speech as how to convey it over a significant distance. That was the task the inventors took on. Jones wisely concentrates on the late 1870s, when, for a brief interval, the history of electronics assumed the dimensions of myth as patents sprang fully armed from the ground while lawsuits circled menacingly in the skies above. The heavyweight contestants were Thomas Edison and Emile Berliner. A lengthy legal dispute took place as to who had been the first to establish the basic principles of the carbon microphone that would render Bell’s telephone system viable (that is to say, a paying proposition). The aim was to pass a direct electrical current through granules of carbon packed loosely between a metal plate and some kind of flexible diaphragm. Sound waves strike the diaphragm, causing it to vibrate. The vibrations alternately compress and release the granules. Compression lowers electrical resistance; looseness increases it. The rhythm of compression and release generates an alternating electrical current whose fluctuations perfectly match those of the sound wave.

The squabble over patents pitted the Bell Company, which would hire Berliner, against Western Union Telegraph, which made extensive use of Edison’s inventions. It was always all about the telephone. So we shouldn’t be surprised, perhaps, that Jones opts at once for a deep dive into the murky world of electronic eavesdropping and wiretapping. Eavesdropping is an age-old practice. The term originally meant to stand within the narrow area of ground protected from the rain thrown off by the eaves of a building in such a way as to overhear what is being said inside it. The covert placement of an electronic listening device was subsequently to save a lot of people a lot of furtive hanging around in the vicinity of open doors and windows. Jones has much of interest to say about the ‘new invasion of privacy’ ushered in by the microphone and its associated technologies, starting in 1905 with the rather wonderfully named Detectifone. Trailing wires as they did, however, these devices were scarcely inconspicuous. Happily there was a solution to hand: the transistor, ‘which is, like fire and the wheel’, Jones adds, ‘one of the most important developments in the history of the world’. I began to wonder at this point whether the terms of the argument weren’t beginning to shift a little. Microphone and transistor – a mechanism that controls the flow of electricity through a circuit – are both key elements in a telecommunications system; apart from that, they have nothing in common. It feels as though we are being invited to think about (or wonder at) the microphone’s capacity to transmit a signal to the exclusion of its capacity to receive or absorb a sound wave. After all, wiretapping – like its equally malign successor, phone hacking – involves the interception of a message travelling through someone else’s system. You don’t even have to supply the microphone.

I decided to rewatch Francis Ford Coppola’s classic surveillance movie *The Conversation* (1974). During a virtuoso opening sequence, electronics wizard Harry Caul (Gene Hackman) eavesdrops on a couple who do their best to remain out of earshot by mingling with the crowd in San Francisco’s Union Square. The idea is that the collation of data transmitted from three separate sources in and around the square to the equipment in a nearby van will overcome the inevitable gaps and opacities in the record of the conversation which any one of them on its own is able to provide. Coppola doesn’t so much describe as enact – thrillingly – the hubris that sustains this feat of orchestration. Harry makes it clear that he doesn’t care what the couple in Union Square are talking about. ‘All I want is a nice fat recording.’ In a later scene, a colleague suggests that, since eavesdropping is a natural enough instinct, it might be fun to figure out why these particular people have been targeted. Harry remains adamant: ‘If there’s one sure-fire rule that I’ve learned in this business, it’s that I don’t know anything about human nature, I don’t know anything about curiosity. That’s not part of what I do.’ Nice fat recording accomplished, he attempts to deliver the requisite ‘material’ to his shady employer, which is where the trouble starts. The film’s subject is the unravelling of his feat of orchestration.

Jones’s next step is more orthodox. He supplies an admirably succinct survey of the microphone-enabled entrenchment of mass media in global culture during the middle years of the 20th century: the golden age of radio, the transition from silent to sound film, adventures in the recording studio. The device itself, however, has – with one or two notable exceptions such as a vivid account of the exploits of Geoff Emerick, sound engineer to the Beatles – largely receded from view. Jones is by no means alone among commentators on media history in revealing a preoccupation with what happens after the sound has been absorbed into the device and sent on its way as a signal. There’s not a lot wrong with this approach. Media systems are after all where the money lies, and therefore the power and influence, so we need to know how they work. But it does seem odd that a book in a series called Object Lessons, the aim of which is to elucidate the ‘hidden lives of ordinary things’, should have relatively little to say about the look and feel of the object in question. Of the seven illustrations included in it, only three feature a microphone in active use, and then primarily as set-dressing. Another is of a microphoneless broadcast radio receiver. What’s lost by this meagreness is the inducement an image of an object can provide to investigate the variety of purposes it has come to serve, the needs it has fulfilled.

Jones’s analysis of the microphone’s effect on the politics of the radio age begins with American presidents. Warren G. Harding’s Armistice Day address at Arlington National Cemetery in 1921 ‘was sent’, he notes, ‘via telephone lines to New York and San Francisco, perhaps communicating to a greater audience than any single human being had before’. Harding was soon to be outdone by Calvin Coolidge, whose 1925 inauguration address was the ‘first to be broadcast, reaching around 22.8 million people’. I’m not sure that statistics alone fully account for the success of FDR’s celebrated ‘Fireside Chats’ – the majority of which were recorded in the White House – in creating what Jones terms a ‘sense of intimacy’. In this case, we really do need the visual record. The publicity shots show the president at his desk, a sheet of paper before him, sometimes a glass of water. On the desk stand a gaggle of microphones (CBS, MBS, NBC): bulbous heads waiting to be patted, a radio-age version of the pet dogs integral to the Roosevelt brand (Fala, the most famous of these, had a secretary to himself).

Intimacy, by contrast, was wasted on Hitler. During the 1920s, messianic harangues had secured his reputation as a public speaker. Rooseveltian crooning made him nervous because it neutered his theatricality. The rhetoric really did have to be seen to be believed. When Josef Goebbels took over the newly formed Ministry of Public Enlightenment and Propaganda in March 1933, he decided to keep the Führer out of the studio. Henceforth Hitler’s speeches would take the form of live events broadcast to audiences assembled not in the home, but in halls, factories and stadiums, or wherever zealots could install a bank of loudspeakers. Goebbels used radio to mythologise his leader. As with Roosevelt, although to a contrary effect, the optics mattered. Photographs tend to show Hitler mid-gesticulation, the phalanx of microphones in front of him less a row of heads to be patted than an assault course to be overcome through sheer force of will.

As the century drew to a close, a further advent awaited. ‘In 2026,’ Jones writes, ‘thanks to the microphone (and a range of other technologies, not least of which, of course, is a little thing called the internet), anyone can sing a song and (in theory) reach an audience of several billion people.’ The real ‘little thing’ in all this is the microphone itself, reduced by the systems which feed the current obsession with connectivity to a cog in a vast machine. Miniaturisation hasn’t helped. The game-changer is the so-called lavalier microphone – the name derives from the type of necklace worn by the Duchesse de la Vallière, mistress of Louis XIV – which can be hidden in clothes or on the body, thus permitting relative freedom of movement. Jones describes himself as a journalist and stand-up comedian, and an elegiac note seeps into some compelling pages on the advantages to the performer of an old-style handheld device. He needn’t worry, I think, even if he’s probably wise to ignore one of the internet’s more gnomic feedback loops: podcasts or short-form videos in which a content creator muses on the inventive uses to which a lavalier microphone can be put for the purposes of, well, content creation. Clipped to the rim of a designer mug? Lodged among the tines of a balloon whisk? Like a handheld microphone, all of these devices – and I don’t want to go into the mic cover unforgivably known as a ‘dead cat’ – are needed as much for their aptness as a prop as for their omnidirectional sound pickup pattern. Their objecthood perfectly renders the monetisation of narcissism.

The point about the microphone as a visual aid is a minor one. Behind it lies the more fundamental question incurious Harry Caul fails to ask until it’s too late. What does the thing exist to do? Sometimes the significance of a device becomes fully apparent only when read against the grain of the history of its technical development. According to Jones, the word ‘microphone’ was ‘first used’ in the 1820s by Sir Charles Wheatstone, a prolific experimenter in the fields of optics, acoustics and electrical engineering. In fact, it had been the standard term for a certain kind of auditory device at least since 1684, when Narcissus Marsh, an English clergyman, published his ‘Introductory Essay to the Doctrine of Sounds, Containing Some Proposals for the Improvement of Acousticks’ in the *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society of London*. (Narcissus got lucky: his brothers had to answer to the names Epaphroditus and Onesiphorus.) A gifted musician and a scholar with interests extending from comets to caterpillars, he had served for four years as provost of Trinity College Dublin. In his essay, which has to do with the science of reflected and refracted sound, Marsh spoke of the ear trumpet as a ‘microphone’. The analogy intended, which held good for the next two hundred years, was with a recent invention in a different field. Any self-respecting 18th-century encyclopedia of the arts and sciences contains an entry along the lines of this one from John Harris’s *Lexicon Technicum*: ‘MICROPHONES are Instruments contrived to magnifie small Sounds, as *Microscopes* do small Objects.’

Wheatstone, inventor of the concertina, came from a family of musical instrument-makers. The tuning fork featured heavily in some of the early acoustic experiments he undertook. His ‘microphone’ consisted of two flat pieces of metal riveted to rods which joined together at a single point. Held firmly to the ears by a ribbon, the device amplified the sounds made by the vibration of a violin or guitar, or, more explosively, a vessel containing liquid on the boil. Wheatstone was by no means uninterested in the transmission of sound at a distance. But in this case the closest analogy, of which he is unlikely to have been aware, was with the stethoscope, first described by the French physician (and flautist) René Laënnec in *De l’auscultation médiate* (1819). Auscultation is the action of intent listening, or hearkening. Harry Caul doesn’t begin to hearken to the recording he has made of the conversation in Union Square until his curiosity has been aroused by the fate of a person who wasn’t even there. Replaying it in his mind, he realises that he had not at first so much misheard (the equipment is not at fault) as misunderstood the critical exchange within it. He hadn’t wanted to know.

That curiosity has its costs is a thought entertained by George Eliot at a defining moment in *Middlemarch*. Some disappointments are so unexceptional, Eliot observes, that it would on the whole be better not to make too much of them. ‘If we had a keen vision and feeling of all ordinary human life, it would be like hearing the grass grow and the squirrel’s heart beat, and we should die of that roar which lies on the other side of silence.’ Her contemporaries proved to be less squeamish.

There was​ a third contender in the microphone stakes, nothing like as well known as Edison and Berliner, but every bit as ingenious. When he was young, David Edward Hughes and his two brothers performed regularly in music halls – on one auspicious occasion before Queen Victoria herself – as a trio known as the Child Prodigies (his instrument was the harp). In 1840 the family moved to America. By 1850 Hughes was teaching music at the Roseland Academy in Bardstown, Kentucky. But he had seen the Morse telegraph in action, and it struck him at once that the system would work a whole lot better if messages could be typed in on a keyboard at one end and printed out at the other. The telegraph instrument he subsequently designed made his fortune. In the autumn of 1877, after completing an ambitious European sales drive, he settled in London. There he planned to pursue further independent research in electrical engineering.

The microphone Hughes first demonstrated in public at the Royal Society and the Society of Telegraph Engineers in May 1878 had no diaphragm and did not rely exclusively on carbon. More important were the differences in purpose: Hughes’s device had a far broader range of applications than the telephone as an amplifier of sound. Yes, he told the Royal Society, it would indeed serve to transmit ‘articulate speech’. More exciting still, however, was its capacity to ‘render audible sounds which have hitherto been inaudible’. Introducing him to the Telegraph Engineers, his friend and de facto publicist William Preece told the audience to expect the unveiling of an instrument that acted ‘towards the ear’ in the same way as a microscope acted ‘towards the eye’. There was a further striking difference. Hughes did not seek a patent. Like some paragon of the early internet era, he wanted his invention to be available for all to use. He certainly made his mark on the popular imagination. The most vivid of the experiments he conducted in public was a demonstration of the microphone’s capacity to pick up the sound of a fly moving across a wooden board. Later that year, W.S. Gilbert began work on the libretto of *The Pirates of Penzance*. ‘No sound at all,’ the pirates sing as they steal upon their prey with cat-like tread: ‘We never speak a word,/A fly’s foot-fall would be distinctly heard.’

Telephones may have been the coming thing, but the idea of a machine that could capture the roar on the other side of silence continued to exert a considerable fascination. One of the tall tales recounted in Mark Twain’s *A Tramp Abroad* (1880), the burlesque journal of a European walking tour, concerns the noise – heard initially as the murmur of a distant storm, then as the grinding of machinery, finally as a troop of soldiers on the march – which interrupts a fitful doze in a hotel room. Fearing he would never get to sleep, Twain claims to have ‘close-reefed’ his ears by bending the flaps down over them in several folds: ‘But it did no good … the faculty was so sharpened by nervous excitement that it was become a microphone and could hear through the overlays without trouble.’

Eliot had been even more prompt than Twain in responding to news of the recent invention. In her last book, *Impressions of Theophrastus Such* (1879), the narrator, a middle-aged London bachelor of no identifiable profession, spends a good deal of time arguing with his friend Trost, who ‘always tries to keep up my spirits under the sight of the extremely unpleasant and disfiguring work by which many of our fellow-creatures have to get their bread, with the assurance that “all this will soon be done by machinery.”’ Theophrastus is not so sure. He feels little enthusiasm for technological advances such as ‘a microphone which detects the cadence of the fly’s foot on the ceiling, and may be expected presently to discriminate the noises of our various follies as they soliloquise or converse in our brains’. But there’s a faint flicker of admiration in his acknowledgment of the machine’s ability to detect ‘cadence’. Eliot, like Marsh, like Wheatstone, like Laënnec, like Hughes, was a gifted musician. She had learned the habit of hearkening.

The telephone was by no means the only medium whose further development had come to depend less on extending the scope or reach of the audible than on clarity of transmission. In Hollywood, wide-ranging technical advances during the 1930s secured the ascendancy of intelligibility over naturalness as the primary criterion for the reproduction of sound in cinema. But that was Hollywood. In Soviet Russia in the late 1920s avant-garde filmmakers came to think of sound less as the fulfilment of a visual image than as a raw material in its own right. Dziga Vertov chose to compare his seminal ‘Kino-Eye’ documentary technique to the microscope; but he had trained as a musician at the Białystok Conservatory before enrolling in 1916 at the Psychoneurological Institute in Petrograd. Keen to extend the scope of the audible, he set up a Laboratory of Hearing. He later recalled a variety of unsuccessful attempts to write a poem that would ‘draw up the sounds of a sawmill’. Futurism was all the rage and Luigi Russolo’s *The Art of Noises* (1913) made a big impression on him. ‘Let us cross a large modern capital,’ Russolo exhorted, ‘with our ears more sensitive than our eyes.’ Sounds worth hearkening to included the eddying of water or gas in metal pipes and the flapping of flags and awnings. Vertov’s own art of noises finally found full expression in *Enthusiasm: Symphony of the Donbas* (1931), propaganda for the implementation of the first Five-Year Plan (1928-32), shot for the most part among the coal mines and iron foundries of the Donbas. In March 1930, Vertov had taken a crash course at the laboratory of the inventor Alexander Shorin, a key figure in Soviet sound recording, who designed special portable equipment for him to use. On location, he recorded image and sound both separately and simultaneously using different devices. The most moving passages in the resultant ‘symphony’ revive his youthful fascination with the sawmill.

Russolo’s open-eared stroll down a city street subsequently became an important point of reference for the Canadian composer and campaigner R. Murray Schafer, who in the late 1960s founded the ambitious and enduringly influential World Soundscape Project at Simon Fraser University in British Columbia. To Schafer, however, the urban-industrial eddyings and flappings which had so delighted Russolo were mere ‘noise pollution’: an incessant, all-enveloping – ‘everything is closemiked’ – barrage. His familiarity with the 19th-century novel convinced him that people had once known how to distinguish meaningful sound from hubbub. ‘To dwellers in a wood,’ Thomas Hardy’s *Under the Greenwood Tree* begins, ‘almost every species of tree has its voice as well as its feature.’ Then there’s the stalwart Gabriel Oak in *Far from the Madding Crowd*, roused one morning from his bed by a sharp change in the ‘note’ – usually an ‘idle twinkle’ as anonymous as a clock ticking – of a ‘sheep-bell’ in a field up the hill. Schafer understood that nostalgia would constitute as futile a response to noise pollution as attempts at abatement. He succeeded in establishing instead a ‘*positive* study programme’ which by the end of the 1970s had taken institutional shape as the new discipline of acoustic ecology. The project’s researchers took their microphones out among the madding crowd in towns and cities, where they went to great lengths to record and analyse those ‘sound events’ and ‘sound objects’ distinguished either by their ‘individuality’ (church bell) or by their ‘domination’ (police siren). Such researches required the form of ‘clean hearing’ Schafer thought of as ‘clairaudience’ (like ‘microphone’, an etymological graft of sound onto sight). I’m not sure if he was aware of the term’s mid-19th-century use to describe the sort of trance liable to see you ‘intromitted’, as one proselytiser put it, ‘into the spiritual world’. But he certainly studied the techniques of the mystics (Eastern and Western, ancient and modern). Contemplation he regarded, if not as a prerequisite for clairaudience, then at the very least as a ‘habit and skill’ worth acquiring.

The​ current crop of contemplators is as likely to be intromitted into an institute of marine biology as it is into the spiritual world. These days you can hardly move in the more remote areas of the planet for the scrum of sound artists engaged in attaching microphones to coral reefs or the underside of ice floes. Jeff Rice even managed to drop one into the root system of the gigantic quaking aspen (*Populus tremuloides*) known as ‘Pando’ in the Fishlake National Forest in Sevier County, Utah, a colony of 47,000 stems which constitutes one of the world’s oldest and largest living organisms. Rice was particularly struck by the eerie low rumble recorded during a thunderstorm: the sound, he thought, of the vibration caused by the violent shaking of millions of leaves in the canopy above. Sound events, it goes without saying, filter up from below as well as down from above. Few contemporary practitioners have done more for the art of underwater listening than Jana Winderen, whose *Soundscape of the Anthropocene Ocean* (2021) – like much of her recent work a hypnotic medley of clicks, crackles, bleeps, yelps, chunterings and exhalations punctuated by pulses of sombre mechanical urgency – was composed to accompany the publication in *Science* of an article of that title by a team of climate scientists. The Thames provided the soundscape for a 2024 installation in the Natural History Museum’s Jerwood Gallery. ‘When I lower the hydrophones into the river,’ Winderen explained, ‘another soundworld appears: stridulating underwater insects, ticking of plant photosynthesis, grunts from fish and sounds from mammals, including us.’ Stridulation is the creaking noise made when parts of the body are rubbed together. Not quite the fly’s footfall, perhaps. But pretty close.

In the final scene of *The Conversation*, Harry Caul, believing himself subject in turn to round-the-clock surveillance, dismantles his apartment in search of the bug he’s convinced someone has planted there. The ferocity of the assault suggests that he would much rather find one than not, because evidence of the superior skill of an enemy would at least amount to the ultimate vindication of the efficacy of the systems he has devoted a whole career to designing. The failure of his search forces him to acknowledge that their functioning or not won’t in itself make a great deal of difference. The blame for what has happened lies squarely – as it was still possible to believe in 1974 – in human nature. But then so do the consolations. As the credits roll, Harry sits slumped on a chair amid the debris, the latest in a long line of musical sound engineers, playing the saxophone.
