# 正文

For those​ who work in the field of modern German history, ‘Why Germany?’ is the oldest and most familiar question. Why did the nation of Goethe, Beethoven and Kant bring on itself and the world the catastrophe of Nazism? Packed into the perplexity is the idea of an unbridgeable binary opposition: on one side, Schubert’s *Winterreise*; on the other, the extermination facility at Treblinka. The question is so compelling and has been asked so often that it is easy to lose sight of its unusualness. Confronted with the murderous energy of Stalinism, we don’t ask why the nation of Turgenev and Akhmatova also produced the Holodomor and the Great Terror. We don’t ask why the stupendous bloodletting of the Taiping Rebellion took place in the land that had known Du Fu and Li Bai. And we are unperplexed by the fact that the France of Voltaire and Diderot also administered the sugar plantations of Saint-Domingue, one of the deadliest systems of labour exploitation known to history.

The charisma of the question ‘Why Germany?’ is rooted in two things. The first is the still bewildering intensity and malevolence of the German extermination of European Jewry. The second is our investment in a specific image of Germany as a country of unworldly poets and thinkers: it is the Germany of Schubert and Kant that gives us pause, not the Germany of Lothar von Trotha, genocidal commander of the 1906 campaign against the Herero and Nama of German South-West Africa. Underpinning the supposed paradox is the presumption that people who are cultured and sophisticated will somehow be forearmed against extremism, hatred and murder.

Katja Hoyer opens her book on the city of Weimar in the interwar years with exactly this quandary. ‘The question of how and why a nation that prided itself on its culture and civility enabled the catastrophe of Nazism haunts us to this day, because we fear a repeat.’ The value in reopening this question at a ‘granular level’ lies, she proposes, in the promise that if we can understand ‘the mistakes made by people in the past’, we will be able to avoid making them again. The task is not to ‘condemn’ but to ‘explain’, for ‘in the latter lies the key to understanding history and ultimately to drawing meaningful conclusions from it.’

Hoyer proposes that Weimar, which was the capital of the federal state of Thuringia, is a good vantage point from which to observe and interpret the German path out of liberal democracy into a racist, ultraviolent dictatorship. In 1919, the German national assembly moved there to escape the turbulence in Berlin and to draw up a progressive constitution, establishing Weimar as the eponym of the new republic. Adolf Hitler was fond of the town, and visited it more than forty times. It was in Weimar that he regrouped after the failure of the Munich putsch and his imprisonment in Landsberg. The first official, all-German Nazi Party rally was held there in 1926. And it was in Weimar and Thuringia that the Nazis celebrated early electoral successes. In 1930, the city became the seat of the first state government to include a Nazi minister. Most important, Weimar captures the contradictions that seem to make the question ‘Why Germany?’ so compelling. It was the laboratory in which the men and women of the Bauhaus experimented with bold new forms of expression (until they were forced out by the cultural establishment), but it also became a stage for early rehearsals of Nazi governance. It was (and is) the home of the Goethe House, a place whose dreamlike sequences of rooms and exhibits recall an era of phenomenal cultural fruitfulness. But it also became, in 1937, the site of the Buchenwald concentration camp, constructed on the Ettersberg, the prominent hill north of the city.

My doctoral supervisor, Jonathan Steinberg, used to observe that history was different from the natural sciences because ‘our subjects speak back to us in a way that molecules and galaxies do not.’ Hoyer exploits this loquacity to good effect. The substantive chapters of the book offer a beguiling chronicle woven together from the diaries and recollections of an assortment of people who lived in the city during the interwar years: the stationer Carl Weirich, the apprentice book printer Hanns Wagner, the schoolgirl Jutta Hecker, the hotel owners Rosa and Arthur Schmidt, the social democrat Kurt Nehrling, the gay socialite and diarist Harry Kessler, the conservative housewife and mother Käte Lehmann, the ardent young Nazi Baldur von Schirach, the Jewish shoe-shop owner Ludwig Leopold, the grande dame Elisabeth Förster-Nietzsche, sister of the philosopher and self-appointed custodian of his legacy, and many more.

Hoyer continuously refreshes the perspective, moving from one pair of eyes to another. In the opening chapter on the First World War, her surrogates are scattered to diverse vantage points. Hanns Wagner, still a teenager, finds himself in a shallow trench somewhere in Belgium, surrounded by the shattered corpses of his Weimar schoolfriends. Floundering through basic training in the spring of 1915, 29-year-old Carl Weirich faints from exhaustion in front of his commanding officer after a short jog in full kit and is released as unfit from military duty. Jutta Hecker visits the Weimar military hospital with her fellow pupils to entertain the wounded men with songs and dramatic performances. It is precisely the disparateness of these narrow shards of vision that brings home the vastness of an event whose effects were felt in every domain of life.

In August 1924, a national jamboree for right-wing extremist groups across Germany convened in Weimar for a ‘German Day’ under the captaincy of the former military supremo Erich Ludendorff. Here, Hoyer juxtaposes her testimonies to more jarring effect. We witness the antisemitic firebrand Artur Dinter ranting against the ‘Jewish republic’ and calling for the execution by hanging of its leaders. The 26-year-old drifter Joseph Goebbels is moved to ecstasy by the oratory of the speakers: ‘Weimar is Goethe … And then this festival life in the town today. All of these young people who fight with me. My heart is lifted! Oh, our blessed youth! We enthusiasts, we fanatics! Holy flame ignite!’ But Harry Kessler is less impressed: he notes that the crowds are small and uncommitted, full of ‘children walking along like sheep’ and ‘gentlemen with shouldered umbrellas’, who seem amused at ‘the many comically fat or skinny figures’. The diary of Carl Weirich, who happened on that day to be hiking with relatives, dwells on the excellence of the Thuringian bratwurst they ate together and makes no mention of the German Day. At moments like this, Hoyer can exploit the tension between the retrospective knowledge of her readers and the future-blindness of her witnesses.

After the 1929 elections, when the Nazis secured the third largest share of votes in Thuringia and the second largest in Weimar itself, the book’s political narrative gathers momentum. The Nazis lacked a majority but had the leverage to secure the ministries of the interior and education for Wilhelm Frick. Frick made uninhibited use of his powers, sacking left-leaning and Jewish civil servants and replacing them with people loyal to the Nazis. These measures were flanked by high-volume culture war initiatives, such as the banning of Erich Maria Remarque’s *All Quiet on the Western Front* from use in schoolrooms and the decree ‘Against the Negro Culture for German Folkdom’, the purpose of which was to purge the ‘influences of alien races’ from ‘all cultural areas’. Frick would eventually fall from power, once the parties of the centre recovered their will to act and joined forces against him. But the damage was done: there was no purge of the new appointees, and this brief experiment in Nazi governance revealed how weak the resistance to a more concerted assault on liberal-democratic norms was likely to be.

After the appointment of Hitler as chancellor and the consolidation, first, of a government of ‘national concentration’, and later of an openly Nazi regime, Weimar became a less compelling perch from which to observe what was happening. In the aftermath of the centralisation of powers known as *Gleichschaltung*, Thuringia and the other federal states forfeited their quasi-autonomy. The city ceased to be a driver of events and instead became a setting for national initiatives, such as the construction in 1937 of a large concentration camp. Hoyer reports that members of the city’s Nazi elite succeeded in persuading the authorities not to name the camp after the Ettersberg on the grounds that Goethe had loved to stroll in the forests there. Giving its name to a place of punishment would compromise the region’s precious cultural branding. The choice of the neutral name Buchenwald (beech wood) saved Weimar from the fate that befell the little Bavarian town of Dachau, once famous for its artists’ colony.

It is often observed that the combination of ‘jeopardy and survival’ is a crucial ingredient of good TV writing: audiences are drawn into narratives when characters face ‘dramatic questions’ whose resolution is uncertain. Good screenwriting, the writer and writing coach Tom Vaughan has observed, ‘is filled with dozens of smaller dramatic questions’ that extend from ‘moment to moment’ and ‘scene to scene’. Hoyer exposes hopeful prospects and vulnerabilities before revealing how they play out. Will Carl and Friedel Weirich’s sickly child survive? Will Elisabeth Förster-Nietzsche succeed in protecting her beloved Nietzsche Archive from bankruptcy? As Nazis congregate for meetings and conferences in the Hotel Hohenzollern run by the Schmidts, will anyone find out that Rosa Schmidt has four Jewish grandparents? How will Emmy Sonnemann adjust to the demands of life at the side of her porcine new boyfriend Hermann Goering? Will Kurt Nehrling, a Social Democrat committed to resisting the dictatorship, succeed in avoiding detection and arrest by the Gestapo?

One cares more about some of these predicaments than others. The manipulative and obsessive Förster-Nietzsche and the narcissistic Sonnemann are not people who engage sympathy. By contrast, the scene in which Carl Weirich rushes to the house of his parents-in-law to find that his daughter has died of a brain inflammation is among the book’s most affecting:

> The child had passed away in the arms of her mother and grandmother. Carl and Friedel were inconsolable. Numb with pain, they followed the procession carrying the small wooden box that contained the lifeless body of their only child to Oranienburg’s cemetery. Pastor Thiele, who had married them only four years earlier, spoke at her tiny grave. The next day the parents travelled back to Weimar alone and bereft.

Hoyer draws here on what Elizabeth Bowen described in 1938 as the ‘stupefying universality’ of individual sorrow. But *Weimar* portrays many different kinds of experience. In one scene from 1921, a 19-year-old Marlene Dietrich, who lived briefly in the city, surrenders her virginity to her violin teacher, a 37-year-old married man whose wife was pregnant with his third child: ‘He groaned, heaved, panted,’ she recalled in her diary. ‘Didn’t even take his trousers off. I just lay there on that old settee, the red plush scratching my behind, my skirts over my head.’

Although the book focuses on the twenty years up to 1939, Hoyer folds into her story the murderous escalation of Nazi violence during the Second World War. The introduction opens with Carl Weirich at Buchenwald in the spring of 1945, staring in disbelief at piles of emaciated corpses under the watchful eyes of American soldiers. The epilogue, subtitled ‘Poets and Hangmen’, recounts the execution of Nehrling at Dachau in December 1943. It is here that the story of Arthur and Rosa Schmidt, threaded through every chapter of the book, comes to the darkest possible end. Hoyer cites in full Arthur’s meekly worded letter from January 1945 to the commandant of Auschwitz, in which he requests the return of the ashes and wedding ring of his wife, who had been murdered in the camp two months before. He was unaware that the ring had been melted down and the ashes scattered among the remains of thousands of others.

The quest for an explanation of what the historian Friedrich Meinecke called the ‘German catastrophe’ has been at the heart of historical writing on modern Germany since 1945 and the history of this inquiry is itself of great interest. The influential ‘critical school’ that flourished at the University of Bielefeld from the early 1970s proposed that the uneven development of the German nation-state – the *Sonderweg* or special path – created frictions and incoherences in which demagoguery, xenophobia and elite manipulation flourished. It was fashionable for a time to blame the artisans and shopkeepers of the lower middle classes, whose fear of *déclassement* was thought to have motivated a migration towards the Nazis. Marxists argued that fascism was the reactionary political manifestation of German capitalism in crisis. A massive study of voting behaviour led by Jürgen Falter complicated the picture by showing that the NSDAP polled strongly across all classes in 1930-32 and that the single most important driver was neither class nor social status or regional provenance, but religion, Protestants being more likely to vote for the NSDAP than Catholics, whatever their class. These were arguments that focused on the political choices Germans made between 1930 and 1933.

The​ question, and the tools required to answer it, change if you focus not on the collapse of the Weimar democracy in 1933 – which had parallels in other countries, such as Italy and Spain – but on the surge of violence in 1938-40 and the waves of wholesale extermination in 1941-44, for which there exists no historical parallel. As the American historian Helmut Walser Smith observed, shifting the ‘vanishing point’ of the inquiry in this way focuses our attention on the very numerous perpetrators who oversaw or took part in the persecution of Jews, ran concentration and extermination camps, or manned the *Einsatzgruppen* and police battalions charged with carrying out ‘ethnic tasks’ in Central and Eastern Europe. Works exploring this question have focused on the brutalising effects of the First World War, the formation of specific postwar cadres such as the functionaries of the SS and SD or the commandants and staff of the camps, or the malleability of men keen to demonstrate loyalty and group adherence in an environment from which all ethical constraints have been purged. All of these analyses have stimulated discussion and controversy and this is just a tiny sample of the pertinent arguments. Explaining these things is not easy.

Hoyer’s book is not in dialogue with any of these debates. The literary form she adopts pitches the book towards evocation rather than analysis. More important, her protagonists are for the most part not perpetrators in any significant sense. They function so well as surrogates precisely because most of them are relatable figures, apolitical onlookers or even victims. (An exception is the sometime leader of the Hitler Youth, Baldur von Schirach, who as Gauleiter of Vienna in 1940-45 was responsible for deporting tens of thousands of Viennese Jews to camps in German-occupied Poland, but his crimes fall outside the framework of Hoyer’s narrative.) In one of the most disturbing scenes in the book, a Weimar schoolteacher refuses to begin a German lesson. He stands in front of the children pointing at Joachim Appel, the only Jewish pupil in the room, and announces: ‘I won’t start teaching while that pig Jew is in the class.’ Hoyer evokes the boy’s surprise, humiliation and anger as he flees amid the catcalls and insults of his fellows, but we learn nothing more about the cruelty and abusiveness of the teacher, who was presumably also a Weimar native. The Weimar Nazis who appear at various points in the book remain shadowy figures. Hoyer mentions that the Weimar Gestapo was so overwhelmed with handling political denunciations from the general public that an order was issued forbidding them, but it would be interesting to know more of the content of these complaints and the motivations driving them – Robert Gellately used anonymous denunciations to excellent effect in his classic study from 1990 of the enforcement of Nazi racial policy. His conclusion was that the grip of the Gestapo on German society had less to do with surveillance and terror than with the willingness of ordinary citizens to support the objectives of the regime.

One cumulative effect of this patterning of attention is that the enormities of the Nazi years appear as misfortunes falling like poisonous rain on the city. Commenting on the violence of Kristallnacht in November 1938, Hoyer reports that ‘the reality of what Nazi antisemitism looked like in practice’ now ‘came home’ to the Weimarers; later she observes that ‘Nazi antisemitism took no heed of the fact that Weimar’s Jewish community had been small and highly assimilated.’ Hoyer knows, of course, that the dark forces unleashed by the regime were also present in Weimar itself – the book is quite clear about the episodic centrality of the city to the rise of National Socialism. But the tension between the aspiration to ‘explain’ the journey of a nation into political darkness and the deployment of likeable surrogates who engage the sympathy of readers remains unresolved.

Hoyer refers to the importance of explaining the ‘mistakes’ made by people in the past. ‘Mistake’ is a significant word in this context. Voting for the Nazis was surely a mistake. Just over a third of all German voters – including Carl Weirich, apparently – chose the NSDAP in the summer and autumn of 1933. But denouncing a hidden Jewish family to the authorities, or beating up an elderly Jewish woman, or volunteering to take part in the administration of a camp whose inmates are systematically denigrated, beaten and killed: these are not ‘mistakes’. In July 1947, as the process of denazifying postwar West Germany unfolded, the historian and former Buchenwald inmate Eugen Kogon published his essay ‘The Right to Be Wrong in Politics’ (‘Das Recht auf den politischen Irrtum’). There was a fundamental difference, he proposed, between a mistaken political judgment and culpable negligence or participation in acts of cruelty and criminality. Only the latter implied guilt and were punishable.

Hoyer’s enjoyable chronicle immerses us in a world on the edge of disaster, hinting darkly at resonances with current political conditions in Europe and the United States. In the introduction, she declares that she has refrained from ‘commentary and analysis as a historian’ for fear that this will impede the reader’s ability to ‘observe events, decisions and developments as they unfold’. The announcement sits uneasily with Hoyer’s avowal that historians owe it to the present to ‘explain’ the behaviour of people in the past. And if that is the purpose, then the starting point ought to be those works that have asked and tried to answer the same question. History is more than a subject matter; it is a collective enterprise that drives an advancing frontier of knowledge. A book that aspires to be more than a chronicle of past people and events will benefit from dialogue with the work of others who have deepened our understanding of the questions it claims to answer.
