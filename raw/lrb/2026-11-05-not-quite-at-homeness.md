‘And immediately/Rather than words comes the thought of high windows’. Note the rhetorical sleight of hand: what, if not words, is Larkin coming forth with? But you fall for his gambit because during the preceding lines of ‘High Windows’, speaking voices have dominated. First that of an ageing grouch – a familiar Larkin persona – casting a rueful glance across the street at ‘a couple of kids’ and imagining the carefree sex they are having. (The poem was written in 1967, just as the pill was becoming available.) Then a second observer is introduced: one from an earlier generation, sizing up how the first appeared during his own youth and guessing that he too would be headed down ‘the long slide’ to happiness. Twentieth-century history points that way, each speaker assumes: from repression to liberation.

Then suddenly, the ‘thought’ – a vision, in effect – intervenes. The ‘slide’ reverses upwards: through those high windows, into ‘deep blue air, that shows/Nothing, and is nowhere, and is endless’. Coupling bodies and history itself fall away before the sky’s eternity. The 1960s were noisy with talk about futures, often utopian in complexion. But the future is of its nature a non-thing, ungraspable: that realisation haunts Larkin, an observer born in 1922, even as his attention is caught by the era’s excited hedonism.

A fellow observer was the painter Michael Andrews, six years Larkin’s junior, and also from provincial clerical stock. Andrews was caught up in dreams of parties. In 1962 he pictured a clique of bohos late-night jabbering, juddery forms swaying in a Soho club’s dim tunnel. That six-foot-wide panel, *The Colony Room*, was followed by *The Deer Park*, more expansive and fantastical: washy apparitions of contemporary film stars and schmoozers reeling in a hotel lounge. In *All Night Long* (1964), Andrews stretched the theme across a twelve-foot-wide triptych. Gathered around some swish riviera bar in the steamy small hours, jet-setters and molls, lolling, smooching, skinny-dipping, reach for the high life – or overreach, flailing drunkenly. Wild conviviality, frenetic glamour: later in the decade, Andrews’s bemused fascination with these magnets for desire drew him towards the counterculture, as he assembled studies of hippies with a view to delivering what Christopher Lloyd, in *Michael Andrews: Painter of Masterpieces*, suggests might have been ‘the ultimate party picture’.

Then abruptly, the grinning apparitions vanished. No more socialites in Andrews’s art, no human figures at all: instead, a hot-air balloon glides between neon-lit skyscrapers and over English hills and coasts, in a series of seven big canvases collectively titled *Lights*, works of the turn of the 1970s executed not in fleshly oils but in vaporous spray gun acrylics. In the finale of the series, we look down from the craft at its shadow as it drifts across a sunlit sandy beach facing a northern horizon, whether to land or to head out for the ocean. The inflated globe with its basket of ballast, this minimal signifier that there is a person at hand with a direction and a desire, is no more than a breaker away from the deep blue endlessness.

I compare the shifts in Andrews’s imagery to those in a poem because metaphor was his business. Andrews was not the kind of artist who looks for the sake of looking. His gaze roved, seeking out analogues. Whether he chose to depict partygoers or antipodean mountains or tropical fish or Essex estuary shores, an expressive anxiety was at hand. What, if not sights, was he proposing to the viewer? As with any considerable poet, the force behind his restlessness eludes precise definition, and Lloyd does not presume to pin it down. But he usefully frames him within his times.

Andrews was a beneficiary of the postwar consensus. He arrived at the Slade School of Art in 1949, a gangly, studious and awkward 21-year-old from Norwich with National Service behind him, and was able thereafter to secure a permanent foothold in the London gallery system. The extraordinary 1952 canvas with which he made his mark – *A Man who Suddenly Fell Over*, acquired six years later by the Tate – shows a portly, bald gent taking a tumble in the street, with a loss of dignity that feels at once monumental and emblematic. A later grand project of Andrews’s early thirties, *The Family in the Garden*, is by contrast an affirmative study of bourgeois stability: stretched across nearly ten feet, it portrays dad and mum – Methodists made good – taking afternoon tea in suburban Norwich with Andrews’s brother, sister and grandmother. In the sequence that ran from *The Colony Room* onwards, the readings of society rerouted. He was feeling for the aspirational fantasies of a buoyant era, sensitive to their urgency and their absurdity, not least when he depicted a reception to celebrate a new chancellor at the University of East Anglia in 1966. Those party pictures witnessed the expansion of the notional ‘where it’s at’ from exclusive downtown cenacles to communal deliriums.

Andrews’s studio bookshelf was reshuffled correspondingly. Paris existentialism made way for the critiques of social conformity advanced by Alan Watts and R.D. Laing. From those, he seized on the phrase ‘the skin-encapsulated ego’ as a cue for his balloon series. But with that departure from ground level at the end of the 1960s, his imagery – like that of ‘High Windows’ – shook itself free from any concern with the zeitgeist. In his later years, before his death from cancer in 1995, aged 66, the ambition was undimmed, but the thinking became more or less allegorical.

The self – as Laing and Watts would have it, a deceptive yet hard-to-escape construct – encounters staging posts, most affectingly depicted in a 1979 canvas of Andrews teaching his young daughter to swim. It sets out on pilgrimages: notably to Uluru in the heart of Australia, which Andrews, leaning on his Protestant upbringing, designated a ‘Rock of Ages Cleft for Me’; but also to ancient British landscapes, concluding with the course of the Thames. It brushes as it does so against features of the world that Andrews conceives as archetypal. Insofar as this older Andrews still thinks about social dynamics, he employs animal stand-ins, for instance when he paints a snorkeller’s view of barracuda stalking tuna.

Andrews’s work does not possess a cohesive ‘look’. The facture veers from the vigorously self-correcting hog-brush hatching of the dun-hued *A Man who Suddenly Fell Over*, to passages of loose-armed De Kooning-style gesturing in the 1960s party pictures, to luminous acrylic stains in the virtually line-free fish paintings, resolving, during his last few years, into a dialogue between paint-pouring and descriptive precision. In all this his oeuvre refracts, if not reflects, a sequence of London art-world trends. Fresh-eyed and dextrous when working from life in oils or watercolour, Andrews also from the outset made reference to photographs: his was never a purely drawing-led practice. For these reasons, he did not achieve brand recognition of the kind secured by Francis Bacon and Lucian Freud – two of the drinkers portrayed in *The Colony Room* – with their readily identifiable manners and bodies of imagery. Yet those older friends of his would, I think, have understood my own testimony as a painter in Andrews’s wake: that his work presents as high a challenge as anything in 20th-century British art.

A witness statement, for sure, is not an argument. Quality – what it is that sets a painter’s mark-making on an exalted plane – may always at root remain mysterious. Pushed to explain Andrews’s excellence, Bacon fell back on: ‘It’s just his touch I suppose.’ Is there a way of expanding on that? Lloyd’s researches (those of an admiring acquaintance rather than a confidant) allow us to infer this much: that Andrews reached for imagery that might analogise his own passage through the world, a body of experience in which not-quite-at-homeness and among-but-not-of-others loomed large. That sense of dislocation extended to the sights he chose to depict. There was no determinate way, for him, in which the stuff of the world became the stuff of paint.

At the same time, Andrews practised his art with an unremitting (and quite possibly inherited) probity. Even if the visible exceeded definition, his chosen medium might just allow him to reach out to it locally. Each weight of pigment that landed on the canvas was a fresh foray, an attempt to seal a specific concord with this or with that. The terms of that concord might not, however, apply when other handiwork attended to other facets of experience. Andrews was constantly alert because he was constantly anxious – meaning that although he looked over the world’s shoulder, as it were, with a degree of metaphysical reserve, he interpreted its variety with a uniquely astonished eye – and indeed an inimitable touch.

The force and the paradoxicality of the Andrews project were noted by Jonathan Raban in a 1991 essay published to accompany an exhibition at the Whitechapel Gallery of the Uluru paintings. At the age of 54, Andrews – thinking on his habitual grand scale, but as Lloyd shows, often acting on slender cues and scrabbling for funds – had taken himself off for the first time to the southern hemisphere, to a tourist destination glimpsed in a Sunday supplement ad. A week of tramping, water colouring and photographing the sacred sites of a remote and ancient culture became the departure point for nine expansive canvases stained, airbrushed, imprinted and splotched in radiant acrylics. A head-on view of the looming mountain dominated the show, fourteen feet wide, with the markings that pit its rock face and that offer cues for Aboriginal storytelling transcribed. Reacting to Andrews’s ‘excited discoveries’, Raban remarked that ‘this extraordinary, flesh-toned, speckled thing is evidently possessed of some kind of massive significance; but what it signifies is beyond the reach of the painting. It retains its fundamental alienness and unknowability.’ Having voyaged out to his own world’s end, Andrews had, in Raban’s reading, hit on a means of defining that sphere: ‘a world in which we move uneasily, as trespassers with good intentions, encapsulated in the skin of our own culture’.

The voyage had begun under the direction of William Coldstream, Andrews’s tutor at the Slade. Epistemic diffidence – how can one be sure what’s out there? – was structured into Coldstream’s approach to painting from observation: assume nothing, merely nudge forward, one mark at a time, until interrelations establish themselves. Virtue, according to this schooling, lay exactly in ‘touch’. Whenever Andrews engaged in portrait painting from life, he appears the doctrine’s most dazzling disciple. But he prioritised uncertainties independent of Coldstream’s: those of a dedicated reader pondering his own passage through life and others’ company; those (as Lloyd reports) of an intruder on the scene hitting the bottle to overcome his shyness. These caused him to latch onto Bacon, more as an exemplar of imaginative boldness than as the proponent of any alternative artistic approach. Systems and methodologies were what he intended to escape.

With comparable motives, he sought out a partner from the glamour world he liked to depict. June Keeley, as sassy as he was nervy, was head hostess at Churchill’s, a louche Bond Street nightclub. In the early 1960s, Andrews diverted her from the attentions of a habitué called Tim Willoughby, who died shortly after in a sailing accident, leaving a large estate in Perthshire to his sister, Jane. By that circuitous route, Andrews gained access to the bluest of blue-blood patrons. He and June took to holidaying at Drummond Castle; he made a painting of its formal gardens. This was followed by others exploring the Highlands and the working lives of deer stalkers, plus commissions from the Barons de Rothschild and Thyssen-Bornemisza. A painter with a love of British landscape but a New York School yearning to engulf the viewer in cinema-screen-scale canvases had in his later years acquired a social niche that could just about sustain his ambitions.

Christopher Lloyd, a former Surveyor of the Queen’s Pictures, comes to his subject from that well-mannered sector. With the assent of June Andrews, he has drafted a respectful and reliable overview of her husband’s career. As a scholar of the Renaissance, he has faced, as he admits, the odd hurdle. He laboriously explains – perhaps for his own benefit – that the 1960s were a transformative decade; among the studies of its stoners, he fails (on page 217) to recognise Keith Reid of Procol Harum; but then, Andrews himself failed to recognise the Beatles when he collaged images of four figures dressed in animal suits into his view of the gardens at Drummond. At times Lloyd’s protagonist displays a certain genial comic innocence, reminding me of Paula Rego’s reaction when I quizzed her about her old mate from the Slade. ‘Mike? Pah! He was a *pussycat!*’ (Well, yes – a peaceable painter, inhabiting a peaceful milieu.)

Lloyd has taken his subtitle from another colleague. Lawrence Gowing, wryly defending the slow rate of production for which Andrews was notorious, wrote that ‘he paints hardly anything but masterpieces.’ I’d question whether this assertion of art-historical authority supplies Lloyd with a sensible basis for relating pictures by Andrews to works by Michelangelo and Correggio: Andrews seems to have operated less in the shadow of his dead forebears than most. But then, how should we salute what he delivered? The final canvas he completed, *Thames Painting: The Estuary*, is (unlike almost everything else of his) on just about permanent public view, if you go to the Pallant House Gallery in Chichester. Over seven feet high and six feet wide, it wallops you – a convulsion of close-at-hand riverbank mud succumbing to distant sky-reflecting waters, of warmish silt suspended in turpentine ceding to a chill milky cloudscape with just a glimmer of sun. The slippage into the flux hits a snag where a jetty juts up, surmounted by diminutive rivermen with their fishing lines and rowing boats, but this only emphasises the fact that the rest of the canvas is virtually beyond form, beyond colour – beyond resolution into a definite image, in fact, for crisscrossing dribbles of reflective varnish change its look as you shift your viewpoint. Here is an artist of consummate daring and control painting the end of identity and the end of his art.
