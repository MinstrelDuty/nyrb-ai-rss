# 正文

At the side of the road close to the cemetery in the small town of Predappio in Emilia-Romagna, Valentino brushed his dishevelled grey curls away from his face and wiped clean the white band at the bottom of his black trainers. It was 26 July, the Sunday before Mussolini’s birthday – one of three days in the year when neo-Fascists travel en masse to the place where Il Duce was born and where his remains now lie. Valentino had arrived at 8.30 a.m. sharp to avoid the rush, but some people were already outside the entrance to the family crypt. They were wearing T-shirts with the slogan ‘Continuità Ideale’ and the initials RNCR RSI. ‘Continuità Ideale’ refers to the supposedly unbroken spiritual connection between Mussolini’s ideology and contemporary far-right movements. RNCR RSI – an offshoot of the Raggruppamento Nazionale Combattenti e Reduci – honours those who fought for the Republic of Salò, the German puppet state led by Mussolini which was set up after he was overthrown and Italy surrendered to the Allies.

There had been crazy storms all morning, forked lightning splitting the sky. The previous afternoon, I had been in Bologna, about fifty miles away, eating the ‘anti-Fascist pasta’ served up outside the town hall every 25 July, the anniversary of the day that Alcide Cervi – a partisan and farmer – rustled up macaroni with butter and parmesan to feed everyone in the town of Campegine following Mussolini’s arrest in Rome in 1943. Pasta was one of many things Mussolini disapproved of, believing it made Italians sluggish and exhorting them to eat rice instead. Five months later, all seven of Cervi’s sons were executed by a Fascist firing squad.

In the cemetery at Predappio I walked past a stone eagle and a column taken from the Roman Forum, and down a flight of stairs to the whitewashed vault. This small space contains the tombs of fourteen members of Mussolini’s family, including his wife, Rachele (but not his mistress, Clara Petacci, who was shot alongside him), and four of his five children with Rachele (but not Edda, whose husband, Galeazzo Ciano, was executed on Mussolini’s order after voting to remove him from power). Centre stage, beneath a large marble head, Mussolini’s stone sarcophagus was draped with red and black roses and sheaves of wheat intended to resemble the *fasces* – a bundle of rods around an axe – that symbolised the power to carry out punishment in ancient Rome and from which Fascism takes its name. Set into the walls are relics including an urn containing a piece of Mussolini’s brain.

In front of the sarcophagus, Valentino gave a Roman salute, made the sign of the cross and wrote ‘Ti voglio bene’ (‘I love you’) in the visitors’ book. He dated his entry 26.07.CIV EF (the Fascist calendar used Roman numerals and considered 29 October 1922, the day after the March on Rome, the start of the ‘Era Fascista’, so we’d now be in the year 104). A marshal rebuked me for taking a photograph of the visitors’ book and stepped forward to stop me flicking through it. But I had already seen enough: ‘Iterum rudit leo’ (‘the lion roars again’); ‘Semper fidelis’; ‘Per un mondo più pulito, torna in vita Zio Benito’ (‘for a cleaner world, return to life Uncle Benito’).

Mussolini transformed his birthplace into a monument to his own myth, broadening its narrow main street into a boulevard punctuated with reminders of his supremacy. The Mercato dei Viveri, a semi-circular portico with giant arches, opens in the centre to frame a view of the rough stone house on the hill above, where he was born. In 1938, before a visit from King Victor Emmanuel III, Mussolini ordered the demolition of the monumental staircase linking the two for fear it undermined the story of his rustic upbringing.

The boulevard once known as Viale Benito Mussolini is now named for Giacomo Matteotti, the socialist leader murdered by Blackshirts in 1924. The Caproni Aircraft Factory, which Mussolini brought to Predappio despite the absence of a skilled local workforce, was later used to breed chickens and grow mushrooms; most of it now lies empty. Chisel marks on the post office bear witness to the removal of decorative *fasces*. But on the façade of the church of Sant’Antonio di Padova, the date of its foundation is inscribed as both AD MCMXXXIV and EF XIII (1934). Mussolini’s ego endures. It makes itself felt in the Palazzo Caproni – upmarket flats built to house the aircraft factory’s engineers – whose metal fascias look like the flaps of aeroplane wings; in the great cylindrical towers of the police station; and in the white Roman travertine lintels of the dilapidated Casa del Fascio, the local party headquarters.

For more than seventy years after the war ended, the town elected only left-wing mayors. And yet, ever since 1957, when Rachele reclaimed her husband’s corpse, Predappio has relied on Mussolini for its living. Before that his body had been hidden in a cupboard in a Capuchin monastery, in an attempt to prevent his cult from being rekindled. But the new Christian Democrat prime minister, Adone Zoli, realised he could use the return of the body as a bargaining chip to secure far-right support in parliament. Since then, up to 100,000 people have come every year to visit the crypt and buy tat from the Fascist souvenir shops. The curious arrive all year round; hardliners come on the Sundays before Mussolini’s birthday, 29 July; the day of his death, 28 April; and the anniversary of the March on Rome, 28 October.

At 10.30 a.m. on those days, they walk the mile or so from Piazza Sant’Antonio to the cemetery, often led by the dictator’s great-granddaughters, Orsola and Vittoria. The Sunday in October sometimes attracts a counter demonstration by the National Association of Italian Partisans (ANPI) because 28 October is also the anniversary of the town’s liberation in 1944: the 18th Lwów Rifle Battalion held back to mete out a calendrical comeuppance.

Whatever ambivalence the residents of Predappio feel about their town being synonymous with Mussolini, they seem prepared to thole it. Between 2017 and 2021, when a combination of restoration and family feud meant the crypt was closed (or open only on the three big days), visitor footfall and income plummeted. In 2019, Roberto Canali put the issue at the centre of his mayoral campaign and won. Though he stood as an independent candidate, he is backed by the right-wing coalition that makes up the current Italian government: Giorgia Meloni’s Fratelli d’Italia, Matteo Salvini’s Lega and Forza Italia (founded by Silvio Berlusconi). Canali’s victory gave Predappio its first right-wing administration since the end of the war and the crypt is open all year. A plan by his predecessor, Giorgio Frassineti, to turn the former Casa del Fascio into a museum challenging the neo-Fascist narrative has been downgraded: the building will now be ‘dedicated to the history of the [whole] 20th century’.

In the town centre, the neo-Fascists were stocking up at the souvenir shops. The sight of Nazi armbands and copies of *Mein Kampf* is still shocking, but there was something faintly embarrassing about men covered in Nazi tattoos buying mugs reading: ‘When everything is going badly ALWAYS SMILE; think, you could have been born a communist.’ One man with an ‘88’ tattoo (for ‘Heil Hitler’; H is the eighth letter of the alphabet) pushed in front of me as I waited for a pot of tea, but apologised when admonished by the woman behind the counter.

Piazza Sant’Antonio had filled up with police vans, and armed officers in bulletproof vests were pacing restively. It soon became obvious they were going to outnumber the visitors. Mistaking two men with cameras perched on a stone staircase for journalists, I climbed up for a chat. They turned out to be plain-clothes officers monitoring the crowd of fifty to a hundred people. ‘The numbers are low,’ one of them said. ‘There will be no trouble today.’

Valentino was sitting alone in a nearby park. This was his second visit to Predappio. His first had been in early April, after he turned 75, the age his father had been when he died. His father had served during the war in North Africa, Albania and Greece, and had been taken prisoner by the Germans after Italy’s surrender. He continued to admire Mussolini, and Valentino had come to Predappio to honour his memory. I realised then that the ‘I love you’ in the visitors’ book, with the informal pronoun *ti*, must have been addressed to his father. But what did Valentino think? ‘Mussolini did good things and bad things,’ he said. ‘Italy was weak and he was strong, but we were not prepared for war – that was a mistake.’ His opinions were not so different from those I had heard from dozens of Italians. ‘Mussolini created jobs,’ they claimed. ‘He drained the marshes.’ ‘He brought a humiliated Italy international respect.’ ‘It was only when he allied himself with Hitler that it all started to go wrong.’

As we talked, Orsola and Vittoria decided there weren’t enough people for a march; instead, everyone should make their own way back to the cemetery gates for the speeches and laying of wreaths. Some people drove. I walked. By the time I got there, rows of people were standing behind a ten-metre Italian flag that had been laid on the ground. Some carried RNCR RSI banners with the names of their regions. Facing them on the steps were Orsola and Vittoria, and an old man swamped by his black-feathered Bersaglieri hat. Orsola said her great-grandfather had disliked celebrating his birthday, though he used to receive thousands of cards. There were prayers. Many of the neo-Fascists had brought dogs, which barked during the minute’s silence. The ceremony ended with a roll-call, in which the names of Mussolini and other dead Fascists were answered with a shout of ‘Presente!’ Under the Scelba Law, both the roll-call and the Roman salute, which often accompanies it, are illegal if they are likely to provoke public disorder. Orsola had urged people to place their hands on their hearts instead. Three or four arms shot up anyway.

It would be comforting to imagine that the low number of marchers means the decline of the far right, but the evidence is against this. Meloni has distanced herself from the Mussolini nostalgists, but she began her political career in the youth wing of the Italian Social Movement (MSI), formed after the war by Mussolini’s supporters, and its tricolour flame remains in her party logo. Other groups have accused her of selling out. Of these, Roberto Vannacci’s new party, Futuro Nazionale, poses the greatest threat. Vannacci, a retired army general, MEP and Putin apologist, founded it in February after breaking away from Salvini’s Lega. It’s now polling neck and neck with the Lega and just behind Forza Italia, and might succeed in pushing Meloni further to the right.

The low turnout in Predappio was a consequence in part of the bad weather, and in part of a row that has broken out between Mussolini’s great-granddaughters and Forza Nuova, led by Roberto Fiore. Unlike Futuro Nazionale, Forza Nuova is a fringe movement without any meaningful electoral support (it splintered from an MSI splinter group nearly thirty years ago). Its new local headquarters, festooned with flags and ‘Italy for Italians’ banners, were unmissable a few hundred metres from the cemetery. At last year’s 28 October commemoration, Forza Nuova ignored a police order to march separately in the afternoon and instead gatecrashed the great-granddaughters’ morning ceremony, swelling attendance to close to a thousand. They knew the sisters’ position on the Roman salute but ignored it. Thirty people were caught on camera and identified, but there were no prosecutions. The new party HQ opened at the end of June – Canali said it wasn’t breaking any laws – and then Fiore announced a boycott of the July ceremony.

Italy’s relationship with its Fascist past is explored in Stephen Gundle’s book *Mussolini’s Ghost: The Afterlife of a Dictator* (Oxford, £25). Having effectively switched sides, Italy’s reparations were less severe than Germany’s. It had to pay $360 million and surrender its colonies (Libya and Ethiopia) and the parts of Yugoslavia annexed after the First World War, but there was no Nuremberg-style tribunal. Italians bought into a self-serving narrative: that Mussolini’s regime was, as Gundle puts it, ‘a mild form of dictatorship compared to Nazi Germany, that its colonial wars were motivated by civilising ideals, that it achieved much in the realm of social reform and welfare, that it unified and modernised the country’ and that its people were good-hearted innocents dragged against their will into the war. These delusions were aided by the West’s reluctance to do anything that might push Italy, which had the largest Communist Party in Western Europe, towards the Soviet bloc.

Hitler had no children to act as apologists, but Mussolini’s many descendants have exerted an influence on politics and the public imagination. Though in life he had been embarrassed by his wife’s provincialism and the sometimes wild behaviour of their children – Edda, Vittorio, Bruno, Romano and Anna Maria – after his death Rachele managed to deflect attention from his atrocities by stressing their family life. ‘She became an icon of sorts,’ Gundle writes, ‘a physical reminder of Mussolini, in some respects his feminine continuation.’

The strength of anti-Fascist sentiment immediately after the war should not be underestimated. But from the 1990s onwards a succession of populist politicians – Berlusconi; Umberto Bossi, who founded the Northern League (the precursor to the Lega); Beppe Grillo, who co-founded the Five Star Movement; Salvini and Meloni – have fed off Mussolini’s continuing appeal and the abiding allure of Fascist iconography. In an age of statue-toppling, it is jaw-dropping to see the words ‘Mussolini Dux’ still carved into the 17.5-metre marble obelisk that fronts the sports complex of the Foro Italico in Rome.

A couple of days before I arrived in Predappio, I read that Hitler’s birthplace in Austria had been compulsorily purchased and turned into a police station. On the pavement in front of it is a memorial stone from Mauthausen concentration camp: ‘For peace, freedom and democracy. Never again Fascism. Millions of dead serve as a warning.’ Mussolini’s birthplace is now a museum, while the Villa Carpena, his house 15 km away in Forlì, has been turned into a Casa dei Ricordi or ‘house of memories’. Seized by the state after the war and used to house refugees, it was handed back to Rachele in 1957 and she lived there until her death in 1979. Soon after that, Vittorio, a film producer, and his second wife, Monica Buzzegoli, moved in. Monica remained in the villa for a few years after Vittorio’s death in 1997, then sold it to Domenico Morosini and his wife, Adele Grana, who scoured the country recovering lost documents, uniforms and furniture.

I had been told about the ‘neutral’ guided tours, but nothing could have prepared me for the fever dream that lies behind the pale yellow walls of this Liberty-style mansion. The grounds, overrun by hens, peacocks and doves, contain a junkyard of memorabilia: heads on plinths, stone lions, old cars, a boat swing, a figure of Santa Claus, a statue of Padre Pio, a miniature temple with an eternal flame, given to Mussolini by Emperor Hirohito, signs for streets named after prominent Fascists. In the midst of this runs a path with tricolour memorials on either side. They carry tributes to various militia groups and battles: ‘Alle Brigate Nere’ (‘To the Black Brigades’); ‘Ai ragazzi di Bir el Gubi’ (‘To the boys of Bir el Gubi’ – the Libyan site of two wartime engagements with the British). The centrepiece is a metal pole bearing the words: ‘Milano, 1945’. Red paint drips down towards a dangling Christ meant to evoke, I assume, the bodies of Mussolini and Petacci, which were strung upside down from the girders of a petrol station in the city.

You can only enter the house as part of a group. Mine was made up mostly of couples and families. A young woman ushered us in, meticulously clipping a tiny corner off every ticket before starting her spiel. Each room is laid out as it was, the walls jam-packed with photographs of babies, weddings, family outings and pieces of propaganda: ‘I would only like Italians to remember one day that, above all, I loved them, and that every act and thought of mine was directed towards the greatness of Italy.’

Here, the guide told us, is Rachele’s kitchen. Behold her pots, her crockery and her book of rustic recipes. Here is the dining table. To your left, the old iron cradle in which Mussolini was rocked; to your right, Rachele’s hatboxes. One room contains a gun cabinet and a fragment of the propeller from the Piaggio P-108 bomber in which Bruno Mussolini died in 1941. The beds are made up, as if still slept in. When Anna Maria was seven she was struck down with polio. In her room, we relived the night Mussolini fell to his knees and wept after a doctor said no more could be done for her. She survived, with a spinal deformity, but died from complications of breast cancer at 38. We stopped to admire her orthopaedic corset. A flouncy doll adorns Edda’s pillow. We were encouraged to sympathise with Mussolini’s distress as he ordered her husband’s execution. But what else could he do? It was a test of his authority. Creepiest of all is the uniform laid out like a corpse on Vittorio’s bed. This is what Mussolini was wearing three days before his death. Half a dozen sets of rosary beads were curled like snakes in its folds. A father asked his son to pose for a photo beside the foot of the bed; he was grinning from ear to ear.

Back in Predappio, the woman at the desk of the birthplace museum was managing expectations. This was not, she said, a house of memories, but a photographic archive of the architectural transformation of the town under Fascism. Did I still want to visit? The museum gave an insight into the way it must have felt to watch your hometown change from a backwater into the showcase for a rampaging new ideology. No one was guarding the visitors’ book here. It was also full of fawning, supremacist messages. I turned to the back page, where someone had drawn a cartoon figure throwing a swastika and axe into a bin. ‘This book is trash,’ they had written. ‘Fuck Benito and Fascists.’ At the bottom, in smaller writing, they had added: ‘Nice museum, though!’
