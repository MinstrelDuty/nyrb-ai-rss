# 正文

Tom Cruise​ has made a career out of playing cocksure studs sporting with danger: the teenybopper pin-up with a death wish. When he was young, he seemed to literally shine, reflecting light off his Ray-Bans and his famous back-of-the-cereal-box smile (the kind of all-American smile Jean Baudrillard called ‘auto-prophetic’: ‘Smile and others will smile back’). But even when he smiled, he looked strained. There was effort in that charismatically tight jaw.

Cruise has worked hard to make life look easy. From early in his career he was keen to advertise a body that would do anything. ‘He’s prepared to hang himself on a meat hook,’ his mentor, Paul Newman, once said. As time went on, this boyish zeal became worryingly tireless. In the *Mission Impossible* series, which concluded last year, he scaled rock faces and skyscrapers, battled with g-force. Unlike older action stars – Harrison Ford, say – Cruise is known for characters whose attitude towards duty isn’t grudging: they are eager to serve. Only occasionally has this manic servility been taken to its naturally destructive end, as a trigger-happy cadet in *Taps* (1981) or a paraplegic Vietnam vet in *Born on the Fourth of July* (1989). Otherwise, Cruise’s efforts have made his men the best in the business, whether that business is cocktail-making, pool hustling or piloting a fighter jet. He plays life’s winners, and he’s one of those winners, so why is he always so desperate to please?

When a Cruise performance is good, which isn’t always the case, it’s because he turns what should be an actor’s undoing – an apparent lack of inner reserve – into frightening vacuity: a desperate want of personality. He’s great as try-hard neophytes and fast-talking yuppies because overcompensating is his métier. The best directors he’s worked with have identified the aggression and anxiety behind his need to prove himself (his association with Scientology, which traffics in the language of efficacy, only adds to this image). They also know that Cruise’s most compelling feature isn’t his smile but his high, beseeching voice. I’m not sure he’s ever been better than he is in *Risky Business* (1983), whining to the school nurse that his life will be ruined if he doesn’t pass his midterms. Cruise’s best deliveries are at once speechifying and pleading, and they leave you unsure about which of these modes is stronger (think of the four-word appeal in *Jerry Maguire*: ‘Help me, help you’).

The main character in Alejandro González Iñárritu’s latest film, *Digger*, is a Texan oil tycoon called Digger Rockwell. He’s the best in the oil business and the most powerful man in the world. Eudora Welty said that one way of looking at Moby-Dick is that ‘his task as a symbol was so big and strenuous that he *had* to be a whale.’ The same can be said of Digger: he’s so cartoonishly larger than life that he had to be played by Tom Cruise. Cruise has transformed himself for the role, just as he did for *Tropic Thunder* (2008), in which he played a large, balding composite of Scott Rudin and Harvey Weinstein to surprising critical acclaim. Digger has a pot belly and a wrinkled brow; Cruise’s shaggy chestnut hair has been replaced by a wispy comb-over, and the voice, which Cruise rarely alters for roles, is a hammy Southern drawl. (Digger also lacks the usual eloquence of a Cruise character: he speaks crassly, splutters over his words.) The last time Cruise played a villain seriously was more than two decades ago, silver-haired and wolfish as a hitman in Michael Mann’s *Collateral* (2004) – the last chill of sociopathy before his Herculean efforts at reputation laundering began. There’s a reason Digger’s prosthetics look like one of the latex masks Cruise peels off in the *Mission Impossible* films: he’s not meant to be Tom Cruise.

At the start of the movie, Digger wakes up in his wood-panelled estate, the bedroom curtains opening onto a misty vista – eerily unreal, like a Thomas Cole oil painting of nature unplundered. He dotes on his mangy white cat, who sleeps next to him, and then staggers through the house’s many hallways, donning cowboy boots and fooling around with a baseball bat, all the while bellowing at various assistants. Digger looks like one of Roy Andersson’s pallid living corpses, but his movements aren’t stiff. There’s a showboating physicality to the role – it’s one big stunt. Digger’s problems begin when an employee informs him of a methane leak at one of his drilling platforms in Greenland. Digger has ignored warnings from his nervy head of environmental safety, Dr Ganesh (Riz Ahmed), and now a rig explosion has launched an ice shelf ‘five times the size of Washington DC’ towards Europe, imperilling millions. Soon, the senescent president (a red-tied John Goodman) and his coterie of Hegsethian ghouls are relying on Digger to save the world.

If these jokes sound dull it’s because there’s a twist half an hour into *Digger* that reassures us that Iñárritu is in bad faith with the film’s satire. He knows it’s not funny – and not only is there a reason it isn’t funny, but there’s virtue to this unfunniness. *Digger*, it turns out, isn’t a satire, but a film about satire, and more precisely about what satire can and cannot do. Iñárritu isn’t trying to make his own version of Adam McKay’s celebrity-filled disaster of a disaster comedy *Don’t Look Up* (2021), but something closer to one of Charlie Kaufman’s metafictional feints. In other words, we’re back in Iñárritu’s usual, heavy-spirited territory: the long sermon of *The Revenant* (2015), or the histrionic lassitude of *Birdman* (2014).

There are obvious reasons why Cruise would want to work with Iñárritu, who won the Best Director Oscar for both those films, along with Best Actor for Leonardo DiCaprio in *The Revenant*. Like Cruise, Iñárritu is a perfectionist and a try-hard. His films have a recognisable – and recognisably virtuosic – grammar, with their long tracking shots, constant camera movement and seamlessly stitched-together scenes. This style has been developed in collaboration with the cinematographer Emmanuel Lubezki, who perfected his swirling camerawork under Terrence Malick and Alfonso Cuarón. In Iñárritu’s previous films, the point of this technique seemed to be to create the impression of immediacy, as though events were happening in near-real time. The problem is that Iñárritu is a more programmatic director than Malick or Cuarón: you feel his administrative distance in each panning shot. The cinematography in *Digger* is less insistently showy than in *Birdman* or *The Revenant*, but the camera moves with the same proleptic certainty. Sometimes a change of angle seems to arrive a beat too early. This makes sense: Iñárritu’s camera is alienated from his action, and this alienation is the technological equivalent of Cruise’s tightly controlled acting style – pre-judged, inimical to spontaneity, disallowing of even one second of abandon.

Judging from *Digger*, Paul Thomas Anderson’s *One Battle after Another*, Ari Aster’s *Eddington* and the last two films by Yorgos Lanthimos, the auteur’s vehicle of choice in the mid-2020s is a form of elevated comedy (if not a contradiction in terms then an unwelcome collision). Like *Digger*, these works are maximalist and absurd, peopled with cartoon villains and overburdened by relevance. Their stars are big and their themes bigger: conspiracy, climate catastrophe, fascism. There are differences – Anderson is a less cynical director than the others – but if this mode of political satire has a common guiding star, it’s Sterling Hayden as Brigadier General Jack D. Ripper in *Dr Strangelove*, gravely intoning that the communists are after his ‘precious bodily fluids’ as he orders a nuclear attack on the Soviet Union. Only Hayden’s gravitas belonged to an older world, one that trusted in satire as a form, and now we’re left with the merely juvenile content of his speech: Goodman’s president, we’re told again and again, has ‘cancer of the asshole’. Besides Cruise, the cast of *Digger* is trapped in the world of shallow political cartoon. The only actor who is allowed to do more is Jesse Plemons, who plays the owner of a data-mining company and a dubious patron of the arts. He hovers menacingly in the background of frames, blanched with all the powdered guile of a Restoration fop.

Like *One Battle after Another* and Lanthimos’s *Bugonia*, *Digger* is shot in VistaVision, a fine-grained widescreen format first used in the 1950s (Anderson is thanked in the credits, presumably for his technical help). The argument directors make for VistaVision is that its size allows for greater detail: look at the depth of wide shots, the texture of the skin. For all its hugeness, *Digger* is a detailed film. Iñárritu has lavished care on the world of his bad satire; in fact he’s a little bit too in love with it. As the special lenses were being crafted for the cameras, I suspect Iñárritu was thinking about Digger’s mansion (with its taxidermied alligators pinned to the ceiling, its walls of pastel oil paintings) or the interior of his private jet, and not the briefly glimpsed world outside. A long shot at the end is meant to depict Earth post-climate catastrophe: it’s the least imaginative image in the film, and the one that looks most like it was lifted from any disaster movie of the last twenty years. No doubt Iñárritu would say that’s the point.

There’s nothing new to Iñárritu’s misgivings about satire – he is rather old-fashionedly modernist in his addiction to representational failure – but it is odd that the always efficacious Cruise has made a film that distrusts art and doesn’t believe in action. *Digger* is set to lose $245 million, making it one of Hollywood’s all-time biggest flops. Still, Cruise continues to smile at press junkets, in his snugly fitted T-shirts, and we’re back to the question of what he wants. On the basis of *Digger*, it can’t just be the answer he usually gives: ‘To entertain.’

Cruise plays a second character in *Digger* who is closer to the Cruise of these recent press appearances: hard-working, maniacally self-serious about art. At one point this character – an actor – shouts at his fellow cast members, and there’s a trace of the Cruise who snapped at his crew for not following Covid protocol (‘I’m on the phone with every fucking studio at night, insurance companies, producers, and they’re looking at us and using us to make their movies’). This Cruise spends a lot of time staring into a mirror, and, as if to emphasise his commitment to his vocation, there’s a poster for *Opening Night* on his dressing-room door. It’s a funny choice, because it’s hard to think of an actor more different from Cruise than Gena Rowlands – she’s *all* inner reserve – or a director more different from Iñárritu than Cassavetes. But for a second you find yourself thinking about Cruise the ageing actor, wondering if the projects he’s chosen weigh on him, what he’s going to do as the wrinkles form, and whether this world-famous can-doer is hung up on what he can no longer do. It’s where you wish the film would spend time, but Iñárritu’s camera has other, less interesting places to be.
