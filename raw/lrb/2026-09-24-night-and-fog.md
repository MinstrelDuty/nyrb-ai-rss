# 正文

In August​ 1942 a French farmer called Maurice Courtin was released from the POW camp Stalag I-A in East Prussia, where he had been imprisoned for two years in dire conditions. German guards put him on a train back to France along with 1300 other prisoners from Stalag I-A and Stalag I-B. Courtin was delighted at the prospect of being reunited with his wife, his children and his farm. When he arrived in Compiègne, north of Paris, he shared cigarettes with some of the young Frenchmen on the station platform. He then walked with these men to another platform where a train was waiting to take them to Germany. Courtin, together with the other released POWs, helped the men find seats and stow their luggage before waving them off.

Two months earlier, Pierre Laval, head of the Vichy government, had announced the creation of the *relève*, or ‘relief’, a scheme that would enable thousands of French prisoners to return home. But their repatriation had strings attached. Under this exchange programme, three skilled French workers would have to volunteer to work in German factories for every released POW. Laval had agreed to supply the Nazis with 250,000 workers. Vichy propaganda framed volunteering as a duty, a way of helping the 1.8 million French soldiers who had been captured in May and June 1940; workers who didn’t sign up were cowards. It was also hoped that the better wages, food and employment opportunities in Germany would encourage workers to volunteer. The arrival of the first contingent of returning prisoners in Compiègne was a media event and Laval himself was on the station platform.

But the *relève* proved to be a disaster. The rate of exchange worsened to one POW released for seven skilled workers. It caused huge resentment in France and spurred thousands of young men and women to join the Resistance. In September 1942, Vichy began to conscript recruits to the *relève*: as the historian [Eugen Weber wrote in the *LRB* in 1993](/the-paper/v15/n11/eugen-weber/hitting-and-running), this ‘drove young men into outlawry’.

Prisoner exchanges during the Second World War remain underexplored. Convoluted negotiations between the Allies and the Axis, conducted through the Red Cross, began in 1940 but were so drawn out that few prisoners were swapped using this channel. Only a small number of Germans were in Allied captivity in the first years of the war, but the number rose rapidly after the Allied victories in North Africa. In October 1943, Germany released 4200 sick and wounded Allied POWs in exchange for 800 German soldiers, chiefly from the Afrika Korps, who had been taken prisoner in the Western Desert campaign in 1942. The Allied prisoners, who were mainly British, were sent to Gothenburg in neutral Sweden and then sailed to Britain on the steamer *SS Drottningholm*. Similar exchanges occurred in the same month in Oran and Barcelona. Two Anglo-Italian exchanges also took place during the war.

Throughout the war displaced civilians, women, children and diplomats trapped behind enemy lines were released on humanitarian grounds. In June 1944, 25-year-old Ruth Adler was one of sixty Jewish women and children interned in the Vittel camp in eastern France who took part in an ‘exchange transport’: Jews who had passports or identity papers from Mandate Palestine, Britain or elsewhere in the British Empire were exchanged for German citizens held by the British in Palestine. Adler’s train left Vittel on 30 June for Istanbul, where the exchange took place. The Allied prisoners arrived in Haifa on 10 July.

The poet and playwright Itzhak Katzenelson wrote *The Song of the Murdered Jewish People* (‘Dos Lid Funem Oysgehargetn Yidishn Folk’), which depicts his experience of the Warsaw Ghetto, at Vittel. After taking part in the uprising of April 1943, Katzenelson escaped to Warsaw’s ‘Aryan’ side, where he managed to obtain papers that made him out to be a citizen of Honduras. Since Jews in Warsaw who were South American nationals were eligible to be exchanged for German prisoners interned by the Allies, Katzenelson was one of about sixty Jews deported to Vittel a few days later. He completed his fifteen-canto elegy there in January 1944. But the Jews in Vittel with these forged papers were declared stateless in March 1944, and with the prospect of exchange gone, Katzenelson tried to find other ways to ensure that his lament reached an audience. On learning that Adler was being sent to Palestine, Katzenelson entrusted her with a copy, which she folded into the leather handle of her suitcase. By the time she finally left for Palestine, he had been deported to Auschwitz and murdered. Adler gave the poem to his relatives in Palestine; it was published in Paris in 1945 and had an immediate impact. When the world’s first Holocaust museum was founded in the Western Galilee in 1949, it was named after Katzenelson.

Prisoner exchanges led to information on conditions in German prisons and concentration camps being circulated in Allied nations. Some accounts of incarceration were also published during the war, including *Paris-Underground* by Etta Shiber, an American woman in her sixties who was living in Paris when war broke out and became active in the Resistance. She was captured by the Nazis, and spent eighteen months in prison before being released as part of an exchange and returned to the US in 1942.

Shiber had operated as part of a duo with her friend Kate Bonnefous. Kate Robins was born in London in 1886; she moved to Paris just after the First World War, where she met and married Henri Bonnefous. They separated in the late 1930s, but remained on good terms. Henri would play a crucial role in his former wife’s Resistance network: through his contacts in the south-west he arranged for a *passeur* near Libourne to guide Allied soldiers across the line of demarcation into unoccupied France.

Shiber and Bonnefous first met in 1926 when Shiber visited Bonnefous’s dress shop in the ninth arrondissement when on holiday in Paris. The women were very different. Bonnefous was independent-minded and unconventional: she had had a child with an Italian lover, was divorced, owned her own business and drove her own car. In contrast, Shiber had lived a sheltered life on the Upper East Side. Her husband, William, had been the wire chief of the *New York American* and *New York Evening Journal*. Despite their differences, the two women became firm friends, spending time together during Shiber’s frequent trips to France. When Shiber wired Bonnefous with the news of William’s death, her friend, who had recently separated from Henri and moved into a new apartment near the Arc de Triomphe, invited Shiber to move in with her. Over the next couple of years, the women lived quietly, shopping at local markets, cooking and walking their dogs.

Shiber and Bonnefous were respectable middle-class women who, like some of France’s best-known resisters, had no history of challenging the authorities. But confronted with the Vichy regime and the Occupation they felt they had to act. Bonnefous had started volunteering at the Foyer du Soldat, a welfare organisation, when the war began, delivering food and crocheting woollen mufflers for soldiers on the front. Aged 53 in the spring of 1940, she was also among a handful of female ambulance drivers for the American Hospital of Paris (the French Red Cross didn’t allow anyone over 48 to drive their ambulances, and married women could do so only with the permission of their husbands). The Dunkirk armada, which took place in late May and early June, left behind forty thousand British soldiers in France. Most quickly fell into German hands, but in the summer of 1940 around three thousand still remained in hiding. This haunted Bonnefous, whose 29-year-old son, Len, was in the French navy and would later be captured by the Germans.

She was desperate to help British soldiers escape. As one of the men she rescued later remarked, Bonnefous remained ‘British to the backbone. If you were British, she would do anything to help you.’ Shiber was more hesitant, but in the end responded to Bonnefous’s insistence on the need for immediate action. In the summer of 1940, the two women created a network that helped British soldiers to get out of France. The first men they rescued were injured British POWs interned in Doullens, 125 miles north of Paris, where the Germans had requisitioned a hospital. As Red Cross workers, the women were permitted to take care packages to wounded soldiers. Bonnefous and Shiber would drive to Doullens in Bonnefous’s black Peugeot 402, which bore the Red Cross emblem. When they got to the hospital, they would tell the soldier they intended to rescue where to find the car. The Peugeot 402 had an unusual feature: the boot was hidden from the outside, located under the back seats. The soldier had to squeeze himself into the tight space and close the latch. The women would then drive him back to their Paris apartment, where they used their own rations to nurse him back to health. On one occasion, according to *Paris-Underground*, a pair of German soldiers flagged down the car and asked for a lift; they had no idea that they were sitting above an escaped British serviceman. When the POWs were fit enough, the women obtained false identity cards and travel passes for them and arranged their transit into unoccupied France.

Before long, Bonnefous and Shiber began working with Édouard Régniez, a young priest in the village of Conchy-sur-Canche, not far from Doullens, who brought small groups of escaped servicemen to their door: the men had been hiding in barns, attics or woods in his parish. By the autumn of 1940, the women’s escape line, assembled from scratch without any guidance from British intelligence, ran the length of France, stretching from the far north, via their Paris flat, to Marseille. It was an extraordinary feat. Shiber and Bonnefous were among up to half a million men and women in France who engaged in some kind of Resistance activity, out of a population of forty million. Later in the war, established escape lines involved networks of thousands, but in 1940 the lifespan of such routes was measured in weeks. The pair were caught after four months. After a short trial in March 1941, Shiber was sentenced to three years’ hard labour, while Bonnefous, whom the Nazis deemed the brains behind the operation, was sentenced to death for assisting the enemy. The sentence was later commuted to life in prison.

At Fresnes prison, where Shiber was held, women were supposedly entitled to six hundred calories a day, but in reality received two bowls of thin broth with a few chunks of vegetables floating on top. The United States, not yet a combatant nation, maintained diplomatic relations with Vichy. Fortunately for Shiber, the American consulate learned about her plight and arranged for the American Friends Service Committee to bring her weekly food packages – tins of tuna, potato salad, bread and sugar – which she shared with her cellmates. Almost every day the prison chapel bell rang, indicating that another prisoner had died. Shiber had three heart attacks while in prison. ‘In moments of depression,’ she wrote in *Paris-Underground*, ‘I heard that sound and told myself that one day it would sound for me, that I would never leave Fresnes alive.’ But in May 1942, she was released and returned to the US in exchange for Johanna Hofmann, a 30-year-old hairdresser, who had been convicted in 1938 of spying for the Germans.

Back in the US, she began to write up her experiences in wartime France, in particular her role in the escape of 150 British soldiers. *Paris-Underground* was published by Scribner’s, rapturously received, and sold more than half a million copies, remaining on the *New York Times* bestseller list for eighteen weeks. In 1945, it was adapted into a film with Constance Bennett and Gracie Fields in the leading roles. But despite its initial success, the book didn’t have a life beyond the war. Shiber died in New York in 1948, and her memoir was forgotten.

In​ *Paris Undercover*, Matthew Goodman pieces together Shiber’s life, giving an intricate account of her German-Jewish origins in late 19th-century New York, her move to Paris, her life in Nazi-occupied France and, most crucially for Goodman, her role in the writing, publication and promotion of *Paris-Underground*. She was unusual among members of the Resistance in being an older woman. Most resisters, both men and women, were young; few had family commitments. The three most celebrated women resisters were in their thirties: Germaine Tillion, Marie-Madeleine Fourcade and Lucie Aubrac, born in 1907, 1909 and 1912. There weren’t many who came of age at the end of the 19th century (one exception is the socialist Suzanne Buisson).

Younger people who made regular trips across northern France tended to arouse German suspicion; in contrast, Shiber and Bonnefous flew under the radar, at least for a while. As Goodman recounts, they took advantage of being older women, sometimes even getting German soldiers to pay for their extra petrol in exchange for chocolates and silk stockings. When chaperoning British servicemen who couldn’t speak French on French trains, Bonnefous, always dressed respectably and with official-looking identification from the Foyer du Soldat, rarely attracted the attention of French or German guards. ‘Over time,’ Goodman writes, the women ‘would come to understand that their natural appearance – the lined faces, the eyeglasses, the grey in the hair – concealed them more effectively than any wigs or false noses, providing almost a cloak of invisibility’.

Every reader of Shiber’s book was left wondering what became of ‘Kitty Beaurepos’, to whom *Paris-Underground* is dedicated: letters poured into Scribner’s offices asking for information on her whereabouts. The French authorities did not know her location because she had been deported to Germany as a ‘Night and Fog’ prisoner – Hitler’s Nacht und Nebel decree, issued in December 1941, authorised the abduction of individuals alleged to be ‘endangering German security’. After being taken to Germany, these prisoners would disappear into the night and fog; all traces of them were erased, and no details of their whereabouts given to their relatives. Five thousand such prisoners were taken from France and sent to German concentration camps and prison fortresses, where they wore jackets bearing the letters ‘N.N.’.

Bonnefous was held in several German prisons, where she was tortured and made to undertake forced labour. At Ziegenhain prison the women worked for twelve hours a day untying knots on ropes and stuffing feathers into duvets. Of 140 women inmates, Bonnefous was the only one from England, something the German guards did not fail to notice. A bungled escape attempt led to solitary confinement for fifteen days in a dungeon, where she was forced to march naked across the cement floor; if she stopped moving the guards whipped her legs with leather straps.

Early in 1945, Jauer Castle in Lower Silesia, where Bonnefous was then being held, was ‘liberated’ by Soviet troops. While we are now familiar with the sexual violence committed by Soviet troops at the war’s end, next to nothing is known about the rape of female prisoners. At Jauer, all of the hundred or so women still alive were targets of sexual violence, irrespective of age. Goodman writes of ‘Soeur Élisabeth … struggling with the man on top of her, trying to fight him off with one hand while brandishing a crucifix with the other’. Bonnefous, aged 58 and a ‘walking skeleton’, was not spared. She spent three weeks under the control of the Red Army soldiers and later declared she would have preferred to have been a prisoner of the Germans for six months. Eventually, she was repatriated to Marseille. Even though there was more than enough evidence of her vital contribution to the Resistance, she was given only the lowest level of compensation, Grade Five. She was, however, also awarded an OBE and the French Croix de Guerre (with silver star). Bonnefous died in 1965, aged 79.

When *Paris-Underground* was published in 1943, Shiber knew nothing about what had happened to Bonnefous. She hadn’t had any news since their trial in Paris in March 1941 and wrote in *Paris-Underground* that she wondered whether her friend was still alive. While researching *Paris Undercover*, Goodman unearthed Bonnefous’s handwritten notes about her years of imprisonment and her medical reports. He tracked down both women’s relatives and those of the people they rescued; he was also granted access to various family papers. He began to realise that *Paris-Underground* was exaggerated or fictitious in places, and to think about the consequences of this. Many details didn’t add up, including Shiber’s account of the beginning of the two women’s resistance activity. The book describes how she and Kitty stopped at a café near Orléans and encountered a fleeing RAF pilot called William Gray. The women decided to hide Gray in the boot of their car and take him back to their apartment in Paris, where they worked out a way to deliver him across the line of demarcation into the zone libre. But Goodman realised that there had been no William Gray. Shiber and her publisher had made the story more dramatic in order to capture the attention of the wartime audience. Most significantly, Goodman casts doubt on the number of British soldiers the two women rescued; he suggests the number is closer to twenty than 150.

Does this matter? Shiber did take part in illegal resistance activities; she was arrested, imprisoned and exchanged for a German woman interned in the US. What’s more, the reader of the book, as Goodman acknowledges, is made aware from the outset that names and places have been changed in order to protect Shiber’s friends and associates, who were still in enemy hands in Europe. Anyone picking up the book would have realised that Shiber, who was not an experienced writer, had not written the book alone: ‘in collaboration with Anne and Paul Dupré’ appears beneath her name. She also showed an awareness that prisoner exchanges can be morally complicated: of Johanna Hofmann she wrote, ‘I’m sure she’s back in Berlin already, setting to work to do us all the harm she can.’

At the same time, as Goodman points out, Shiber and her publishers acted irresponsibly in some ways. They made little effort to disguise the names of Shiber’s friends and fellow resisters, especially that of Kate Bonnefous. ‘Kitty Beaurepos’ isn’t really different enough. Copies of *Paris-Underground* found their way into the hands of the Gestapo, and Bonnefous was taken from her prison cell in Lübeck and tortured for having lied about the number of soldiers she had rescued. She was lucky not to be executed. Here, Shiber’s exaggerations were dangerous.

There are reasons to value *Paris-Underground*, however. For instance, Shiber’s account of life in Nazi-occupied Paris shows a vivid domestic world seldom seen in comparable memoirs. She describes the depredations and injustices of the Occupation, not least the looting of apartments by the Nazis. She notices that ‘linen was used to wrap up the confiscated objects,’ and recounts the parting shot of one angry French worker to the ransacking German soldiers: ‘Wait a minute! You’ve forgotten to take the nails!’

The winter of 1940-41 was one of the coldest of the century. Since coal could not be found anywhere, Parisians would take blankets with them when they went to visit friends and relatives. Some people even burned books ‘whose literary value was considered inferior to their worth as fuel’. When Hitler ordered the repatriation from Vienna of the body of Napoleon’s only son, the duc de Reichstadt, in December 1940 for a ceremony at Les Invalides, Parisians joked that ‘they’re taking our coal and giving us back ashes.’ Not every Parisian was condemned to freeze. As Shiber notes, bars and restaurants with a large German clientele had special charcoal privileges, as did residents in blocks where a German state employee lived.

The historian Paula Schwartz has written about Parisians’ frustration with shortages and rationing. In *Today Sardines Are Not for Sale* (2020) she describes a protest on 31 May 1942, when a group of women stormed a grocery store. They grabbed cans of sardines, throwing them into the crowd and shouting: ‘Housewives, help yourselves!’ Our knowledge of what daily life was like after the Germans made themselves at home in Paris has improved, but we still know little about the summer and autumn of 1940, when the Vichy regime and the German Occupation were still new. Shiber writes of a special lunch planned by Margot, Kitty’s maid, to celebrate the rescue of the ‘150th English soldier’. She had planned to bake a cake, but ‘not one of the necessary ingredients could be found,’ not even the candles.
