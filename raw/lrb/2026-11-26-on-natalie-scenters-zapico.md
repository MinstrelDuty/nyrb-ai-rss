# 正文

They called​ it macaronic: verse composed in multiple languages. Many medieval and Tudor poets combined English with Latin or French. One, John Skelton, made such mixing a speciality. Macaronics became less common around Shakespeare’s time, as poets began to anticipate readers (women, for example) with limited formal schooling. John Milton, who spoke and read a dozen languages, wrote poems in four, but didn’t switch between them in the same poem. Neither did most poets of the 18th and 19th centuries, despite the occasional Latin tag. When the macaronic style resurfaced in T.S. Eliot’s *The Waste Land* it was a matter of quotation and pointers: fragments collected from German, French and Sanskrit. Ezra Pound wrote macaronically as a flex, sometimes in languages he didn’t know well (Chinese characters in the *Cantos*, for instance). Macaronic verse makes sense, aesthetically and emotionally, when it says something about poet and audience, and about the way the poem fits in between.

In recent decades macaronic poetry has become popular among US poets who grew up speaking Spanish. Alurista, Juan Felipe Herrera and others who emerged in the 1970s, alongside Mexican American political movements, made a proud interplay among languages in macaronics and self-translations such as Herrera’s *Akrílica* (1989). Roque Raquel Salas Rivera and Urayoán Noel, both Puerto Rican, later did the same. (Noel even gave his projects new labels like ‘non-mono-aural’ and ‘lingurality’.) Brandon Som interweaves English with Spanish and Chinese: his second book, *Tripas* (2023), made him the first macaronic poet (and the second Mexican American) to win a Pulitzer.

Natalie Scenters-Zapico’s third collection, *My Perfect Cognate* (Copper Canyon, £12.99)*,* belongs to this macaronic tradition, but sits uneasily within it: half the book’s poems (some sonnet-sized, some multi-page) depict a life lived uncomfortably between languages. The other half of the collection focuses on postpartum depression. Scenters-Zapico’s early work described her life with her husband, José Ángel Maldonado, in and near the twin cities of El Paso and Ciudad Juárez on the US-Mexico border. Both Scenters-Zapico and Maldonado now teach at the University of South Florida in Tampa, but *My Perfect Cognate* portrays their safety – physical, cultural, intellectual – as deeply precarious.

The whole book is inventive, but one invention stands out: the *falso cognato* or false cognate poems, whose facing-page versions look like translations but are not. In one of these a Catholic statuette shows ‘an angel’s cheeks/swollen red, forever ready to blow/the horno at his lips’. In Spanish, we then see ‘un ángel siempre listo para soplar el horn en sus labios’, an angel forever ready to blow the trumpet. *Horno* means ‘oven’, however. Will this modern angel bake Mary alive or asphyxiate her? ‘Where is the éxito?’ ‘¿Dónde está el exit?’ But *éxito* is ‘success’: good luck finding that while you’re stuck at home, weaving ‘dark fábrica’. *Fábrica* means ‘factory’.

The *falso cognato*, as a form, is well suited to Scenters-Zapico’s melancholy exploration of what it means to feel at home nowhere and to live in constant fear of being misunderstood. Her sense of estrangement comes not only from her uncertainty about where she ends and her baby begins (that frequent predicament for new mothers), or from her depression, but from her experience of growing up along the border, which has left her with a sense of the *unheimlich*. ‘Spanish is a trap. English is a diversión,’ not a motorway bypass but a pointless game. ‘It’s no one’s falta I feel regalo in one/& buy my groserías with the other.’ English is, and is not, a *regalo* (gift). Spanish may nurture the poet, but it also makes her feel uncouth: *groserías* are ‘swear words’.

You don’t need to be bilingual to read these poems, but you have to be prepared to look things up and to recognise that all languages leave some things out. You also need a grim sense of humour. Another ‘Falso Cognato’ begins: ‘This broom of rosemary in my kitchen/declares me empresa. I’ve lived sin/& sinned. I’m not embarazada/to tell you.’ *Empresa* means ‘business’. *Sin* is ‘without’, which makes the poet at once sinless and sinful: ‘He vivido without/& he pecado.’ The once-purchased empress, in her well-swept kitchen, appears both embarrassed and *embarazada* (pregnant): she may have trouble riding that broom.

Only three poems (or six if you count the English and Spanish separately) bear the title ‘Falso Cognato’, but others produce similar effects. Three (or six) more, each called ‘En Cognato’, serve as a kind of control group, with Spanish that matches – semantically though not sonically – the English on the facing page. ‘Every year the city is overrun by piratas’; ‘Cada año la ciudad está invadida por pirates.’ (The pirates are kids at Halloween.) In another ‘En Cognato’, ‘My boss doesn’t care about the bebé on my hip’; ‘A mi jefa no le importa el baby en mi cadera.’ Is a bebé, in the eyes of an English-speaking boss, the same as an American baby?

‘I say English words/as if in Spanish. I say Spanish words as if/in English,’ Scenters-Zapico writes in ‘In Cognate’. The resulting discomfort makes her want to break things. She produces ‘text only a brain/that breaks English & Spanish can decipher … I find/the circuit breaker hidden in my chest.’ For other poets of the border – Gloria Anzaldúa, for instance – a dual existence brings a sense of defiance and joy. For Scenters-Zapico, however, it leads to a series of *falsos cognatos* and mistranslations (etymologically, a failure to bring across). No wonder she chose to place last in this book her only poem about a miscarriage: ‘the loss of this little/never-was or would-be – my body’s failure/to carry’ becomes ‘a perfect cognate in my mind’. She also notices the gap between what we demand from mothers and what we reward from fathers. Even the best fathers make her think of drones, bees in a hive or cameras above a border: ‘The fathers all get an evening off from being/great fathers. Their babies are all at home//with mothers no one, not even the drones, can find.’

Alongside the form she seems to have created, Scenters-Zapico adopts devices her contemporaries have pioneered. One is the deconstructed single word: like Solmaz Sharif and Monica Youn, she organises poems around dictionary definitions. ‘If a drone is *a remote-controlled or autonomous vehicle*’ designed for a ‘*setting too dangerous or difficult for a human operator*’*,* then ‘how can drones be used where I played hide & seek in the desert with my brother & sister?’ It’s a rhetorical question. How could they? ‘Can I fly a small drone to the other side of this life?’ Would such a flight be escape or suicide? There’s also the rearranged, part-erased government document. The longest poem in *My Perfect Cognate* draws half its lines from terms (‘construction immigration provisions construction’, ‘nationality inadmissible deportable individual’) used in a congressional bill for Trump’s border wall.

Buried in legislative hostility or feeling trapped at home, Scenters-Zapico sees endless borders and walls. ‘Earth is a heaven I hope/to scale, like a wall.’ ‘The trick is to pretend/the ladder keeps going.’ She also sees mirrors. ‘God knows I asked for a mirror to see between my legs. God knows I was nowhere in its reflection.’ Lyric poems can resemble mirrors, each poem showing just part of the poet’s true face. Observing those faces, readers may well feel like spies:

>           Some spies are true mirrors  
> that show the face refracted, yet whole  
> & still recognisable. Some spies  
> are as false as the mirror desilvering  
> in the bathroom.

The prose poem ‘Espejismos’ (‘mirrorisms’) examines ‘convex’, ‘concave’, ‘non-reversing’ and finally ‘two-way’ mirrors: ‘I assume I’m being watched by anything I can see my face in: the freshly waxed floor, the polished doorknob, the sheen of a sedan … My bathroom mirror, that two-way Judas, has an agent on the other side on hold with DHS.’

Walter Benjamin said that every document of civilisation is also a document of barbarism: a bilingual document, if you will. Scenters-Zapico writes for those most vulnerable to the murderous alphabet soup of DHS, CBP, ICE, USA, but also for anyone who lives with fear or uncertainty. Sometimes she’d rather die. ‘The river of shorn paper’ she crosses in dreams becomes a river of documents, but also a river dividing sleep from waking, gods from mortals and life from death. Traversing it, ‘I forget my name.’ On waking, ‘most mornings I wonder/who I can pray to that will make sure I never//have to survive waking again.’ In ‘The Gun & the Cowboy’ she stacks her thoughts into unadorned sentences: ‘I am doing my best not to kill myself before I die.’ ‘Another day. I read my poems & wonder. *Where is the world?/*Another day. I fall asleep to the dead pounding at my door.’

Such sentiments, in such taut lines, might remind you of Sylvia Plath. Scenters-Zapico, like Plath, can turn self-knowledge into a caustic agent. ‘Like a hot broth, I’ll strip any language clean,’ she declares in the prose poem ‘Cognate’. She could eat men like air, though she’d rather bury them in bilingual laminar flow: ‘I keep translations deep in my womb … I want this world to speak in a molten tongue that burns through all language & leaves behind black volcanic glass.’ Tongue: both a language and a tongue of flame.

Scenters-Zapico remains compulsively alert to the implications, double meanings, implicit puns and histories within words. In ‘Baby Blues’, the title refers at once to blue eyes (like pearls, or the ‘Epstein pearls’ found in newborns’ mouths); postpartum depression; the pale blue of baby blankets; and the musical genre, which the poem summons up through its repetitions:

> I swaddle my son in a blanket so blue  
> it becomes a river blue unlike
>
> the brown rivers of my childhood.  
> The brown rivers of my childhood  
> which swallow mothers & their children
>
> under currents woven in blue blood.

A mother with the ‘baby blues’ might feel compelled to write about her experience, but she might also fear repeating herself or repelling her listeners. ‘*Don’t write about becoming a mother*,’ the poet imagines her audience telling her. ‘*Don’t write about being a mother./Don’t write. Don’t write again,/you mother –*’. Depression is, itself, a motherfucker. Like the USA and its acronyms, once it singles you out, or finds you in its mirrors, it may never leave you alone.
