# 正文

At​ first glance this photograph of the pianist Erroll Garner – eyes closed, mouth open, his face illuminated by a source beyond the frame – might appear to show a parishioner in a state of rapture. It’s only Garner’s blurred hands that reveal he isn’t praying; he’s playing the piano. Or, rather, he is doing both. Garner is on stage at the New York Jazz Festival on Randalls Island, but he’s also preaching, bearing witness, testifying.

Garner was barely 5’2"; while performing, he propped himself up on multiple telephone directories. But he had a grand, orchestral style, steeped in both Harlem stride piano and classical impressionism. In 1956, when the photograph was taken, Garner was at the height of his fame. The woman who took the picture, Lisette Model, is best known as a street photographer. She counted Diane Arbus, Peter Hujar and Larry Fink among her followers. But the publication of *Lisette Model: The Jazz Pictures* – a book of little-known photographs – is likely to prompt a reappraisal of Model’s legacy (Eakins, £56).

Model was born Elise Stern in 1901 in Vienna. Her father was an Austrian Jewish doctor and her mother a French Catholic. She studied under Arnold Schoenberg as a young pianist and singer. ‘If ever in my life I had one teacher and one great influence,’ she said, ‘it was Schoenberg.’ After her father’s death from cancer, in 1926 the family moved to Paris. By the early 1930s she had abandoned music in favour of photography, and found a mentor in André Kertész. She made her first series of images, *Promenade des Anglais*, on a trip to Nice, where she secretly photographed tourists on the Riviera, cropping the negatives to remove most of the physical surroundings. Published in the leftist magazine *Regards* in 1935, Model’s portraits of the leisure class on holiday weren’t indictments of affluence and privilege so much as creepy, outsized depictions – in a style Arbus would later patent – of her subjects’ loneliness, despair and physical deformities.

In 1938, Model moved to New York with her husband, the Russian painter Evsa Model. She soon attracted the attention of W. Eugene Smith, who said her photographs ‘fired’ him with envy, and began publishing her work in *Harper’s Bazaar*. Carmel Snow, the editor-in-chief, complained of her predilection for ‘exaggerated, grotesque types’ – thinking, perhaps, of her most famous photograph, of an overweight woman bathing at Coney Island – but the magazine provided steady work for more than a decade. Model also photographed audiences in nightclubs for the left-wing magazine *PM*, the Popular Front’s answer to *Time*. (‘sad-eyed youngsters listen to hot jive at a New York nightclub,’ read the caption beneath her photograph of three men ‘in a state of enchantment’.) The Popular Front, a coalition of communists, trade unionists, middle-class liberals, civil rights groups and Hollywood radicals, was at the peak of its influence when Model moved to the US. She joined the New York Photo League, a collective of left-wing photographers that had formed in the mid-1930s to chronicle the American labour movement. (Its members included Smith, Robert Frank and Paul Strand.) Model’s political activities earned her a place on the FBI’s national security watch list, but they also introduced her to jazz. At Café Society, Billie Holiday was singing her anti-lynching torch song ‘Strange Fruit’, written by Lewis Allan, the stage name of the communist songwriter Abel Meeropol, who would later adopt the sons of Julius and Ethel Rosenberg. When the German Jewish exiles Alfred Lion and Francis Wolff founded Blue Note Records in 1939, the original investment came from a communist activist, Max Margulis. In 1948, the communist music critic Sidney Finkelstein published one of the first serious studies of jazz as an art form, *Jazz: A People’s Music*.

‘I was absolutely overwhelmed by jazz because I knew that was America,’ Model wrote. ‘And that was something I really wanted to photograph.’ *The Jazz Pictures* shows her feeling for the musicians, the fans, and the world they were trying to create in a country still defined by segregation, both formal and informal. There’s a plainspoken dignity to her photographs of musicians, but they’re unadorned: no moody atmospherics here. Some of the most striking images depict off-stage moments, such as Louis Armstrong on a tour bus, wearing a tuxedo and holding his trumpet, but lost in thought. As the photographer Berenice Abbott put it, ‘I know of no photographer who has photographed people as inwardly as Lisette Model.’ But the inwardness always stands in relation to the outside, to society and what she called its ‘sterile routine convention’. The photographer’s challenge was to ‘dare to see, to be aware of what there is and how it is’; like Robert Frank, another exile, she used black and white photography to make pointed observations about the American colour line. In her photograph of a young Quincy Jones watching a performance at the 1956 Newport Jazz Festival, Jones, in a dark suit and tie, sits in a sea of white people, a pipe dangling from the left corner of his mouth. The boy sitting behind him has a sullen look, as if his mother has just scolded him. Jones is applauding, but his expression is wary.

As Audrey Sands writes in her introduction, Model received a commission from Picture Press in September 1952 for a book of jazz photographs with texts by Langston Hughes and the critic Rudi Blesh, to be funded by the cosmetics magnate Helena Rubinstein. That same year, however, the FBI launched an investigation of Model, at the behest of Joe McCarthy, on the grounds of her alleged membership of the Communist Party and involvement in ‘front’ activities. Two years later, FBI agents turned up at her home to interrogate her. Far from coming to Model’s defence, Snow, her editor at *Harper’s Bazaar*, warned Rubinstein about her leftist affiliations. Rubinstein withdrew her funding and the project was abandoned. Hughes, who was himself targeted because of his relationship to the Communist Party, wrote a text to accompany another book of photographs inspired by jazz, *The Sweet Flypaper of Life*, published in 1955, but the photographer was Roy DeCarava, not Model.

Model drifted into teaching, both at the New School for Social Research and in private workshops at her West Village flat, where she mentored Arbus. (After three sessions with Model, Arbus’s husband recalled, ‘Diane was a photographer.’) She continued to document the jazz world. She travelled to the Berkshires in 1956 to take pictures at the Music Inn, the ‘Jazz Barn’ that Stephanie and Philip Barber, a young progressive couple, established on an old estate in Lenox, Massachusetts. She photographed Miles Davis, Sonny Rollins, Duke Ellington, John Lewis, Anita O’Day and Dizzy Gillespie. The musician to whom Model felt most attached, however, was Holiday; she considered her ‘one of the greatest American artists, and when I say “American” I mean black.’ Model shot Holiday in performance, reading a letter in bed, chatting on the telephone in a white satin dress and on the day of her funeral in 1959: a daring close-up, the frame cropped to the white silk lining of the coffin.

Today Model’s photos remind us of the anti-racist commitments that brought jazz and civil rights together in the 1950s – in an egalitarian interracial milieu considered more than faintly transgressive by the American establishment. The fashion magazines that bought her photographs cravenly observed the colour line. Sands quotes a letter by Alexey Brodovitch, the art director of *Harper’s Bazaar*, to Gordon Parks: ‘I’m sorry to be frank with you, but there is a very inflexible rule here in the Hearst organisation that forbids our hiring Negroes. I love your work, but I’m sorry.’

Was Model’s collaboration with Hughes and Blesh sabotaged by anti-communist persecution? Sands believes so, and the evidence, though circumstantial, seems compelling. What’s harder to explain is why Model did so little to promote her jazz pictures. Her monograph, published by Aperture in 1979, included not a single jazz photograph, only a passing reference, in the chronology of her life, to the uncompleted book. Phillip Lopate, in the long essay he was commissioned to write for the monograph, linked Model’s jazz images to her beach photographs, her interest in ‘people’s pleasures’ and her fascination with black culture. Model hated Lopate’s text. ‘I thought an introduction was to be written,’ she said, ‘not that I was to be put on trial.’

A new introduction was supplied by Berenice Abbott. ‘Photographers are a new breed of people, born of the 20th century, and therefore not easy to assess,’ she wrote, as if responding to Lopate. ‘To understand their “message to the world”, we need know little of their lives. Nor does a life need to be explained, nor can a life explain a work, especially the photographer’s … All we need to know about them is written in the images they create – naked for all of us to see.’

It was only in 1983, when the prints and negatives of Model’s photographs were recovered from her apartment, that it became clear just how central jazz was to her preoccupations. There were nearly fifteen hundred negatives related to jazz, as well as more than a hundred prints. Why it took so long for these images to be more widely circulated in the four decades since her death is as puzzling as her own reluctance to make more of them. To look at her photographs of Garner, Horace Silver or Bud Powell at the piano is to be struck by her emphasis on musical performance as a form of work – or, as Thelonious Monk put it more emphatically in an album title, ‘Work!’ ‘Before I started to be a photographer, I was a musician,’ Model said late in life. Judging by her jazz pictures, she never ceased to be one.
