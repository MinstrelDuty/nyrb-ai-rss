# 正文

In​ *Tightrope* (1984), Clint Eastwood plays a New Orleans cop assigned to catch a serial killer. The victims are prostitutes, the murders brutal and garish; scattered about the rooms are handcuffs and other evidence of kinky sex. On a side street, following up the latest lead, the hero’s partner looks at the full moon and asks: ‘You think it brings the crazies out?’ ‘They’re always out,’ Eastwood replies. The technique of *Tightrope* is conventional for its period and genre – ominous footsteps, close-ups of unsuspecting victims, telephone rings cutting into everyday action, cops striding two abreast along the station corridors as they plan their next move – but the premise is varied by a psychological quirk. Eastwood’s character turns out to be a familiar figure at strip clubs, partial to sex with handcuffs and, in his nightmares, a killer of women. Or is he the actual killer? The film toys with that possibility until a different suggestion settles in: this hero, a responsible single father and a reliable colleague, may do good things but he can’t be counted on for goodness. Potentially, he is one of the crazies.

Over a career of seventy years, Eastwood’s choice of similar roles had an undesired effect. He came to be identified with that character. As Shawn Levy demonstrates in *Clint*, a comprehensive and steadily appreciative biography, his early years were the ideal preparation. He was born in San Francisco in 1930. During the Great Depression, the Eastwood family took a step down in class; Eastwood Sr lost his office job and found work as a gas station attendant. ‘It wasn’t *Grapes of Wrath* stuff,’ Eastwood later said, ‘but it wasn’t uptown, either.’ His father’s motto stuck in his mind: ‘Nothing comes from nothing.’ At Oakland Technical High School, he wore a leather jacket and the ducktail haircut he would never abandon. ‘Tall, handsome, vaguely dangerous, quiet, with his own car … he inevitably met a lot of girls.’ Drafted in the early months of the Korean War, he reported for service at Fort Ord in California, where his strength as a swimmer paid off. Instead of shipping out for a two-year tour in Korea, ‘Clint Eastwood, buck private infantryman, became Clint Eastwood, army lifeguard.’ It was an odd exemption for a star whose name would come to signify masculine strength and a lonely courage. This mismatch of public image with private experience would be deployed by Eastwood in his later films with an effect of disarming modesty.

Early verdicts on his acting were discouraging. Nonetheless, he was signed by Universal Studios, and by his mid-twenties he was landing minor roles in B-movies (he made a few extra dollars by digging swimming pools in the San Fernando Valley). His break came in 1958 when his agent sold him to the producers of a new television western called *Rawhide.* The series ran for seven years, and Levy plays up its ‘focus on matters of social justice, democratic idealism, racial harmony, the toxic impact of violence’. There is some truth in the catalogue – *Rawhide* was above average for wit and soul – but if you were to search the episodes for protest against ‘the toxic impact of violence’, you would spend a long time looking. The generation who grew up with *Rawhide* are likely to remember most vividly Rowdy Yates, the character played by Eastwood – a tall man against a flat horizon, standing straight and just as upright in the saddle. Yet the presentation was relaxed, not rigid: Eastwood had that memorable quality, and it never changed much. Kenneth Tynan once pointed out that Humphrey Bogart switched from playing heavies in the 1930s to romantic leads in the 1940s without altering his delivery in the slightest way. Eastwood never played a heavy, but he regularly portrayed an ordinary man who had done bad things and knew it. Later, the man might look for expiation, but just as often he was incurable: the viewer was left to portion out praise or blame without help from the narrative.

In 1964, before the last season of *Rawhide* began filming, Eastwood took a gamble by flying to Spain to shoot *A Fistful of Dollars* under the Italian director Sergio Leone. The pay was unimpressive – a flat fee of $15,000 – but the work was minimal and Eastwood, even when he wasn’t on screen, was always the heart of the story. The Man with No Name says very little; he is an image more than a person – facing the camera down a Mexican street, his serape blowing in the wind, a dark cigarette clenched in the side of his mouth. Eastwood had long held a theory that came naturally to someone of his temperamental reserve: intensity of drama is inversely proportional to the quantity of dialogue. Under Leone’s influence this belief hardened into conviction. He loved the Italian’s laconic style: what need for ‘exposition’ in an action movie? As Eastwood explained, ‘the more a character says, the more it takes away from the image of decisiveness, of control. It weakens the character.’ One advantage of the Italian venture was that only Eastwood’s lines would be spoken by the actor; smaller parts would be dubbed in English for American audiences. *A Fistful of Dollars* stole freely from Kurosawa’s *Yojimbo –* a mercenary loner saves a town from two rival bands of crooks – but the film passes the vulgar test: it is memorable. Much of the intended effect could have been achieved with subtitles and only the sound of wind and gunshots. Yet Eastwood’s presence takes over the screen. Filmmakers often say of a star, usually a woman, that ‘the camera loves her.’ Eastwood has been favoured with this unearnable preference as much as any actor ever. The camera loves him.

Despised by reviewers, *A Fistful of Dollars* was a huge popular success. It was the first of what came to be called spaghetti westerns (a description Eastwood embraced), and there were two sequels, *For a Few Dollars More* (1965) and *The Good, the Bad and the Ugly* (1966). Eastwood would later star in an all-American imitation, *Hang ’Em High –* a crude revenge saga burdened by talk. ‘The Man with No Name’ became, in Levy’s words, ‘the Man Who Cares a Surprising Lot about Various Things’. But by then Eastwood could do what he wanted, and the next stage in his self-mythology came in 1971 with *Dirty Harry.* Inspector Harry Callahan of the San Francisco police is an institutional lawman who is forced to recognise that the institutions no longer uphold the law. He chases a psychopathic killer across the city, day and night, before he disobeys orders and kills him on his own.

*Dirty Harry* takes hold of the viewer from its opening sequence. The camera zooms out over the city rooftops, and eventually stops at the killer’s point of view, as he aims a sniper rifle at a girl in a swimming pool far below. The tension, once established, never relents, owing to brilliant nocturnal photography by Bruce Surtees, editing that is excited but always purposeful, and a percussive jazz score by Lalo Schifrin. Most of all, the inner life of the city is conveyed through sound-mixing of extraordinary detail and depth. A slightly earlier San Francisco movie, *Bullitt* (1968)*,* may have served as a stimulus, but Eastwood is more compelling in the lead role than Steve McQueen, and the director, Don Siegel, never lets us forget the disturbing premise: only a rogue cop can do what’s needed against a maniac outlaw.

Eastwood enters the first crime scene chewing gum. Soon after, he catches sight of a robbery taking place as he orders lunch at a burger joint; he asks the owner to call the police, curses when they don’t come, and holds onto his hot dog as he climbs down from his stool and handles the crime himself. He is fired at but kills one robber and wounds a second, approaching him with words that would become famous: ‘I know what you’re thinking. “Did he fire six shots or only five?” Well, to tell you the truth, in all this excitement I’ve kind of lost track myself. But being as this is a .44 Magnum, the most powerful handgun in the world, and would blow your head clean off, you’ve got to ask yourself one question: “Do I feel lucky*?*” *Well do ya, punk?*’ The man’s trembling fingers crawl an inch towards his rifle before stopping. Harry picks it up and walks away, only to hear the robber call out: ‘Hey, I gots to know.’ Harry walks back, points the Magnum at the man’s head, pulls the empty click and, with a smile, chuckles and walks away. He says the same words with a sharper edge in the film’s final scene, and it plays out differently.

What is the viewer to make of this? Harry’s sadism is palpable, yet the action takes a half-legitimate cover from the chivalric underplot: the wounded man is almost helpless, but his gun is still within reach and nothing good will ever come from him. Harry’s speech also carries the shape and allure of a riddle, a game of chance inside the deadly game of cops and robbers. His bitterness seems to have a deeper source than frustration at the law’s delay, and an explanation is offered near the end: he lost all ordinary affections when a drunk driver killed his wife. Yet this personal motive seems an afterthought. The vigilante cop was sure to appeal to a familiar right-wing sentiment of the time – outrage at the social decay supposedly fomented by a permissive liberal government and the indifference to law and order associated with the 1960s counterculture. The film pushes that point once. The psychopath, caught and released by the soft-on-crime authorities, comes back on the street with a metal peace sign for a belt buckle.

*Dirty Harry* holds up half a century later, but its legacy is not confined to its four inferior sequels. Eastwood had been drafted – not quite innocently – on the traditional side of cultural battles that would continue to dominate American politics long after the Vietnam War. Many aspects of his later career can be seen as an attempt to live down this unsavoury association. The phrase ‘Go ahead, *make my day*’ from the fourth film in the Dirty Harry franchise, *Sudden Impact* (1983), was used by Ronald Reagan in 1985 when he dared the Democrats to increase taxes against his veto. Yet the lines were seldom so clearly drawn. When Reagan invited Eastwood to the White House in 1987 as an honoured guest, it was to praise his work on land conservation. Again, Eastwood’s part in the 2012 Republican National Convention – where he staged an imaginary dialogue with President Obama (represented by a chair) – came across as lame and silly rather than contemptuous. He dismissed the episode with characteristic self-depreciation: anyone dumb enough to ask him for a stump speech deserved what they got.

Eastwood’s first film as a director was *Play Misty for Me* (1971)*,* a noir thriller in which he stars as a radio DJ who is obsessively stalked by a woman*.* Two years later, he made *High Plains Drifter*, which begins with a mystery man riding into a one-horse town on the shore of a desert lake in California. He is filmed from all angles; people line the street on the porches of homes and shops to watch him. The horse’s hooves are the only sound. ‘He likes to be by himself, more or less’ is the sum of what we learn about this stranger who says, ‘I’m faster than you’ll ever be,’ before dropping three gunmen who attack him in a barber’s chair. He is told about a US marshal who once tried to rid the town of corruption; he checks into a hotel for no apparent reason; and he suffers recurrent nightmares about a lynching on the main street. Only as he rides off in a mist – having killed several more people and burned down half the town – are we shown a name on a tombstone: he *is* that marshal, a ghost come back from the dead to avenge his own murder. The total effect is a cheat, and surprisingly artless: the dreary dead expanse of Mono Lake is a perfect location for a ghost story, but the film makes no imaginative use of it. The trick ending has nonetheless been admired by boys of all ages.

Of the​ old westerns he grew up with, Eastwood said that his favourite was *The Ox-Bow Incident* (1943), a conscientious liberal message movie starring Henry Fonda and Dana Andrews about the horrors of a lynch mob. He would come closer to that model as his career advanced. But the figure of the stranger was always what appealed most to Eastwood and his collaborators. Here, the prototype that mattered was *Shane* (1953), with the difference that Eastwood’s drifter never comes to know the people he saves and never attracts a disciple, unlike the gunfighter played by Alan Ladd, who wins the admiration of a young boy. In elaborating the myth of the redeemer-stranger, Eastwood would mark a notable advance from *High Plains Drifter* in *Pale Rider* (1985), where the hero may be a phantom once again but he acts now for a cause beyond himself.

The common theme of these stories of police detectives and gunslingers is the man who has come back from something – what it is, precisely, that he must atone for is left uncertain. This reticence lent itself to a mystique. Reviewers often compared him to John Wayne and Gary Cooper, but Eastwood’s manner was at once an inseparable trait and an effect of his restricted emotional range. Pauline Kael marvelled at his physical presence in *Dirty Harry*: ‘Six feet four of lean, tough saint, blue-eyed and shaggy-haired, with a rugged, creased, careworn face.’ But, by the time of *Magnum Force* just two years later, her review opened with an attack: ‘Clint Eastwood isn’t offensive; he isn’t an actor, so one could hardly call him a bad actor.’ His performances often seem to disobey the first instruction of any drama coach: ‘You are not *you* on stage, you are that character.’ Another common lesson – ‘Don’t just stand there; *do* something’ – proves effective for Eastwood when reversed: ‘Don’t just do something; *stand* there.’ He can give the impression of still waters that run deep, assured that no one will ask where they run or why. His aggression is not necessarily more real than his withdrawal into a silent self-containment. The consistency of a good actor is a supple thing – tense or relaxed in unpredictable variations – whereas Eastwood’s steadiness is all in one register. The limitation became, with him, a point of ironic ownership. When his characters swing into action after a drawn-out and expressionless prelude, they don’t spill the secret of an underlying motive. They are what they inscrutably always were.

In life, Eastwood appears to have been much the same. He could be gentle and accommodating, but he was self-protective where his career was concerned and, towards women, opportunistically forgetful. He was married twice – first to Maggie Johnson, in his twenties, and then to Dina Ruiz, in his sixties. Both relationships lasted a long time, but there were intermissions: an affair, for example, with Jean Seberg which led her husband, Romain Gary, to challenge him to a duel. His two onscreen partnerships – with Sondra Locke in the 1970s and 1980s, and with Frances Fisher in the early 1990s – looked a lot like marriages. When his interest waned, the usual policy was to walk away and avoid explanation, but with Locke he took measures to repossess the house she lived in, and later she won a handsome settlement on charges of fraud and contractual interference. He was at the height of his fame, prestige and earning power when he married Ruiz in 1996, and one gets a sense of his assurance and remoteness from a single comment: ‘Instinctively, I knew she was the right person. I was never a guy on a white horse. She’s a self-feeder.’ Yet marriages and relationships tell only part of the story. He has had eight children by six women. When one of the eight whose existence he wasn’t aware of pursued a grown-up intuition – having wondered at the name Clint Eastwood as the father on her birth certificate – he warmly acknowledged her along with two grandchildren.

There has probably never been a Hollywood star who so thoroughly and profitably mapped out a business career. He was the founder and owner of a film company, Malpaso Productions; the mayor of Carmel-by-the-Sea in California from 1986 to 1988; a buyer of properties around the cities he grew up in; and, as of 1999, co-owner of the Pebble Beach Golf Links. He supported environmental causes. Norman Mailer, a writer not easily impressed by actors, interviewed Eastwood for the *Observer* in 1984 and wrote: ‘I rarely liked a man so much on first meeting.’ Orson Welles, possibly looking to enlist him in one of his own projects, claimed to have seen *The Outlaw Josey Wales* (1976) four times and pronounced it one of the great westerns. Even with his load of extracurricular engagements, Eastwood found the time to act in more than sixty films, while he directed forty and wrote the music for half a dozen. Malpaso operated from the Warner Brothers lot, and for some years, around the mid-1980s, the company was estimated to have drawn as much as 17 per cent of the studio’s gross income.

Ask some friends about the Eastwood oeuvre and the titles that come up are a reminder of his versatility: *Escape from Alcatraz* (1979, as actor not director), *Bird* (1988), *The Bridges of Madison County* (1995). A prison break, a biopic about a great jazz artist, a romance. By the early 1990s, he could draw on the best talents across two generations of actors. Gene Hackman, Morgan Freeman, Sean Penn, Forest Whitaker, Laura Dern, Kevin Spacey, John Malkovich, Meryl Streep and John Cusack all said yes to the offer of an Eastwood film, and several came back to work with him again. He relied on the actors to know their craft, and a scene was normally done in one take or at most two; on the rare occasions when he asked for a third take, he would apologise to the cast and crew. In his later films, he stopped saying ‘Action’ to start the cameras – he didn’t like the artificiality of the moment or the adrenaline rush it triggered. Let the actors start when they feel like it.

Among the later films he directed and starred in, *Gran Torino* (2008) holds a special place for his admirers of every political outlook. The reason must have to do with its earnestness as a counter-statement against racism without any moralistic overlay. Eastwood plays an elderly churl, Walt Kowalski (who looks every day of the actor’s 78 years), and the story opens with his wife’s funeral. With barely a semblance of cordiality, he watches his children and grandchildren file into the church and take their seats. His muttered angry talk throughout is laced with racial slurs. He is acutely disturbed by the Hmong families who have moved in next door: ‘How many swamp rats can you get in one room?’ He fought in Korea, where the enemy ‘hacked men to death with shovels’, but he doesn’t like the killing he did, either, which the state of war made legal: ‘We used to stack up fucks like you five feet high in Korea. Use you for sandbags.’ Slowly, Walt makes friends with one of his new neighbours, a shy teenager, and gives him a job looking after his small front yard. For Eastwood, the story must have been in some measure a penitential exercise: after all, he had been lifted out of the fighting by his lifeguard job and thus spared both Walt’s violence and his self-reproach. And yet this film plays a double game: with his thumb and trigger finger, Walt mimes a kill shot at the Hmong gang that has menaced the boy; and in the end, he sacrifices himself to save his neighbours. His will bequeaths his cherished 1972 Gran Torino Sport to the teenager he first rejected and then took on as an apprentice.

The role​ of any popular entertainer imposes a burden that corresponds uneasily with the appeal to popular prejudice. After Dickens published *Oliver Twist*, with its portrait of the Jew Fagin as ringleader and corrupter of a band of child thieves, he was stung by the criticism of friends and later requited the offence by his portrait of Mr Riah, the generous kindly Jew of *Our Mutual Friend*. D.W. Griffith, having celebrated the Ku Klux Klan in *The Birth of a Nation*, offered *Intolerance* as an appropriate answer, proving his hatred of bigotry to be a logical concomitant of his love of law and order. Eastwood isn’t the inventor of a new genre in a new medium, like Griffith, or a genius on a par with Dickens, but his popularity has made him nearly their peer in his felt intimacy with an audience. It has likewise induced a similar pattern, as he looked back on his work: the occasional unthinking reliance on prejudice is followed by a dignified retreat. So, after the vigilante ethic of *Dirty Harry* and its sequels, a kind of revision came with the parallel drawn between cop and killer in *Tightrope*, and the absurd but corrigible racism of the old-man hero of *Gran Torino*.

His turn against the Leone formula took longer but showed up clearly in *Unforgiven* (1992), which is, of all Eastwood’s directed movies, the best for the script, the cast and the economy of the fable. Eastwood plays a retired gunman called William Munny, who is brought back to killing by the offer of a reward to anyone who cleans up the town of Big Whiskey. He goes for it with an aspiring young gun, ‘the Kid’, and his old partner, Ned, played by Morgan Freeman. They kill the bandits to collect the reward, but Munny and the Kid learn that Ned was captured and beaten to death by the town’s sheriff (Gene Hackman). The climax is a revenge killing on a large scale, by Munny, but moral justification for the wild justice of revenge is denied by everything else in the film. (When the Kid shoots his first victim, he says: ‘I guess he had it coming.’ Munny replies: ‘We all have it coming.’) The same baffled wonder in the face of violence has been noticeable in Eastwood’s films about the Battle of Iwo Jima – one from the American perspective and another from the Japanese – and in *American Sniper* (2014)*,* where the assassin-hero is a warrior whose job in the Iraq War places common feelings out of reach.

In 2004, Eastwood made his second all-out bid as director-actor with a story about the rise and fall of a female boxer. He cast a young actress, Hilary Swank, whose previous work in *Boys Don’t Cry* suggested she could meet the gruelling demands of *Million Dollar Baby.* Eastwood took the part of the coach who reluctantly agrees to become her trainer, and he plays it with a mixture of impatience and sympathy that turns to love. A promised sentimental ending is crushed, in the last forty minutes, until the film becomes an almost point-by-point inversion of heroic boxing movies like *Kid Galahad*, just as *Unforgiven* was a brutal revision of *High Noon.*

Eastwood announced his retirement this summer. His most recent film was *Juror #2,* in 2024, and it showed a didactic purpose obvious enough to be taken as his final word. A juror in a murder trial discovers that he may be implicated in the crime; he would like to do the right thing, but he is weak and knows it, and he can’t acquit the defendant without convicting himself. Institutions of the law are respected without any suggestion that they reliably bring justice or truth. But the script has lines that take care not to stir up the crazies: ‘This process, as flawed as it may be, is still our best chance of finding justice’; ‘It ain’t perfect, but it’s the best we got.’ The role of the relentless detective has been transferred from a violent cop to an ambitious prosecutor, who realises too late that she may have sent the wrong man to prison for life. It ends on an uneasy note of compromise, and a dangled question – an honourable partial retraction from the star of *Dirty Harry.* The film lacks energy, and the dialogue for the jury’s deliberation is thin: individual jurors seldom speak more than a sentence, and their exchanges are short and canned. Yet *Juror #2* is still impressive. Few popular artists have been capable of doubting themselves so frankly after a five-decade run of success. The words that come up regularly when people talk about Eastwood are ‘presence’ and ‘grit’.
