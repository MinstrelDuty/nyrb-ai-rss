# 正文

The​ US midterm elections saw Republicans defeated from Maine to the upper Midwest, down to Texas, Mississippi and the Carolinas. Trade policy loomed larger in these elections than it has since 1994, the year after Bill Clinton signed the North American Free Trade Agreement into law. This year, two-thirds of voters said that Donald Trump’s tariffs had hurt the economy at a time of rising food and fuel prices, caused in part by the war in Iran. Will the ‘blue tsunami’ be able to roll back the levies or the war? Congress has largely abandoned the powers to regulate commerce and to declare war, handing them over to the president: Trump launched his ‘liberation day’ tariffs using emergency powers first wielded by President Carter to punish Iran in 1980 (in February the Supreme Court ruled that these powers had never been intended to redress a trade deficit). Without increased self-assertion from the legislative branch, in other words, neither a rapid return to the status quo ante on trade – nor more radical reforms – are in the offing.

For the historian Marc-William Palen, the situation in the US is cause for alarm, but not unprecedented. His first book, *The ‘Conspiracy’ of Free Trade*, came out in 2016 when Trump was running for his first term. It argued that from the end of the Civil War until the Second World War, protectionism was a more powerful impulse than free trade in American life. Free trade was seen as a British ‘conspiracy’ that had been pitilessly crushed in 1897, when President McKinley (outdoing Trump) passed the retaliatory Dingley Tariff through Congress early in his first term; a year later, the US was at war with Spain.

In his new book, Palen gives a more explicitly political judgment of the turn to economic nationalism, and the ‘militarism, jingoism and war’ that go along with it. *Pax Economica* surveys the range of progressive movements that have been affected by or have taken up the notion of free trade since the mid-19th century, from liberals, radicals and revolutionaries to feminists, federalists and evangelical Christians. All have viewed free trade, which emerged from critiques of mercantilism in the 18th century, as a means of engendering peace. ‘Neo-mercantilism’, which involved the strategic use of protectionism, evolved in relation to industrial Britain, whose new regime of accumulation depended more than any before it on access to an open world market.

The resulting opposition between free trade and protection structures Palen’s book, which starts by tracking the reception of the great ideological champions of the two systems: Richard Cobden and Friedrich List. List was born in 1789 in the German state of Württemberg but spent much of the 1820s and 1830s in France and the US. His experience of these industrial latecomers resulted in *The National System of Political Economy*, published in 1841. The book was aimed at policymakers in countries such as the US, which feared being reduced to ‘hewers of wood and drawers of water for Britons’. List recommended a number of interventions that would help laggardly states catch up, including ‘educative tariffs’ to nurture ‘infant industries’. These suggestions also made him popular in Britain’s formal (and informal) colonies, whose views about free trade had not been sought.

Protection was sometimes wielded in these colonies as a defensive weapon. In Ireland in the 1880s this took the form of an anti-British boycott. By 1906, the National Congress in India saw Arthur Griffith’s Sinn Féin policy as just ‘another name for our “Swadeshi”’, in turn inspiring Wafd-led campaigns to ‘buy Egyptian’. List’s book was perhaps most effective in arming movements for independence: it laid out a plausible economic path to be followed afterwards, based on the developmental success of the US, Germany and Japan.

Cobden, who was born in Sussex in 1804 and was running his own business by the age of 25, spoke from the heart of the free trade empire, but Palen sees him as an insurgent, resisting attempts to return to the mercantilist ‘jealousy of trade’, which could only lead to war. Cobden had more worldly success than List – he made a fortune in calico-printing in Manchester – but his real distinction was as a proselytiser for the ‘economic peace movement’, making trips to Italy, France, Germany, the US and Canada. The Cobden Club was founded in London soon after his death in 1865 and served as the movement’s unofficial headquarters.

In 1860, a trade accord between Britain and France was agreed, named after its two negotiators, Cobden and Michel Chevalier, an engineer who was behind the liberal turn of the Second Empire; later, the deputies Frédéric Passy and Yves Guyot supported ‘libre échange’, linking it more clearly to the cause of peace via new leagues and congresses. Cobdenism also gained adherents across the Rhine: in the Progressive Party and its offshoots, Eugen Richter, Franz Hermann Schulze-Delitzsch, Alfred Fried and others mixed mutualism and calls for constitutional government at home with ‘scientific pacifism’ abroad. Cobden Club members in the US and Canada created copycat organisations, including the American Free Trade League and the Commercial Union; they also acted as counterweights in Grover Cleveland’s Democratic administrations to congressional Republicans lusting after Nicaragua, Samoa or Hawaii.

According to Palen, the stars of the commercial peace movement were not only national politicians, journalists or jurists, but ‘international men’ à la Cobden – bestselling writers whose names became -isms. Henry George’s ‘single tax’ on the assessed value of land – extending free trade to what remained a virtual monopoly in the old and new worlds alike – took Cobdenism to its limit: it was an assault on rentier power that George believed would eliminate the martial spirit and the war motive that arose from it, as well as the need for other regressive or growth-hampering taxes. Norman Angell pitched his book *The Great Illusion* at the rentier elite itself, though that didn’t stop it from selling two million copies between 1910 and 1913, with its message that war was bad for business. The economist J.A. Hobson opposed Chamberlain’s campaign for an imperial customs union and the Second Boer War on Cobdenite grounds; *Imperialism* (1902) won him acclaim across the Atlantic among critics of the Spanish-American War of 1898.

All three writers recognised the ‘growing pacifistic affinities between capitalist liberal radicals and socialist internationalists’, spurred by the ascendency of economic nationalism and imperialism. For Palen, this was more than a fin-de-siècle flirtation, forming an overlooked ‘Marx-Manchester tradition’. Socialists could cite Marx in support of free trade. He had after all attacked protectionists like List in the 1840s as being symptomatic of a backwards German bourgeoisie, which impeded the spread of industrial capital and with it the contradictions that were the conditions for socialism. But Palen shows that the leading minds of social democracy owed at least as much to Manchester in the lead-up to the First World War. Whatever their differences before the war, Eduard Bernstein and Karl Kautsky shared an analysis of the rise of rival imperialisms and the way to mitigate them, arguing that a ‘United States of Europe’ could act to abolish war through free trade, arms control and arbitration. Both spent years in exile in London. There Bernstein befriended the elderly Engels, as well as ‘ethical socialists’ whose influence over him proved more lasting: that the New Testament rather than *Capital* was the key text for Keir Hardie and Ramsay MacDonald did not diminish his admiration for either*.*

The idea that free trade could be the harbinger of peace and goodwill among nations was almost always fired by religious faith. Priests and pastors crisscross this history, moving between religious and secular groups in Europe, the US and Asia. In the mid-19th century Joshua Leavitt, a Congregationalist minister, was editor of the *Emancipator* and the *New York Evangelist,* and a contributor to the *League*, whose motto was ‘free trade: the international common law of the Almighty’; some Irish Catholics in New York, Chicago and Dublin were led to Georgism by their priests. After 1918, the YMCA and YWCA became vehicles for Protestant pacifism, producing minister-intellectuals of the ‘social gospel’ such as Kirby Page, who combined a Hobsonian analysis of US imperialism in the Philippines and Latin America with firmer opposition to racism in his call for a ‘new Christian social order’.

Palen devotes a chapter to free trade feminism, but the women he profiles were as likely as men to have come to the issue via adjacent causes and to divide along the same lines on race, empire and war – divides based, in Palen’s view, on their fidelity to Cobdenism. What was different was their focus: in particular, their concentration on the cost of food and the hunger created by war, and persisting after it. This made them among the most effective advocates of the movement as well as the most inventive. Florence Kelley, who corresponded with Engels and translated *The Condition of the Working Class in England*, founded the National Consumers’ League in 1891 to educate Americans about sweatshop labour. She joined Jane Addams at the Second Congress of the Women’s International League for Peace and Freedom in Geneva in 1919, where they brought their experience of urban poverty in the US to postwar relief efforts in famine-racked Europe. Emily Balch, another member of the league, was an economics professor at Wellesley, and had worked to bring the war to a negotiated end with Henry Ford and the Neutral Conference for Continuous Mediation. All were enthusiastic about the idea of a League of Nations, at least at first.

Whether their plans for concerts, confederations and global government would be able to find effective expression in such a body was not settled until the Second World War. A century after the repeal of the Corn Laws, the ‘pacifistic interwar torchbearers’ of Cobdenism oversaw ‘the creation of a more liberal order’ after the war, one that ‘bore the trappings of their Pax Economica’. The agent of change was the US State Department under Cordell Hull and Will Clayton – passionate free-traders who backed the UN, IMF, World Bank, Marshall Plan and the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade – acting in ‘alliance’ with grassroots feminists, Christians and co-operativists determined to avoid a return to ‘the economic nationalist status quo … that had deepened the Great Depression’.

In just a few years, however, Palen writes, ‘multilateralism was floundering’ as the Cold War began, and policymakers ‘started to identify their envisaged postwar Pax Americana more with the projection of US military might than with economic co-operation’. American preponderance was not the problem. For the ‘commercial peace movement’, the backing of the leading power of the day could even be ‘considered essential for obtaining their democratic, equitable, non-interventionist and interdependent economic order’. The ‘culprits’ blocking this path were the ideological trifecta of ‘neocolonialism, neomercantilism and neoliberalism’ – the last of which co-opted radicalism by invoking its language (such as free trade’s ‘power to promote peace and democracy’) without the substance.

Almost two decades on from the 2008 crash, the hollowness of the claims for the Washington Consensus is clear. Yet opposition to it has led some on the far left to forget that free trade has not always been equivalent to the ‘hawkish right-wing neoliberal championing of free markets’. Palen argues that in a new era of authoritarian nationalism and protectionism, progressives must recover ‘a radical economic cosmopolitan tradition that for so long created alliances between peace-minded liberal reformers and leftist revolutionaries’. Though he prefers to locate them on a spectrum he calls ‘left-wing’ or ‘left-leaning’, his book revolves around radical liberals. In tracing their roots to the 19th century, he joins a number of historians who are seeking to disentangle the ideology of liberalism from the company it kept in the mid-20th century. In Palen’s case, this means looking to a time before the Cold War, when ‘cosmopolitan idealists’ stood up to ‘nationalist realists who viewed geopolitics as a matter of perpetual war’.

His claim that free trade united progressive forces around an effective peace programme rests on this reconstruction. But it isn’t clear that the binaries on which he builds his case are more accurate than those inherited from the Cold War. On the one hand, his framing of the struggle over free trade in ideological terms has the merit of reminding us of the relative autonomy of ideas in the present. On the other hand, his detachment from the material interests tied to trade comes close to internalising the worldview of many radicals, who saw themselves as above such base calculations. As the title of a Spanish homage put it in 1865, *Cobden, Moralista.*

List was no autarkist. Born in the wake of decrees abolishing what remained of feudal titles, tithes and tax exemptions in France, he sought to replicate the revolution across the Rhine, in a unified, constitutional, tariff-free Germany. He was forced to flee in 1825 as a result and ended up in Philadelphia, where he discovered Alexander Hamilton and the ‘national economists’ around Henry Clay, as well as the protectionists Mathew and Henry Carey. The publication of *Outlines of American Political Economy* in 1827 made him known beyond Whig circles. When Andrew Jackson became president in 1829, he granted List citizenship and in 1830 sent him to Hamburg as American consul. List instead spent much of his time in Paris, returning to the US a few years later to write his *National System of Political Economy*. French debates on how best to balance commercial liberty with national sovereignty were in full swing under the July Monarchy. List drew on the work and policies of the French president Adolphe Thiers and others inspired by Napoleon’s continental system to argue that free trade at home ought to be combined with ‘educative tariffs’ – or ‘wise protection’ – to encourage the growth of key industries, until such a time as they could stand on their own against foreign competitors.

In other words, the chief critic of ‘cosmopolitical economy’ was about as cosmopolitan as they come. He did not single out his homeland, but explained how France and the US might move with it towards the uplands of modernity; as editor of the *Readinger Adler* he ran articles supporting Greek independence and took the side of the new Latin American republics against Spain. But not everyone, as Palen points out, was deemed worthy of the blessings of nationhood: ‘uninstructed, indolent’ peoples in the tropics required civilising by those from temperate climates.

Yet this justification for colonialism was largely indistinguishable from that of John Stuart Mill and it is a stretch to argue that the Listian system in itself was ‘far more synonymous with militarism, jingoism, war and imperial expansion’ than the liberal sort. Not only are ‘exceptions’ to this ‘rule’ so numerous as to render it meaningless (Palen lists China, India, Egypt, Argentina, the Philippines, Cuba, the pan-Africanists and much of the anti-colonial world as adopting Listian ideas to gain, plan or preserve their independence). More to the point, List was not simple-minded: his prescriptions were based on a historical analysis of England, as an ‘example and a pattern to all nations’. The acquisition of colonies, use of tariffs and promotion of an activist state from ‘the days of Elizabeth’ had allowed England to achieve industrial growth at the expense of the manufacturing power of India, before ‘kicking away the ladder’. The English were hypocrites. List admired them. The ‘torrid’ peoples of this planet have for their part largely ignored his advice to stick to agriculture.

If Palen exaggerates the differences between List and Cobden, he minimises the divisions within liberalism between radical and more mainstream currents. The Manchester School was never just a byword for peace. In the mid-19th century, it had a more obvious association with laissez-faire. That is why so many liberals outside Britain kept their distance from it, often coining a separate term for the faith in free markets central to the creed there: *Manchestertum* in Germany; *liberismo* in Italy. The timing of the repeal of the Corn Laws in 1846 was, as Cobden noted, a result of the famine in Ireland, which free trade was supposed to alleviate in the form of cheap grain imports. In practice, laissez-faire exacerbated problems caused by the failure of the potato crop, since British officials viewed markets – once freed from the shackles of unwise legislation – as the alternative to ‘philanthropy’ and other forms of interference, from soup kitchens to price-setting and export controls. Rather than peace and prosperity, this led to repression, the suspension of civil liberties and the dispatch of troops and ships. Palen writes that Irish nationalism took a protectionist turn after the death in 1847 of Daniel O’Connell, without noting the role of the famine (in a country of eight and a half million, more than a million died and another million emigrated).

In fact, laissez-faire is rarely mentioned in this history, dismissed along with the ‘enduring myth’ that the first age of globalisation was one of free trade ‘run amok’. Palen argues that the limitations of the peace movement in the 19th century had nothing to do with either, but were instead the result of ‘white supremacy’ and ‘Euro-American-centrism’. Missing from this account are the markets or any discussion of the class that promoted them as the great palliative. The appeal of radicals was circumscribed in places where price-setting and opening up to global trade had done terrible harm, and where the state could jumpstart development.

Without the impetus of market fundamentalism, it is impossible to understand the shift that took place a few decades later with the rise of New Liberalism. Severing the link between free trade at home and abroad, the proponents of New Liberalism posited that state intervention in domestic trade should do more than protect individuals from harm, instead helping them to realise their capacities through education or by insuring them against sickness, injury or old age. The revival of Cobdenism at the turn of the century entailed major changes. Hobson initially demoted foreign trade in his vision of a socially reformed Britain: redistribution of income, he argued, would obviate the need for it. The new Cobdenites almost all endorsed a global governing body of some sort, since the ‘universal republic’ Cobden foresaw had failed to materialise and frictions had multiplied along with commerce. For Palen, the reason is simple: too many states had chosen a different guide in List. But other states rejected both. What to do with countries that declined to trade freely, or at all?

The response of liberal imperialists was that they should be forced to do so. Sending gunboats to Canton, Kagoshima and Alexandria extended the trade in opium to China and upheld the extraterritorial rights of merchants in Japan and bondholders in Egypt. Cobdenites were critical of this approach, but didn’t manage to alter it. Palen ignores the reasons for this by focusing on the powerful reach of radical middle-class pacifism, without weighing it against rival currents of liberalism. Since the ‘century of global market integration’ up to 1914 ‘derived in far larger part from the imperialism of economic nationalism than from the imperialism of free trade’, the latter does not detain him. Inattention to British liberal imperialism in particular means Palen doesn’t attempt to evaluate critically the challenge his radicals really posed to war, or what might be learned from instances when they failed to do so.

In the first decade of the 20th century, the common liberal-radical response to the possibility of a great power conflict was Angellism: since economic interdependence made war irrational, it should be deepened, and efforts should be made to explain to the public and politicians that ‘war does not pay.’ Palen passes over the failure of all this to prevent, contain or cut short the slaughter that followed. It was a liberal government committed to free trade that plunged Britain into war; radicals had promised to stop war at all costs, but in the end only a few resigned from cabinet. Others leaped at plans to divvy up the German and Ottoman empires – or to blockade the former, in a liberal hijacking of globalisation, rather than a revival of mercantilism.

Intriguingly, Palen’s study suggests the radicals found less support among their own leaders than among those of moderate socialists. The result was not what he leads us to expect, however. Rather than strengthening the movement against war, this served to weaken socialists’ vigilance in the summer of 1914; tensions between rival capitals in Europe were on the wane, according to their Angellite-inflected analysis, and so there was no need for national parties to do much about the pledges they had made a few years earlier to prepare for general strikes in order to prevent military mobilisation.

What Palen labels the ‘Marx-Manchester tradition’ depended from the start on a more motivated reading of Marx than he allows. Marx was not permitted to address the Free Trade Congress held in Brussels in 1847. Reading the speech he had prepared, it’s not hard to see why. Pointing to the class harmony it had supposedly induced ‘between the different classes of one and the same nation’, Marx argued that ‘the fraternity which free trade would establish between the nations of the earth would not be more real; to call cosmopolitan exploitation universal brotherhood is an idea that could only be engendered in the brain of the bourgeoisie.’ So much for the central conceit of Palen’s ‘peace workers’: in Marx’s view, there could be no comity of nations under capitalism. His endorsement of free trade was contingent even in these early years. Insofar as it hastened social revolution, it merited qualified support: ‘in this revolutionary sense *alone* … I am in favour of free trade.’

For right-wing social democrats, the evolution of capitalism made its overthrow unnecessary, even undesirable: gradual reforms engendered by universal suffrage could do the trick. Palen reduces the ‘point of divergence’ between these essentially liberal and socialist conceptions of change – of means as well as ends – to a matter of timing, of ‘whether the peace and prosperity from worldwide free trade was itself the desired end’ or ‘next progressive step towards a socialist Pax Economica’. But this isn’t the way things happen. For many free traders in his story, the point was to pre-empt socialism, while for the revolutionaries the threat from economic crisis and war was so grave as to brook no delay. They were liable to come to blows well before they reached their respective destinations; indeed, to arrive at these destinations required it.

Has collaboration​ between the two on the basis of free trade been any more effective at uniting progressive forces around peace since 1914? Labour’s adherence to sound finance spelled disaster in 1931, when cuts to the dole and public pay to appease the markets led to the government’s collapse. Britain didn’t ‘repudiate’ or ‘abandon’ the gold standard and free trade; it was thrown off them by a faith so inflexible it led as good a liberal as Maynard Keynes to revise the prewar tenets of political economy.

The eclipse of Britain by the US as leader of the liberal order at the close of the Second World War is seen by Palen as marking the peak of the left-wing commercial peace movement. But he gives few clues as to the reason for this monumental shift. The representatives of heavy industry tended to favour high tariffs until around 1910, when the peace ‘movement became mainstream enough to start to receive financial backing from wealthy industrial benefactors’, such as Andrew Carnegie, Henry Ford and the soap magnates Joseph and Mary Fels. It isn’t clear from Palen’s account why the movement’s moment of multilateral triumph proved so vanishingly brief at the end of the Second World War. Yet, viewed in context, free trade was ill suited to the postwar economic landscape. American manufacturers were so dominant that when Washington imposed an ‘equal playing field’, it led to huge trade deficits, capital flight and political crises across Western Europe. Switching tack, the US not only supplied allies with dollars, but allowed them to protect their home (and imperial) markets, in exchange for removing left-wing parties from office.

Palen gives the adaptation of the Social Democratic Party in Germany to this new order a euphemistic gloss: he writes that it ‘tempered’ its ‘left-wing vision of globalisation’ to ‘the hard realities of the Cold War and the nationalistic demands of the welfare state’. This led to the Godesberg Programme of 1959, which ‘curtailed the power of monopolies, protected private property, provided aid for the developing world … opposed nuclear weapons, and called for free trade as a requirement for world peace’. He doesn’t mention that the SDP also renounced the goal of replacing capitalism with socialism; or that two years later, the ‘pacifistic heritage’ the party supposedly shared with left-wing liberals was no barrier to its backing rearmament inside Nato.

Since Nato doesn’t make an appearance in his book, the EU can be described without qualification as a ‘peace project’, and the decision of British voters to leave in 2016 can be said to threaten ‘the very fabric of the European peace project’. For thirty years, membership has always followed insertion into a military alliance led by the US, which has conducted wars in the Balkans, and as far afield as Afghanistan, Iraq and Libya. Brexit has failed to disrupt this part of the project: Andy Burnham is as eager as his predecessors to press a united Europe on to victory in Ukraine, using British-made missiles to strike inside Russia. Electoral defeat in 2019 finished off the anti-imperial foreign policy Labour briefly followed under Corbyn.

If ‘remnants’ of the left-wing commercial peace movement are ‘all around us’, they are to be found somewhere other than politics. Today, Palen tells us, ‘you can brush up on your Esperanto’ on Duolingo; play Georgist-inspired Monopoly, ‘the world’s most popular board game’; or shop duty-free – a ‘global phenomenon’ that ‘helped cultivate a new co-operative era of decolonisation, prosperity and peace’ in Ireland in the 1950s, when the first concession selling whiskey and chocolates opened at Shannon Airport. None of which suggests that free trade ever had the power to further mutual understanding; rather, it shows that capital can handily neutralise far bigger threats to itself than these by turning them into commodified schlock.

Theorising about the relationship between trade and the global political order has had to contend with a common problem: capitalism emerged in a world of multiple states, transforming each of them, but without altering the multi-state character of the system. Disputes arising between national capitals posed serious risks to all of them, including the irruption of conflicts none could bring under control. The idea of a pax economica seemed to offer a solution in a form immanent to capital, in which trade itself would smooth out interstate relations. Palen repeatedly calls this a ‘dream’. Yet by the end of his book, the leaders of the commercial peace movement are said to have realised all along that their utopia of trade could only be achieved through the auspices of a leading state or states – a Pax Americana, Europa or Atlantica.

That the two concepts were at one time quite distinct is clear from his own account, however. In 1918, Henri Lambert, owner of the world’s largest glassworks, published his vision of a postwar world, *Pax Economica*, considering it ‘the one hope’ for ‘those who have a justified horror of an autocratic Pax Germanica, who do not want a Pax Britannica – nor wish for a Pax Americana’. It was ‘not a “league of nations” for the enforcement of peace, but a “Concert of Nations” for the establishment of economic liberty and equity’ that would be a ‘safe democratic alternative of the “Balance of Power”’ and a ‘substitute for Hegemony’.

The change from this self-enforcing ‘treaty of economic peace’ to the very forms of coercion it was meant to replace illustrates the gulf between the stories told about capitalism and the actual means used to ensure its reproduction. If we are on the precipice of a new cycle of protection, blocs, exclusive spheres and even land grabs, Palen’s advice to the left would be to avoid succumbing to either a reactive nationalism or a revived neoliberalism. That doesn’t mean a new economic peace movement could – or should – lead the search for an alternative. Doux commerce was never that doux.
