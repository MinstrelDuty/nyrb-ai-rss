# 正文

Towards​ the end of *The Anniversary*, Andrea Bajani considers the Italian ‘tendency to reduce every social act and every identitarian impulse to family’: not to recognise any ‘form of power … as higher than kinship bonds’. The four of his novels published in English explore this cultural trait while registering his growing exasperation with a heritage he shares. Lorenzo, the narrator of *If You Kept a Record of Sins* (2007), addresses his story to his dead mother, Lula, whose funeral he is attending. He is the product of an adulterous fling for which Lula was repudiated by both her husband and her well-to-do family. Later she decided to bring an older man into her home as a father figure for Lorenzo, then had an affair with a business associate who was moving his company to Romania. Lula took frequent trips abroad that turned into prolonged absences, abandoning the boy who, now in his late twenties, goes to Bucharest to bury her.

The book is remarkable for its curious blend of tone and focus. Lorenzo appears to have no concern other than the loss of his mother. No mention is made of friends, activities, a job. Yet the reconstruction of the family story, though spoken to the dead mother (then you did this, then you did that) remains cool and detached. He meets her former business partner and lover, and the man’s younger girlfriend, whose arrival on the scene accelerated Lula’s decline into alcoholism. The business partner tries to draw Lorenzo into his sleazy world, while the girlfriend seems ready to leave the older man for the new arrival; Lorenzo refuses to engage with either. The book teems with similes and metaphors that transform the ordinary into the menacing or grotesque: ‘coat-racks were like skeletons lurking in the lobby,’ a ray of light crossing the room is ‘a sabre slice’. The toothbrush in the bathroom of the apartment where Lula died is ‘an old flag on an abandoned fortress’. Nevertheless Lorenzo cleans his teeth with it. Visits to Ceaușescu’s palace and a coffin factory extend the mood of oppression and doom. The end of the book brings neither catharsis nor resolution, leaving the young man still in thrall to his mother, unable to move on.

*Every Promise* (2010) is more ambitious and disorienting. After failing to conceive a child with Pietro, Sara leaves him. That same day he learns of the death of his maternal grandfather, whom he barely knew. Pietro wasn’t allowed to refer to him as *nonno*, only as Mario; his traumatic experiences in the Russian campaign of 1942 had left him demented and sometimes violent, therefore not worthy of the family. In a strange strategy of substitutions that pervades the novel, rather than mourning his failed marriage Pietro becomes fascinated by this unhappy figure. He revisits the childhood home from which his father banned Mario and finds it inhabited by a man called Olmo, another veteran of the Russian campaign who soon enough begins referring to Pietro as his grandson. Again the novel is rich in metaphor, this time of an unsettling and surreal variety that aligns with the unlikelihood of the plot: ‘Olmo’s house was becoming Russia … the kitchen walls gradually vanishing as Russia made room for itself.’

Meanwhile Sara is pregnant from a casual encounter with a younger man. She confides in Pietro’s mother – who seems to orchestrate her son’s relationships – and the narrator finds himself ‘looking for traces of Sara in my mother’s face’. The mother soon becomes close to Olmo too. Pietro is persuaded to go to Russia and contact the relatives of a Russian soldier whom Olmo’s companions captured and hanged. Sara and Pietro’s mother approve. In one of many bizarre scenes, Pietro, in his Russian hotel room, receives a series of pocket dials from his mother’s mobile. Calling her back, he hopes ‘to climb up from the inside to the edge of the pocket, and from there to escape’. When he returns to Italy he finds Sara waiting for him at the airport. His willingness to address the wounds of the past seems to have opened up possibilities in the present.

*The Book of Homes* (2021) carries experimentation a step further while remaining anchored to the domestic. Each chapter describes one of the homes of a third-person protagonist called ‘I’, a name that allows for some curious formulations: ‘The clattering of dishes … the water running from the faucet are probably I’s first memories, even if he doesn’t remember them.’ Or: ‘In that darkness, I begins to crawl.’ The narrative skips back and forth between ‘Underground Home, 1976’, ‘Home of the Radiator, 1998’, ‘Family’s Home, 2009’, ‘Home beneath the Mountain, 1983’ and so on. Some ‘homes’ are no more than a car or a phone box. The other characters are referred to by their relation to I: Father, Mother, Sister, Wife. So there are sentences like this: ‘When he slips off his shoes and turns on his computer screen, I moves into a place where Wife doesn’t exist.’ Painstakingly, through detailed descriptions of furnishings and even the floor plans of the various homes, the reader is invited to piece together a story of domestic unease and repression: a father afraid of the outside world but a tyrant in the family; a subservient mother whose only permitted contact with her parents (Relatives) is through occasional calls from a phone box; an older sister who tries to rebel but gets no support from I, whose only concern is to keep the peace.

Sometimes a paragraph moves away from the impersonal description of interiors: ‘Through Father’s decree, in fact, Relatives disappear for fairly long periods, sometimes months, sometimes years; in some cases, they disappear for ever. The word, “Relatives”, is abolished from the lexicon of the Home beneath the Mountain. Relatives no longer exist, and if Sister and I say that word, Father threatens to abolish them as well.’ One of I’s earliest companions is Turtle, from which he learns that when his father is on the warpath he can always ‘retreat into the carapace of his room … and stare up at the ceiling, like a turtle staring inside its own shell’. Since I will eventually become a writer, it doesn’t take much imagination to see the elaborate construction of *The Book of Homes* as a shell protecting him from childhood trauma.

*The Anniversary* tells the same story without the shell. The details in both novels – the protagonist’s birth in Rome, his family’s move to a village below the Alps in northern Piedmont, his university years in Turin, his decision to move to Paris, his position as a writer – correspond with Bajani’s biography. On the website of his Italian publisher he says that a few years ago he decided he would write no more unless compelled by an ‘irresistible urge’, setting aside ‘all falsehood, all decoration’. So, ‘without having planned it, one morning before dawn I began to write this head-on book.’ Renouncing the ‘demon of discipline’, he completed a first draft in twenty days. The novel would be ‘a showdown with myself’, since any other approach would mean ‘I wasn’t a free man.’

The anniversary of the title marks ten years since the narrator saw his family for the last time, after which he broke off contact with them, changing ‘phone numbers, houses, continents’. As a result, he has enjoyed ‘the best ten years of my life’. Freedom, it seems, has been achieved. Yet here he is waking in the small hours to write about them. The flaunted experimentalism of the earlier work is gone, but a more subtle narrative guile is at work. After declaring his satisfaction at having ‘erected an impregnable wall’ between himself and his parents, the narrator sets about evoking his mother. Until now he has never ‘given her much thought’; she lived so entirely in the shadow of his father as to seem insignificant. It was the father ‘who demanded centre stage and had authored, as it were, the sole version of the family mythology’.

Why then has he chosen to focus on her? Initially, the task is presented as a writerly challenge, the problem being to separate the mother from the dominant father – ‘a delicate operation’ requiring ‘a surgical disposition’, ‘patience and precision’. Facts and details accumulate, as though in a sociological report: her modest background ‘among the Roman petty bourgeoisie’, her attendance at a ‘prestigious high school’, the subjects she chose to study, her plans for the future. The narrator speculates on her parents’ ambitions for her, her own awareness of the career path before her. All accounts agree that ‘she was never any trouble.’

So far so good. As soon as we move into the realm of action, however, it becomes impossible to keep the father out of the story. Aged seventeen or eighteen, the narrator’s mother took ‘a large bedside alarm clock’ to her first date with his father for fear of being late. Later, she started a literature degree but had to drop out when he got her pregnant. Marvelling at his difficulty visualising her, the narrator seizes on a physical detail no one ever mentioned: a ‘leg that was slightly skinnier between the knee and ankle, a result of childhood polio’, which ‘gave her a faint limp’ and hence ‘violated [her] invisibility’. One of the clearest pictures of her comes when the narrator recalls his seaside holidays with his mother and sister, their father eventually joining them when work permitted. He remembers his mother’s reaction when the PA system at the beach bar would blare out her name to summon her to the phone:

> The image stands out both for the clumsiness of her movement and for the tenderness I felt on seeing that body, with its one thin leg, racing across the burning sand in a bathing suit, for everyone to see. She ran with urgency as her name kept resounding like a siren. Once each summer my father would call (usually to tell us he would arrive the following day), and she would instantly drop whatever she was doing and hurtle past the showers, past the stairs, toward the snack bar.

Any poignancy or affection accruing to the mother has to do with her subjugation to the father. The increasingly vain attempt to keep the focus on her is a measure of the narrator’s hostility towards his father, an act of revenge perhaps – refusing to grant him the importance he claimed. But it could also be construed as a strategy of avoidance. Why not confront the father directly? Perhaps because while his sister sought to rebel and ‘was pleased … whenever a crack appeared in the tyrant’s armour’, the narrator ‘always feared the worst’ and, in his sister’s description, kept the peace ‘with flattery, out of cowardice, legitimising [their father’s] undisputed power, and abandoning her, betraying her … in a battle she couldn’t fight alone’.

Using what he calls ‘the thinking machine’ of the novel, the narrator explores the way the relocation to Piedmont exacerbated the mother’s situation. After punching a man with dangerous friends, the father, fearing reprisals, moved the family to a remote area. He refused to install a phone so as to keep his name out of the directory. Isolated and perhaps lonely, the mother would make trips to a phone box where she used money from her meagre allowance to call her parents in Rome, which her husband saw as a betrayal. When she took a job at a supermarket, he perceived it as another threat to his hegemony. Under the influence of one of her few friends, the mother went to the house of the father’s mistress and left a note on his windscreen. The father reacted with inevitable fury. Later, when the mother’s friend died of a brain tumour, the father claimed he had ‘the power to inflict illness on people who had been, as he saw it, wicked’. In all these cases, the son, who enjoyed a special closeness to his father, strove to dissolve the tension while feeling guilty ‘of failing to stop the unstoppable’, since ‘surrendering myself to him in order to prevent detonations was my main job.’

As the picture builds up and the children become adults, Bajani’s skewed way of telling the story – striving to see the mother but only discovering the tyranny of the father – draws our attention to the narrator’s position with regard to the other characters. The drama of the story lies not so much in the events of the past – a fairly ordinary tale of domestic tyranny – as in the narrator’s difficulty confronting them. So the complacent tone of the opening pages disintegrates as we arrive at the truth of the narrator’s abject behaviour both as a young man and as an adult, his inability to conceive any behaviour outside the mediating role his parents have imposed on him. It’s a good moment to recall the book’s two epigraphs. The first is taken from Anne Carson: ‘If you are not the free person you want to be you must find a place to tell the truth about that. To tell how things go for you.’ The second comes from Louise Glück: ‘That’s why I’m not to be trusted./Because a wound to the heart is/also a wound to the mind.’

Three incidents reveal a gradual drift towards the story’s shameful core. ‘It’s still not clear to me whether my father actually hit my mother,’ one chapter begins. Now twentyish, the narrator has returned home from a day at university to find police in the living room, his mother with blood in her hair, shattered glass, broken furniture, a ‘scene … of devastation’. Nevertheless, when the police leave he asks no questions. Cleaning up with his mother, he feels ‘a kind of crazed tenderness, a peaceful defeat’. (Needless to say, it’s clear to the reader that his father did hit his mother.) Some years later, the narrator is living in Paris and receives a text message from his father saying that his mother has left for Rome without his permission. Immediately the boy has ‘abdominal cramps’ and a ‘skyrocketing pulse’. As the father fires off text after text describing the damage he is doing to the family home – kicking the television, even gnawing at the kitchen table – the son desperately makes conciliatory phone calls. But when, soon enough, the mother returns, he isn’t told. It’s three weeks before he finds ‘the courage to call’. Behind all this lurks the figure of his sister, about whom the narrator has surprisingly little to say.

Since the publication of *The Anniversary* in Italy last year, when it won the Strega Prize and quickly became a bestseller, Bajani has spoken in interviews about the political content of his story and its denunciation of the patriarchal Italian family. Such reassuring assertions, with their implication that action should be taken and things could change, are the false papers under which unsettling literature is often obliged to travel. The allure of the book, one of the finest Italian novels I have read in years, lies elsewhere. Towards the end, the narrator takes his wife-to-be to see his family, then fails to confront his father when he accuses the woman of taking his son away from him. As the couple drive home, she asks the narrator: ‘Who are those two people … And more importantly who are you?’

This is the question the novel addresses, and it is answered in the third climactic incident. The narrator is living in Turin, where he frequents pastry shops and pizzerias, with friendly staff whom ‘I had, in some sense, entrusted with representing my idea of family’. The owner of one of these businesses has pointed him to a therapist, an elderly woman, with whom he discusses his inability even to phone home without suffering stomach cramps, diarrhoea and sleepless nights. In one session the therapist insists he turn his phone on and deal with his father’s furious text messages. He calls his parents in front of her. ‘I don’t think I’ve ever made a more painful phone call in my life,’ we’re told. It’s not so much that he and his mother spoke ‘as if inside a refrigerator’; ‘what made it all unbearable was that gaze behind the desk.’ Afterwards, the therapist tells him she is ‘scandalised’. Only later does he recollect that during the conversation’s ‘fear-drenched small talk’ he had mentioned his fiancée’s forthcoming visit to an oncologist to try to win his father’s sympathy. The sense of shame induced by seeing this miserable ploy – the truth of who he is – through his therapist’s eyes clinches his decision to break for ever with his parents.

The remaining pages, all beautifully translated by Geoffrey Brock, seek to put some distance between this humiliating revelation and the narrator’s present existence as a writer in the US. They include an attempt to universalise private experience by reflecting on whether many expats are fleeing the constraints of their families. But by now the reader has realised that the narrator’s decision to cut off all contact with his family is a declaration of failure rather than of emancipation. It is the novel’s defeatism, its refusal to concede its characters the individuality that goes with a name, or to imagine that any of them could ever change, that makes it so disturbingly gripping. After the phone call from the therapist’s office, there is a final lunch at home, the ending of which, the mother’s farewell to her son at the door to the family apartment, is described on the first page of the novel. As on so many previous occasions, the narrator’s only thought was ‘self-defence’, ‘predicting and diverting my father’s potential incursions’. There is no point in confrontation. ‘Our only option was an endless, mechanical re-enactment of the same roles. The executioner, the victim, the cowardly son trying to mediate.’
