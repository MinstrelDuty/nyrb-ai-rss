# 正文

The Jasper Johns​ retrospective at Guggenheim Bilbao (until 12 October) includes nearly 140 works: more than fifty paintings, along with sculptures, drawings, prints, an artist’s book and a stage design. Working my way through the show, I tried to imagine Johns as the young man whose stab at formal training – three semesters studying art in the late 1940s and a brief stint at Parsons School of Design – was interrupted by the draft during the Korean War. After demobilisation he returned to New York and worked in a bookshop. If Johns lacked conventional training, he acquired something more valuable: an education in the company of painters, composers, choreographers and writers. He met Marcel Duchamp, Robert Rauschenberg, John Cage, Merce Cunningham and Frank O’Hara. He paid attention to their ways of thinking. His pictures do a lot of thinking too, but they are not illustrations of ideas.

Artists used to learn their craft through imitation, and one could learn a lot from copying Johns’s paintings: where the pictorial commas are; the way an image becomes an object; how an edge changes the meaning of a surface. They reveal almost everything about the way they are made, and almost nothing about how they are meant to be read. The lesson, perhaps, is that paintings teach us to look before they ask what we’ve seen.

It’s difficult to grasp how much smaller the art world was in the late 1950s or how vehemently the young artists of the time rejected their antecedents. Johns, who is now 96, emerged as an ironic challenger of Abstract Expressionism, with its studied machismo and doctrine of flatness. His playful riposte to Clement Greenberg’s demand that a painting should be nothing but paint on a flat surface led him to a repertoire of familiar images, which, as they began appearing in galleries, had the makings of an incremental programme for the future of American art. His American flag and other recognisable motifs – ‘factual, unimagined things in the world’, as the art historian Nan Rosenthal described them – seemed to suggest a continuum between Duchamp and Andy Warhol, while rejecting the aesthetics of gesture so important to the Abstract Expressionists and anticipating what would later become known as Pop Art.

The curator of the Bilbao show, Enrique Juncosa, has adopted a revisionist approach. His title, *Night Driver*, is taken from a small drawing of 1960 (not included in the exhibition), which Johns described as the first work he made in response to a personal feeling. We’re asked, in other words, to move beyond Johns’s curiosity about the formal properties of a painting and think instead about emotion and experience. In *Night Driver’*s smudged dark-on-dark layers of charcoal and pastel, a low horizontal division suggests a dashboard and the view beyond a windscreen. Johns said that the drawing recalled his first drive on a highway at night.

In 1954, Johns destroyed all his earlier work – an act of erasure that may have been prompted by his meeting and developing a working partnership with Rauschenberg. In 1953, Rauschenberg had approached Willem de Kooning, the most celebrated painter in New York, and asked him for a drawing to erase. De Kooning gave him a piece that was heavily worked in charcoal, oil paint, pencil and crayon: it took more than a month to obliterate. Johns framed the result and added a caption, *Erased De Kooning Drawing, Robert Rauschenberg, 1953*, establishing it as a work in its own right. Rauschenberg later described it as ‘an act of poetry’. Johns’s first flag painting was made between 1954 and 1955. It was done in encaustic, a technique that dates back to Roman Egypt, in which the pigments are mixed with hot wax. It was followed by his signature series of targets, further flags, maps and numerals (several series titled *0 through 9*), which announced his arrival on the American and then the international art scene.

The exhibition follows a loose chronology, interrupted here and there by the juxtaposition of later pieces with earlier ones to show the way Johns revisits and revises motifs across time and to point to the moments when more personal touches emerge, before he returns to formal, purist concerns. The curation largely sidesteps the pitfall of aggregating works by motif or theme. Johns’s paintings offer no ostensible clues as to their meaning, suggesting that they exist to instruct the viewer on what a painting is and how its elements might be negotiated: the way form is addressed; how ‘looking’ works.

*Drawer* (1957) pokes fun at the solemnity of Mark Rothko’s rectangles of fuzzy colour with a painted version of a drawer that functions as both a visual pun and a straightforward depiction of an object. A joke that’s told repeatedly (as Warhol understood) can make a serious point. Unlike the cynical one-liners of later conceptualism, Johns’s humour is contemplative and dry. A pair of orange balls are squeezed into a narrow horizontal ellipse two-thirds of the way up the canvas in *Painting with Two Balls* (1960) – at once a jab at the machismo of some of his contemporaries and a destabilising element, which seems to create a fissure in the canvas and wedge it open, proposing an optical reality behind the picture plane. The title of the piece is inscribed along its lower edge, as is Johns’s name, integral to the composition. Ed Ruscha’s brazen use of text is difficult to imagine without Johns’s appropriations of the written word, as are the deadpan, conceptual signature paintings of Richard Prince and Christopher Wool (a 1992 work by Wool has the stencilled legend ‘FUCKEM IF THEY CAN’T TAKE A JOKE’).

*In Memory of My Feelings* (1961) takes its title from a poem by O’Hara. The painting, which consists of two panels butted together, is done in gestural greys with a rectangular shape in the upper left-hand corner evoking the rectangle of stars in the American flag. A wire hangs down to the mid-point of the canvas; attached to the end are a spoon and fork. At the base of the canvas (again) is the title of the painting in stencil lettering. It is both anxious and melancholic. A recent infrared scan revealed not only an image of a skull under the surface but the words ‘Dead Man’. ‘In my early work I tried to hide my personality, my psychological state, my emotions,’ Johns said in 1984. ‘I sort of stuck to my guns for a while, but eventually it seemed a losing battle. Finally, one must simply drop the reserve.’ This is very much to the point for Juncosa’s curation.

Johns has always sought to position text in the realm of the image, detaching sign from signified as he requisitions it for the purposes of a painting. Perhaps as a consequence, he responds well to the challenge of reverse ekphrasis. Two rooms in Bilbao, dedicated to works on paper, include framed prints from *Foirades/Fizzles* (1976), his collaboration with Samuel Beckett; 34 prints by Johns are paired with five short prose texts by Beckett. In the catalogue, Juncosa notes an affinity between the two men in their ‘treatment of language as the principal means of expression in their respective disciplines’.

Since the mid-1980s Johns’s work has turned increasingly to visual quotation: other images – the *Mona Lisa*, still lifes, posters, warning signs – are collaged, or painted as though collaged, on the surface of his canvases; the imagery becomes eclectic without losing its immediacy. What he’s willing to divulge in these pieces and what he can’t (or won’t) disclose achieve a satisfying tension. In *The Bath* (1988) and two works called *Untitled* (1991-94 and 1992-94), he reproduces imagery from Matthias Grünewald’s *Isenheim Altarpiece*. The altarpiece was thought to help people suffering from ergot poisoning, or St Anthony’s fire, which causes severe hallucinations, gangrene and often death. Johns started to make these works as the Aids epidemic swept through New York in the 1980s, though nothing in the paintings addresses Aids directly.

Johns is notoriously reticent in interviews, preferring to talk about the way his work is made rather than its ‘meaning’. He has had an extraordinary influence on other artists. For a period in the late 1950s and early 1960s he made casts of objects – flashlights, flags, lightbulbs – for sculptures in bronze, silver and aluminium. It was a brief interlude, but it cast a long shadow over the generation of artists who followed, including Robert Gober, Jeff Koons and Sherrie Levine. In 1970, the minimalist painter Brice Marden painted a monumental canvas, *Three Deliberate Greys for Jasper Johns*, and called Johns to ask him if it was OK to use that title. Johns replied: ‘Sure, as long as the greys are deliberate.’
