# 正文

For a long time​
, people have been telling me to read Laurie Colwin – not friends so much as novelists like Jonathan Lethem, Brandon Taylor, Nick Hornby and Ann Patchett, who mention Colwin when asked to name an ‘overlooked or unappreciated writer’ or one they’d invite to an imaginary dinner party. And then there are all those Substack and Instagram lists suggesting ‘What to Read When It All Feels Too Much’.

Colwin’s three collections of short stories, five novels and two books of culinary essays all have their advocates. But for those whose need of ‘small quiet treats’ or temporary residence in ‘a world of little details and luxuries’ is particularly acute, the Colwinites recommend her second novel, *Happy All the Time* (1978). It’s available as an audiobook and as an ebook, but only the new Weidenfeld & Nicolson paperback edition welcomes readers with a cover image of a double bed made up with a red quilt and four fluffed-up pillows. This is one of a series of reprints featuring vibrant gouaches by Maira Kalman, a friend of Colwin’s. Two depict flower arrangements, while others hint more directly at human presence: a pair of party slippers, a tablescape including shards of a broken plate, a swimmer at full stretch. These don’t give much away, but on the cover of *Family Happiness* (1982) an unsmiling woman rests her ponderous head on a slab of arm. If image and title seem at odds, then perhaps so too are family and happiness. The intended effect is less sardonic than quizzical.

Colwin was born in 1944 in Manhattan and grew up in the upper-middle-class suburbs of Long Island, Chicago and Philadelphia. Her father, Peter, was a director of the United Jewish Appeal and her mother, Estelle, worked as an art consultant while running a home that Colwin described as ‘even more bourgeois’ than her own (and ‘I’m the most bourgeois person in the world’). The difference was generational. Estelle’s era was one of ‘big old houses’ stuffed with ‘Persian rugs, English silver, Limoges plates and Meissen soup tureens’; her daughter’s that of studio apartments which didn’t necessarily have a sink but whose refrigerators were stocked with organic coffee beans and whose shelves displayed an extensive collection of flea-market cookie cutters and a mishmash of ‘cups that bore forget-me-nots, a lily-of-the-valley sugar dish, a cream pitcher with red poppies and a teapot covered with red roses and cornflowers’.

Bourgeois tastes in food also changed across the generations, as *Family Happiness* explores at length. The heroine, Polly Solo-Miller Demarest, is the product of a merger between two ‘old, old Jewish families, the sort that are more identifiably American than Jewish’, and now she’s married into a third. As her name suggests, no family connection or tradition is ever subsumed by another; they simply accumulate. Every week, the ‘tribe’ meets for breakfast, and every week Polly’s father voices his fears about the salmon (too much or too little smoking can be deadly) while her mother ensures that the silver bowls are always filled with perfectly sharp ‘toast points’. In her own home, but only when her husband is away, Polly and the kids prefer ‘nursery food’: ‘shepherd’s pie, mashed potatoes, devilled chicken, vegetable fritters, hush puppies, Queen of Puddings and apple crisp’. Trysts with her lover, meanwhile, start with ‘bread, cheese, butter, a bunch of grapes, a bottle of red wine and coffee’. It’s a quickly assembled lunch, but worth pausing for: he always ensures that the bread is fresh. Other lovers are less adept. In Colwin’s linked story collection, *Another Marvellous Thing* (1986), a man whose wife knows ‘how to serve foie gras’ wonders why his mistress can’t produce ‘an interesting postcoital snack’. He compares her to a ham sandwich ‘without butter or mayonnaise or mustard’ – that is, ‘unadornedly what it was’. Sounds good. And yet he can’t suppress the urge to pass on his wife’s recipes.

‘What people eat and how they serve it’ fascinated Colwin, and not only because (as she once noted) she’d learned about the cultural significance of ‘daily things’ in Anthropology 101. Her sharp and nuanced descriptions of eating, preparing and shopping for meals are psychologically and socially revealing, but remarks like ‘an éclair or two promotes clear thinking’ are also their own reward. It’s not clear whether Colwin asked to write for *Gourmet* (as its editor claimed) or whether, as she herself told it, ‘the magazine of good living’ approached her through Judith Jones (the legendary Knopf editor of Julia Child, Edna Lewis and many others). In Colwin’s version of the story, she was initially uncertain about the gig but then decided that readers might enjoy a break from the magazine’s ‘high fashion food’ to consider fried chicken: ‘It’s so delicious and so many people cook it horribly.’ In any case, the article ‘just fell out of the typewriter’ and she never looked back. *Home Cooking* (1988) and *More Home Cooking* (1993) are collections of the pieces Colwin published in *Gourmet* between 1985 and 1992, when, at just 48, she died suddenly from an aortic aneurysm.

Colwin once said that her career as ‘a feeder of people’ was secured in 1968 during the Columbia University sit-in. When the Students for a Democratic Society complained about her tuna and peanut butter sandwiches, she told them they should be eating paving stones like their comrades in Paris. They responded by pasting a badge on her sweatshirt: ‘KITCHEN/COLWIN’. It was the kind of label that many women of her generation were noisily rejecting. The unpaid labour of housework was a major theme of second-wave feminism, with numerous essays declaring ‘the housewife’s moment of truth’ (Jane O’Reilly) or recommending that every woman should find a ‘wife who will plan the menus, do the necessary grocery shopping, prepare the meals, serve them pleasantly and then do the cleaning up’ while she got on with her ‘studying’ (Judy Syfers).

Colwin published her first short story in the *New Yorker* in 1969 and many of her early pieces pay their dues to the mythopoetic Cheever-esque mode. A few, though, seem to take on the task of consciousness-raising. ‘A Road in Indiana’, which appeared in *Cosmopolitan* in 1972, is the tale of Patricia, once ‘bouncy’ and ‘flip’ but now married to Richard, the first in a long line of academic prigs in Colwin’s work. Richard leaves notes to inform his wife that he’ll be late home due to ‘fac. meeting’ – ‘fridge filthy, I might add.’ His idea of a pleasant evening is to listen to Stravinsky over dinner, then spend an hour or two working on his novel, ‘Pain in its Simplicity’. The story ends predictably enough with Patricia hitting the highway, but Colwin also seems to be playing with the already solidifying formulae of feminist fiction. For a start, Richard’s diagnosis of Patricia’s problem as ‘childish ennui’ suggests that he may have read Betty Friedan’s *The Feminine Mystique* (1963), the book that popularised the idea of the housewife’s ‘bored, diffuse feeling of purposelessness, non-existence, non-involvement with the world’. And like Friedan, Richard believes that education is ‘the key to the trap’. If the joke lies partly in his too-apt reading recommendation (*Bleak House*), it is also directed at the more general assumption that escape from the marital kitchen means returning to the dorm or at least the typewriter. In Marilyn French’s *The Women’s Room* (1977), Mira Ward leaves her husband to embark on a doctorate in English literature at Harvard, while Erica Jong’s *Fear of Flying* (1973), for all its ‘zipless fucks’, ends with Isadora Wing’s dream of receiving her PhD diploma (from Colette, no less) and a renewed commitment to ‘go on working’. Patricia’s ambitions are much less strenuous; she just wants some appreciation for her carrot pudding and to be able to listen to her favourite country songs without being told off.

One response to what Betty Fussell called ‘the drudge problem’ was to suggest that cooking was not housework but ‘an art, or at the very least a craft’. This was the argument that Jane Grigson made in *Good Things* (1971), a literate and scholarly guide to ‘enjoying food’ that became one of Colwin’s touchstones. Grigson begins by challenging ‘intelligent housewives’ who feel ‘they’ve a duty to be bored by domesticity.’ While that might be ‘a fair reaction to dusting and bed-making’, cookery, she insists, is quite different: a form of ‘civilisation’ to be ‘studied’ as well as practised. Grigson’s version of carrot pudding was retrieved from an 18th-century recipe book by Hannah Glasse.

Writing more than a decade later, Colwin shared Grigson’s emphasis on local, seasonal and organic produce, but was less concerned with presenting ‘work in the guise of leisure’ (as Fussell wryly puts it). Her essays acknowledge the labour involved in shopping and cooking, insist that she has no desire to bring back the ‘depressed, bored, non-working housewife of the 1950s’, and emphasise the importance of getting boys into the kitchen so that ‘as adults our daughters do not end up working to a frazzle while our sons sit around reading the newspaper’. But she also reminds readers that roasting a chicken, baking a potato or making lentil soup need not be ‘a big production’, and that, whatever else is going on, ‘people can be cheered up by a nice meal.’

In essays with titles such as ‘The Same Old Thing’ and ‘How to Face the Holidays’, Colwin won the affection of readers for a style that is warm, unintimidating and a little self-revealing. That voice has been hugely influential, as many recent food writers fulsomely testify. We hear its echo in Bee Wilson’s emphasis on ‘an easier life in the kitchen’; in Alison Roman’s championing of ‘nothing fancy’; and in Samin Nosrat’s embrace of imperfection (‘You’re not always going to have the very best ingredients … It doesn’t matter. No one will remember’). Nosrat’s podcast (with Hrishikesh Hirway) is even called *Home Cooking*. Colwin’s characterisation of herself as a ‘domestic sensualist’, and her insistence on her own greed (when one of her puddings is rejected, she eats it ‘all by myself in the kitchen without a trace of guilt, because I did all the work’), was also important for Nigella Lawson. Lawson wrote *How to Eat* (1998), about ‘enjoying life on purpose rather than by default’, before encountering Colwin; if she had, she once said, ‘I’m not sure I’d have bothered.’ But two years later, in the exuberantly camped-up *How to Be a Domestic Goddess* (‘trailing nutmeggy fumes of baking pie in our languorous wake’), she describes *Home Cooking* as one of her ‘favourite books’ and includes a version of its recipe for black cake. More recently Ruby Tandoh has credited Colwin with showing her that ‘writing about food was always about so much more than just the food itself.’

Colwin’s women usually have jobs outside the home – as researchers, book designers, caterers – though few of them seem to rely on their salaries. In *Family Happiness*, Polly complains that her husband (a harassed lawyer) doesn’t ask her about her job in literacy education, but the reader isn’t told much about it either. Colwin is more expansive about work in her posthumously published final novel, *A Big Storm Knocked It Over* (1993), which describes the publishing world in which she herself was employed for many years. She had some pleasures and successes: assisting Isaac Bashevis Singer on some of his most famous stories (‘essentially editorial work’, she later explained, ‘for which Mr Singer was kind enough to give a translator’s credit’), persuading E.P. Dutton to publish Fran Lebowitz’s first book, *Metropolitan Life*, and Deborah Eisenberg (who was working as a waitress at her local diner) to let her pass on some stories to an editor at Knopf. But she quit or was fired from most jobs. Colwin’s husband, the publisher Juris Jurjevics, described her as ‘a big believer in revenge’, and *A Big Storm Knocked It Over* makes satirical sport of a range of publishing types: Sven, ‘compact and well made, like a good canoe’, and addicted to the kind of behaviour that HR would now call sexual harassment; Dita, dressed in ‘the sort of clothes girls might have worn in French convent schools in the 1940s’ and forever ‘trailing cigarette smoke’; and the boss, Erna, who seems to be ‘in training to be an empress’.

What she liked best, however, was ‘hanging around the house’ (her friend Willard Spiegelman described her as ‘an incipient agoraphobic’). In *Goodbye without Leaving* (1990), Geraldine Coleshares worries that she’s ‘one of those meandering types’ with nothing ‘to show’ for her life. When Leo Rhinehart asks, ‘Are you supposed to have something to show for things?’ we know they’ll end up in bed. Lack of productivity is less of a problem in *Happy All the Time*, but Holly Sturgis nevertheless feels obliged to inform her suitor Guido Morris that ‘I don’t work. I’m lazy. I don’t do anything very important … I just live day to day enjoying myself.’ Yes, reader, he married her; but the fantasy which draws readers to these novels is that of meandering rather than romance.

To some extent Geraldine’s concerns seem to be Colwin’s, though no one would think ten books a paltry showing for such a short life. Jurjevics explained that because Colwin ‘thought herself a lazy person’ she was therefore ‘very disciplined’, at least in intense bursts: she liked to see how many pages she could type in twenty minutes. A few months before she died, Colwin called her editor at *Gourmet* to say that she’d completed all her columns for the year ahead and asked: ‘Is it OK if I send them in now?’ This seemingly businesslike approach is perhaps why Colwin’s fiction is often discussed as just another form of everyday cooking, ‘literary chicken soup’ or ‘comfort prose’, if at times ‘as airily pleasing as a perfectly executed soufflé’. These comparisons capture something of her appeal, but they don’t do justice to her skill as a writer or to the philosophical bent of her inquiries into the nature and challenges of happiness.

That perennial concern of American fiction had taken on a new urgency during the 1970s, when, as Margaret Atwood noted, ‘freedom’ seemed to have turned into ‘free fall’. Atwood was reviewing a book by Ann Beattie, a neighbour of Colwin’s on West 20th Street and in the pages of the *New Yorker*. Surveys, self-help manuals and fiction about the well-heeled all turned to the question that opens Beattie’s 1975 story ‘Dwarf House’: ‘Are you happy?’

In trying to answer the question, Colwin is both less nihilistic and less sentimental than Beattie. If she ‘had opinions the way rabbits have bunnies’ (as her friend Jonathan Yardley wrote after her death), she also believed that the point of writing was to test them. Her ‘particular bugbear in life’, she once explained, was ‘the fixed idea’. Colwin’s investigations into the varieties of and obstacles to happiness often have a literary starting point (in books as well as food, she was an Anglophile). ‘My Mistress’, for example, is a prose take on Shakespeare’s Sonnet 130. In ‘The Smile beneath the Smile’, the title of which is surely a play on T.S. Eliot’s ‘skull beneath the skin’, the narrator makes the Eliotic point that ‘love has nothing to do with personality. It has to do with form.’ Colwin’s fiction relies on, and riffs off, many different forms, from the medieval tale of courtly love and the Tudor lyric to the 19th-century novel of adultery and the satirical comedies of Evelyn Waugh, whom she once described as her ‘mentor’. But the forms in which Colwin’s heroines understand love are as likely to come from mid-1960s Motown. In *Shine on, Bright and Dangerous Object* (1975), Olly Bax wonders whether she would have fallen in love with her husband if ‘He’s a Rebel’ by the Crystals hadn’t been playing ‘on the college bar’s jukebox for two years’. The first thing we learn about Geraldine in *Goodbye without Leaving* is that she’s abandoned her doctoral dissertation on ‘Jane Austen and the War of the Sexes’ to become a backing singer for Ruby Shakely and the Shakettes.

Austen is the author with whom Colwin is most often compared, largely because both favour free indirect style and are interested in permutations of character and situation. But there are important differences. Austen’s conceptual pairings (sense versus sensibility, say) are usually presented through the marriage plots of contrasting siblings. For Colwin, however, marriage marks the initiation, not the culmination, of the ‘long ballet’ of ‘temperaments … at variance’. At times, marriage simply seems a new ‘arrangement of things’ – a husband integrated into the home like any other ‘household object’ – but it often provokes existential crisis. How to face the chasm between ‘your old self’, the one that ‘pines and broods and reflects’, and the promised ‘fulfilment’ of the new self? Various options are considered: getting and staying stoned, secret ‘music worship’, swimming, having a baby. It’s not a problem for men, however. Most are like Vincent and Guido in *Happy All the Time,* charming ‘saps’ who hold on to their ‘debutante fantasies’ about the triumph of love while their partners – Holly, a self-sufficient ‘city-state’, and Misty, who ‘can’t stand to do anything without a fight’ – struggle with ‘love, dread, confusion and certainty’. The novel ends with the two couples dining together by an idyllic New England beach. They are ‘suddenly sad’ when the champagne runs out, but another bottle is close at hand. Misty suggests they toast ‘a truly wonderful life’, and, repeating the phrase, the last line reads: ‘They raised their glasses and, by the light of the candles, they drank to a truly wonderful life.’ Perhaps such a life is possible, but this disconcerting echo and the rapid shifts of mood leading up to it don’t inspire confidence. Earlier in the book, Misty had interrupted a similar toast to point out that the 20th century was ‘hardly the great age of happy futures’.

Perhaps being ‘in a cheerfully anxious state’ is the best you can hope for, especially if, like Misty Berkowitz, you’re ‘the only Jew at the dinner table’. Many of Colwin’s protagonists find themselves interlopers in Wasp territory: appealing in some ways (rambling houses with forty-year-old rhubarb patches) but appalling in others (‘a tip on the stock market’ counts as an expression of affection). They worry that their husbands might only find them ‘sociologically interesting’ and eventually run off with ‘some cheerful Christian’ who ‘dreams of little Brownies and Cub Scouts running all over the place’. On the whole, however, the chilly New Englanders are delighted when ‘the only Jew’ rides to the rescue with her ‘nice, warm’ domestic habits. But not always. Take Cordy Spaacks, the protagonist of ‘The Boyish Lover’. A physics professor with a trust fund and an eye for ‘a free meal when he could find one’, Cordy meets Jane Mayer at a humanities department tea party. He’s impressed by her capacity to gulp down seven sandwiches; she finds him ‘winsome’. And so it – by which I mean sex, but also goose-down pillows, lavender soap and the perfect omelette – begins. Jane believes that ‘fate had handed her the perfect other,’ there being ‘no greater magnet for a generous woman than a deprived man’, but in the end she figures out that deprivation (including of her) is what he likes best.

The difference runs deeper than that. An insistence on comfort and pleasure is a recognition that the roof might ‘cave in’ at any moment: only those, like Cordy, with a long-term sense of security can afford the luxury of ‘life-denying’ behaviour. In *Happy All the Time*, when Misty declares herself the product of ‘a family that fled the tsar’s army, got their heads broken on picket lines and has never slept peacefully anywhere’, Vincent points out that she herself slept peacefully in Chicago, where her mother was ‘brought up at the Art Institute’. ‘It’s cultural,’ she retorts, and the matter is dropped. But it’s an idea that Colwin keeps coming back to. In *Home Cooking*, she ends a piece on Friday night dinner by explaining that it’s about celebrating ‘good fortune’ while you can, a sentiment that’s echoed in *Goodbye without Leaving* by Hannah Hausknecht, a ‘plump and happy’ Auschwitz survivor. As a child in the camp, she had made up songs about chocolate cakes and sugar roses; now here she is in New York eating ‘all the things we dreamed about’.

The food is always described in detail, but the sex – of which there is also plenty – is not. One woman makes jokes about ‘rapturous consummation’ and insists that there be ‘no hanky-panky before breakfast’; another sits ‘eating her duck and thinking about sex’; they all ‘hurl’ themselves at their lovers. Colwin doesn’t spend much time describing either husbands or lovers; each fulfils his generic role. The purpose of the lover is to offer a particular kind of pleasure (‘situational adoration’) but also respite from the husband. The women just need a few moments ‘out of reality’, a holiday in ‘a tiny, private kingdom in some remote country’.

The privacy is as important as the adoration, if not more. In Colwin’s novels the ‘daily life’ of marriage and family is a public ‘edifice’ of assumptions and obligations, a space in which everyone can be quickly defined and classified (by their relationships, relatives, jobs – even ‘by their ornaments’). In *Family Happiness*, Polly’s identity is quickly summed up: ‘a good listener’; ‘her mother’s favourite lunch companion and a great social asset’; a ‘perfect flower’; ‘always happy to provide something scrumptious for dessert’. Her lover’s studio is the only place in which she is able to step away from these roles and find herself understood ‘from a private angle’. As in the Bildungsroman tradition, a love affair offers ‘an education in yearning’ designed to enable ‘change and growth and enlightenment’. In *Goodbye without Leaving*, Geraldine even chooses as her lover a man called Rhinehart (that is, ‘strong adviser’). ‘He would kiss me,’ she thinks, ‘and I would turn into Hannah Arendt.’ This kind of thing is all very well, for a while. But it can start to feel ‘banal’ and clichéd (like ‘something in *One Hundred Standard Plots*’) and, outside the Bildungsroman, who can endure schooling, havoc or ‘the kind of happiness that is devoid of any contentment’ for very long? Marriage has its advantages too: order, security, sociability, ‘quietude’. Colwin’s heroines are often unable or unwilling to choose between one existential condition and another. They want to have their cake and eat it.

*Family Happiness* takes its title and many of its preoccupations from a Tolstoy novella about a young woman’s education in different forms of happiness: whether that’s living for oneself, a lover, a child or God; in the excitement of society or the tranquillity of seclusion; with joy, passion, cheerfulness or contentment. Both stories end with the wife’s commitment to family happiness, but also a residual uncertainty about what that might entail. Colwin’s final scene has the Solo-Miller Demarests flying kites. It’s a bit like *Mary Poppins*, but here only the father and the children have kites; Polly, who hasn’t quite given up her lover, remains grounded by ‘the complexity of things’. As a character in another story puts it, ‘after a certain age, no joy is unmitigated.’

The push-pull of adultery and marriage was a theme Colwin often returned to, though the stories written after 1983, when she herself married and had a child, come down more decidedly on the side of family. And yet she kept worrying at the subject. In *Another Marvellous Thing*, which (unusually) includes a married man’s point of view, Francis resorts to proliferating metaphors to understand what’s going on. He thinks of his affair as a French movie (‘it went by frame by frame’), a piece of music ‘replayed over and over again’, and ‘in architectural terms’ as ‘a folly or gazebo or some small chapel done in California Spanish Gothic’. In short, it’s an ‘amazing product of human ingeniousness’ but one that ultimately can’t compete with family, that ‘mighty oak’, ‘trench dug by time’ or ‘kitchen midden’ containing ‘layer upon layer of the things daily life is made of’.

Colwin’s books are mostly variations on this debate (which is why it’s a good idea to pause between them), but at times the struggles of love are rather a displacement of ‘longings which can only be called religious’. Describing herself as having ‘many feelings about God but with no particular vessel to put them in’, Colwin was drawn to those ‘lucky’ but quite alien people who had ‘a fixed course of life, of belief’. Towards the end of her life she took some Hebrew classes, but she remained a ‘watered-down Jew’ and, according to her child, R.F. Jurjevics, ‘it was Catholicism that particularly intrigued her.’ ‘Pilgrim’ is the word that the nun-to-be Mary Abbott uses to describe her friend Geraldine in *Goodbye without Leaving*. ‘On a quest’ for something that would put her ‘heart at rest’, Geraldine, the daughter of ‘relentlessly assimilated’ Jews, looks for instruction in Black music (singing with the Shakettes, researching the lives of female blues singers) and in conversations with Holocaust survivors. She realises, however, that the experiences she’s drawn to are all other people’s. Only when she has a child is her own ‘true purpose – or at least a part of it’ – revealed; the other part is deciding to sing again. It’s the kind of ‘ducky’ ending that Misty would have dismissed as pure wish-fulfilment.

A more candid take on the same themes can be found in ‘Evensong’, a story Jurjevics discovered in their mother’s papers and which the *New Yorker* published in 2023. A woman has an affair with a man who takes her to services at the nearby Anglican seminary. Just as the affair makes her freshly alert to her ‘very own’ husband, so her enjoyment of Christian liturgy becomes the first step towards revisiting her ‘Jewish heritage’. It’s a parable about the way to avoid ‘encroaching chaos’ and yet embrace ‘complexity’, on how to accept that you can never ‘know everything all at once’.
