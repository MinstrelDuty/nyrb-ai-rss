# 正文

The saying​ ‘whoever speaks Arabic is an Arab’ has had a remarkably long life. It has been attributed to Muhammad, and circulated widely among Muslim jurists and scholars in the first millennium. Voiced by pan-Arab nationalists in the 20th century, it seemed to promise a resolution of the competing ethnic, sectarian and regional loyalties in the modern Middle East. The simplicity of the statement – that language can form the basis for solidarity – belies a much more complicated reality. A language needs no territory or bonds of kinship, only speakers. But what is a people without a place?

The question of Arab identity is not, of course, the invention of modern nationalism, or of bureaucrats tasked with classifying local populations or policing borders. As an ethnonym, the word ‘Arab’ has a long and contested history. The historian Robert Hoyland has shown that the term is first attested not as a description of a shared identity claimed by all the inhabitants of the Arabian Peninsula, but as a label used by the Assyrian Empire to categorise desert tribal populations on its south-western frontiers. In this period, Arabia was linguistically fragmented, with multiple languages that would in time give way to something resembling classical Arabic. Later uses of the word as a term of self-identification have been found in ancient rock graffiti in North Arabia, made in the Safaitic script well before the rise of Islam. Here, rather than designating a vast commonwealth or a shared ethnic consciousness, ‘Arab’ was used as an umbrella category for various nomadic groups. Even in these scattered basalt inscriptions found in the desert, it’s far from clear what combination of language, culture and descent the word encompassed.

In the centuries that followed the rise of Islam, a term that had meant different things to imperial administrators and nomads came to carry universal force. With the new formations of religion and state, Arab and Muslim became increasingly intertwined. Yet their equation was a point of contestation, not a social fact, and it didn’t happen quickly. Scholars today are much more likely to view the history of Muslim conversion as a process of slow acculturation than as being ‘spread by the sword’, as the Orientalist cliché has it. Local populations didn’t become Muslim overnight; nor did they suddenly start speaking Arabic. This is not to soften the impact of the Arab conquests of the seventh century, which saw Muslim armies sweep out of the Arabian Peninsula, bring an end to Roman rule over Syria, Egypt and North Africa, and force the collapse of the Persian Empire. Nonetheless, by the time the Mongol warlord Hülegü marched on Aleppo in the middle of the 13th century, the situation had changed dramatically. Arabic was becoming the spoken language of rural populations across North Africa and the Middle East whose relationship to Arab identity was still being negotiated.

The identification of true believers with Arab identity was a deliberate undertaking, intended to assert the sacred status of Arabic and the hegemony of Arabs. The Damascene legal reformist Ibn Taymiyya used the saying ‘anyone who speaks Arabic is an Arab’ in a still influential treatise written not long after the Mongol conquests. In it, he warns Muslims against imitating the religious practices of others. Arabic, he argues, is not just the medium of the Quran as divine revelation, but the voice of Sunni orthodoxy. As the first bearers of Islam, Arabs play a pivotal role in Ibn Taymiyya’s vision of sacred history. But rather than restrict himself to genealogy or to the Arabian Peninsula, he addresses the reality of his period: most Muslims who speak Arabic don’t know whether or not they are of Arabian lineage. Speaking Arabic is a key part of what makes them Arab; being Muslim is another.

These are the historical forces that Yossef Rapoport seeks to explain in *Becoming Arab*. His focus is on social transformation in Egypt, Palestine and Greater Syria over the five hundred years from the age of the Fatimid caliphs in the tenth and eleventh centuries through the Mamluk sultanate to the Ottoman conquest of Cairo in 1517. In exploring the way Arab identity was actively created and shaped, he takes for granted the broader historical context of the change from an ancient ethnonym to a modern nationalist catch-all. Ibn Taymiyya’s observation that most Muslims who speak Arabic have no knowledge of their ancestry isn’t mentioned, though it would help Rapoport’s argument.

Rapoport trains his attention on a single question: why did the rural inhabitants of these regions come to identify as Arabs? Language, pottery, clothing and kinship are the primary indices of the change. He doesn’t entirely ignore the encyclopedists and administrators of the Mamluk state – ‘Umari, Qalqashandi, Maqrizi and their like are all accounted for. But *Becoming Arab* moves beyond the literary record that has privileged an urban elite, beyond the framing determined by jurists, bureaucrats and secretaries, whose volumes memorialising their own interests have often set the scope of historical inquiry. Instead, we learn what farmers, labourers and chieftains gained by asserting an Arab identity.

Church Fathers in Antioch and Alexandria claimed that the pre-Muslim population was awash with unruly Christians, but what exactly the rural, unlettered masses believed before and during the Arab conquests is unknown, at least in any clear doctrinal or sectarian sense. Whatever their earlier practices, their gradual conversion to Islam coincided with the adoption of Arab identity, with each reinforcing the other. For Rapoport, the spread of Arabic, which paralleled the slow retreat of Aramaic in Palestine and Syria and of Coptic in Egypt, served as both catalyst and testament to these changes.

There was, of course, more than one Arabic. The Arabic dialects spoken in rural Egypt, Palestine and Syria between the 11th and 15th centuries are largely beyond the reach of historical linguistics. Yet one phonological marker has survived, and Rapoport follows it attentively. Medieval Arabic philologists documented that certain Arab tribes pronounced the letter *qaf*, the *q* sound of classical Arabic, as a hard *g* – the word *daqiq*, ‘flour’, thus sounded like *dagig*. This became known as the ‘*qaf* of the Arabs’, and is still audible in many Arabic dialects today. The 14th-century historian Ibn Khaldun called this phoneme a ‘distinguishing sign’ that separated tribal Arabs from ‘all other peoples and generations’. Those who wished to root themselves in Arabian ancestry, he concluded, imitate the Arab *qaf* as a marker of *‘urubiyya*, ‘Arabness’. Among the rural populations of Egypt and the Levant this form of Arabic became a shibboleth. It was made use of by the Mamluk authorities when suppressing an Arab revolt in Egypt at the beginning of the 14th century. Suspected insurrectionists were ordered to pronounce *daqiq*; those who did so in the Arab style were executed. Rapoport tells these stories not just for their historical detail, but also as emblems of the deeper social tensions between the urban elite and the local peasants.

As their name implies, the Mamluks (the word means ‘owned’) were originally slave soldiers, predominantly Qipchak Turks, taken from the Eurasian steppes and converted to Islam under the Ayyubid sultanate. The Ayyubids were a Kurdish dynasty founded by Saladin, who captured Jerusalem from the Crusaders and whose ancestors came from the highlands of Armenia. In 1250, the Mamluks overthrew their masters, conquering the majority of the Levant and Egypt. The political economies of both the Ayyubid and Mamluk states rested on the systematic extraction of agricultural surpluses through taxes on peasants. The affirmation of an Arab identity, inseparable from Islam, came to serve as a wedge against the Turkish-speaking soldiers who ruled from the cities. Yet Arab chieftains and their militias sustained the Mamluks’ system of fiscal exploitation; tributes passed through them from village to citadel. Why did a small group of well-armed foreigners get away with subjugating and exploiting the local population? And why did the local headmen, who claimed to be masters of the land and who had precedence in religion and culture, submit to rulers who had only a few generations before been uncivilised infidels, ‘stained by bondage’?

One answer is that, as landlords, the Arab chieftains were more than tax collectors on horseback. Their protection ensured the obedience and loyalty of the rural populations they governed; they dispensed swift justice and maintained bonds of social cohesion. Capable of mobilising cavalry for campaigns and of helping to pacify uprisings, they could also turn the threat of violence against the state. The local farmers fell in line with the Mamluks and their Arab headmen. Rapoport argues that by adopting Arab tribal identity, peasants gained a great deal. They cemented the solidarity of the village clan; claimed a measure of religious prestige, given their relatively recent conversion; and aligned themselves culturally and genealogically with the Arab headmen.

The ideology that bound farmers to their local masters was both genealogical and poetic. A novel form of Arabic chivalric tale emerged during this period, featuring an Arab hero and his band of tribal companions who were embodiments of generosity, valour and steadfastness. These lengthy compositions, which were written in a middle register between the literary Arabic of the urban elite and the spoken dialects of village markets, were recited and memorised, and served to strengthen a feeling of collective belonging in Arab villages. Rapoport writes that the epic cycles describing the exploits of warriors like ‘Antar and al-Battal also supplied rural communities with a code of conduct for clan-based life.

These poems celebrated a caste of noble Bedouins from the deserts of Arabia. But the oasis tracts of the Fayyum, the villages of the Nile Delta, the agricultural plains that stretched from Gaza to the Jordan Valley, and the farming communities of Lebanon and Syria were populated almost entirely by sedentary cultivators. Using an uneven collection of archival materials – tribal marriage contracts, land tax registers, provincial court records, miniature paintings – Rapoport argues that Arab tribal identity in these lands was not a primordial inheritance, but was asserted by clan leaders, reinforced by urban administrators and gradually absorbed by the peasant farmers they sought to govern.

Rapoport joins a small but growing cohort of historians, among them Ramzi Rouighi on the Berbers, Boris James on the Kurds and Wissam Halawi on the Druze, who are expanding the social history of the Middle East. The result has been a more detailed understanding of non-elite communities that have been excluded from the usual narratives of conquest and dynasty. But despite Rapoport’s creative use of sources, the subject of *Becoming Arab*, the indigenous farmer, remains obscure. The village farmers at the heart of this book almost never speak: they are objects of governance, names on tax rolls and parties in legal transactions managed by the bureaucratic apparatus that created what survives of the archive. This silence is perhaps inevitable. At the same time, Rapoport’s focus on identity may not help him to discern these people’s economic and social interests: their daily lives were probably less concerned with culture, language or self-fashioning than with the control of land and labour, and the legal standing that protected both of these things.

These are not merely academic questions. Dispossession and displacement have defined much of the modern Middle East, and the history of who counts as Arab, and on what grounds, is by no means a settled or innocent matter. It was debated throughout the long period of Ottoman rule, and in the 19th and early 20th centuries, with the rise of European colonialism and the weakening of Ottoman power, urban elites in Cairo, Beirut and Baghdad extended the label of Arab identity even further. But the map of the Arab world as charted by the Arab League can’t paper over the complexity of the situation. Formed in Cairo in 1945 with seven founding members – Egypt, Iraq, Jordan, Lebanon, Saudi Arabia, Syria and Yemen – the league today comprises 22 nations. Its boundaries sweep from Morocco on the Atlantic coast to the Gulf. The unity suggested by the map has been elusive. However hard the 20th-century advocates of pan-Arabism tried to yoke these disparate territories into a coherent whole, the promise of a unified Arab polity remained distant.

The boundaries of the modern map reflect the rigid geometry of colonial statecraft: the borders were set by successive negotiations with the European powers who had overrun the Middle East. With a view towards building strategic alliances, the Arab League expanded into a geographical impossibility – or at least its membership came to stretch beyond countries that had an Arab identity. Somalia and Djibouti, both former colonies, joined in the 1970s; the most recent member of the alliance, joining in 1993, is the Comoros, an archipelago halfway between Mozambique and Madagascar. These islands are popularly said to take their name from the *juzur al-qamar*, the Islands of the Moon, a phrase used in Arabic by medieval seafarers and merchants and in the classical Arabic geographies that followed. Here, as in other corners of the Arab world, Arabic is just one of many languages carried along by the currents of history, and Arabs themselves, at least in any ethnic sense, are few and far between.

What it means to be Arab is more than chest-thumping nationalism or a romantic vision of unity. Genealogy is only part of the story. Rapoport turns at the end of his book to the present: the Israeli judiciary casts Bedouins as nomads with no legitimate ties to the land in order to justify the removal of village communities from territories they have cultivated and inhabited for centuries. And yet the central claim of *Becoming Arab* – that the formation of Arab identity in the medieval Middle East is itself a historical construct – is not without consequence. It is a short and devastating conceptual leap from there to the idea that Arabs are not a people, though it is one that Rapoport neither makes nor invites. The modern dispossession of Arab farmers throughout the region has never been primarily a consequence of who they are, but of the land they hold. The reclassification of communally owned and cultivated land as state property under Ottoman and colonial rule was one among many mechanisms of divestment. Rapoport does not argue that the Arabs of Egypt, Palestine and Syria have no claim to indigeneity, or that Arab identity is a fiction. At its fullest, *Becoming Arab* speaks for a vision of identity capacious enough to resist the blood logic of modern nationalism and strong enough to survive being made and remade through practice, law and language.
