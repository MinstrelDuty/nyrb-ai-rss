# 正文

In​ 1993 I took the bus from Newcastle to Elterwater in Cumbria to see the barn where Kurt Schwitters made his final *Merzbau*: a sculpted relief attached to the interior wall of the barn and left unfinished at the time of his death. The sculpture had been relocated some years earlier to Newcastle’s Hatton Gallery, under the direction of Richard Hamilton. There it lurked (at least in those days) in a poorly lit space otherwise used as a student café. The barn in Elterwater was shuttered and locked and seemed on the verge of falling down; a faded plaque was the only record of Schwitters’s presence. He died in nearby Kendal in January 1948.

Schwitters was the most woebegone artist of the 20th century. His collages, made from the detritus of everyday life, trace the story of his modest success in the Weimar Republic before wartime exile returned him to obscurity in the final years of his life. He arrived in Britain in 1940 after three years in Norway, and spent fifteen months in the Hutchinson Internment Camp on the Isle of Man with other German nationals before moving to London and then to the Lake District. On his arrival at Hutchinson he was asked to give a statement explaining his presence in Britain. ‘I left Germany, because the Nazis called me for being “degenerated Artists”,’ he wrote. ‘My bokks have been burned, and I was no more allowed or able to work in Germany.’ The pathetic document is on display at the exhibition of his life and work at the Musée Picasso in Paris (until 7 February), almost half of which is devoted to his years in exile. Thirteen of his collages and paintings were confiscated in the 1930s as part of the *Entartete Kunst* campaign, and were included in the *Schandausstellungen* (‘shame exhibitions’) of modern art that toured Germany, culminating in the notorious *Degenerate Art* exhibition in Munich in the summer of 1937. By this time, Schwitters had fled Hanover with his son, leaving his wife, Helma, to guard his first *Merzbau*, an eccentric structure, like an Expressionist film set, built across several rooms of the family home on Waldhausenstrasse. (‘Merz’, from ‘Kommerz’, was Schwitters’s term for his own interpretation of Dada.) The house was obliterated by Allied bombs in 1943. Even in Norway the stigma of ‘degeneracy’ clung to him. At Lake Djupvatnet, a hotel refused him a room in case it upset German tourists. At Lysaker, where he settled for three years, he began another *Merzbau*, this one designed to be packed up in his luggage and taken with him, though in the event he left it behind and it was destroyed by fire some years later. The *Merzbau* are routinely described as ‘immersive’, but the experience of entering the reconstruction in Paris (on loan from the Sprengel Museum in Hanover) is disappointing. The room is devoid of atmosphere, and makes you realise how much these spaces were animated by Schwitters’s personality and the world in which he made them. The *Merzbau* are best experienced through the few surviving photographs of the original installations.

Schwitters was at his strongest creating intricate collages of the flotsam of modern life. Some of the best are on display in Paris. They form a thread that runs from his early Dada days in Hanover to his association with the New Typography movement and the De Stijl artists in Holland, and finally the decade of exile in Norway and England. *Mai 191* combines torn papers printed with Gothic script, possibly from a political poster. The title comes from a fragment bearing the date ‘Mai 1919’, cut off by the frame. Most of the collages contain text, but the words are never the focal point, appearing more like snatches of speech or random shouts. The lettering often provides the title of the work, as with *Untitled (Kwatta)*, in which the word ‘Kwatta’ appears on a printed fragment glued upside down at the bottom of the frame, as if the image is saying its own name. Where Picasso created still lifes and scenes in his collages, Schwitters created images of the world in motion, moments of passing consciousness. *Doremifasolasido* (1930) is composed around the word ‘Parkett’, printed on a ticket for the stalls of the Ufa-Lichtspiele cinema in Hanover. The title syllables are nowhere to be found, leaving the impression that Schwitters was recalling a song heard in a film; perhaps the title is just another passing thought. Although his collages seem an imprint of his life, and often include addressed envelopes, Schwitters was not leaving a biographical trail, but a wider impression of the world. Where he attempts more direct references to contemporary events the imagery is weaker. His largest collage, *Le Point sur le i*, made in Norway in November 1939, is an accumulation of all the usual manila-coloured material – envelopes, calendar leaves, theatre and bus tickets – but also features an image of the Nazi aviator Hugo Sperrle and a reference to Danzig (the Danzig crisis earlier that year had ended only with the outbreak of war in September). It has none of the intrigue or charm of his smaller, more intimate collages. The art historian Klaus Hinrichsen, a fellow inmate at Hutchinson, describes a poetry reading Schwitters gave at the camp. It consisted of him repeating the word ‘silence’, starting at a whisper and gradually growing to a shout which then became a scream. He ended by smashing a teacup and saucer. This must have cost him with the camp authorities, but it was a cathartic experience for the other internees: Hinrichsen wrote that ‘the sheer audacity, combined with the perversion of screaming the word SILENCE, suddenly released in the refugees all the pent-up frustrations of being interned, being treated as enemies, being victimised by officialdom.’

Schwitters’s poetry often seeks this sort of psychic release. The *Ursonate*, which he worked on and performed throughout the 1920s and early 1930s, falls somewhere between Joyce and Marinetti:

> Fümms bö wö tää zää Uu,  
> pögiff,  
> kwii Ee.  
> Oooooooooooooooooooooooooooo,  
> dll rrrrrr beeeee bö…

But you had to hear Schwitters perform it. The recording he made is the basis for most interpretations, giving sense and structure to the phonetic outpouring just as Joyce’s sing-song recordings do for *Finnegans Wake*. The brilliance of the *Ursonate* is the way it liberates speech sounds from linguistic meaning. It is played on a loop in the exhibition space at the Musée Picasso, making it impossible to concentrate on the works in the first few rooms. You just have to submit.

Schwitters’s exile poems are charming but not very memorable, even slightly embarrassing. Any attempt to analyse them descends into comedy. In the late 1940s, Schwitters corresponded with his old Dada comrade Raoul Hausmann, at that point living in Limoges, and the two concocted a new poetry movement called PIN (‘Poetry Is Now’), for which Schwitters wrote amusing doggerel. E.L.T. Mesens, co-director of the London Gallery, refused to publish their efforts – they failed, as he saw it, to engage with the world of 1946. Schwitters’s plays on language don’t feel like a ‘profoundly anti-authoritarian project’, as the exhibition claims, but like jests and games that only make sense when seen alongside his collages, or performed by Schwitters himself.

For all this, Schwitters created some of his best work in exile. His Norwegian paintings return to the academic style of his student days in Dresden, but also employ a thick impasto to render the ice and fjords. Up close one is reminded of Frank Auerbach, arriving in London ten years later, layering paint on his canvases as if trying to impose depth on a flattened, alien scene. Schwitters’s assemblages – relief sculptures made with objects and often painted – also changed; he switched bus tickets for pebbles and theatre advertisements for pieces of dried seaweed, and the rhythm became that of the ocean rather than the city street. The assemblages he made before he was in exile – simple constructions of coloured shapes, made under the sway of Hans Arp – are less convincing. They have none of the vitality of his collages and are given life by the repeated intrusion of a perky yellow rectangle, which after a while begins to feel like a stand-in for Schwitters himself. He depended on this element of humour: when it is absent, when he tries to make serious ‘art’, things go awry. His eccentricity seemed to blossom when he arrived in England and began to make paintings less like ‘art’ than anything he had created before. *Roter Winkel, Graue Feder* (‘Red Corner, Grey Feather’, 1944) is made up of swooshes and lines of thick paint, dotted in an elegant miasma like the corner of a Vuillard, but ungainly and forced elsewhere. Only Picabia could produce a painting at once so ugly and so beautiful. The Nazi exhibition was still touring Germany when Schwitters arrived in England. Whether he was painting deliberately ‘degenerate’ works, as Oskar Kokoschka had done with his *Self-Portrait of a Degenerate Artist*, is not clear, though this form of revenge must have passed through his mind.

The collages he made in the last two years of his life are extraordinary. *Gold out of Brown* (1946) is a concoction of envelopes and packing paper interspersed with text in English, including snippets from ‘The Dance to Braid’ by the poet Lascelles Abercrombie. It has an elegance that rivals Picasso, and suggests that Schwitters had come to see himself, despite his obscurity and neglect, as an old master of modern art. He used prints of Old Masters in his collages, but this was not a retrograde step. Time, he wrote to his companion, Edith Thomas, was always running out. Her name appears at the top of the beguiling collage *Anemone* (1947), which combines a Ministry of Food ration book with brightly coloured fragments of painted canvas (perhaps an abandoned painting of anemones), like emissaries from America, with its lick-the-page advertising and consumer plenty. It was just at this moment that Eduardo Paolozzi was unwittingly launching Pop Art in London with collaged images taken from American magazines. Schwitters didn’t live to see it. At the Musée Picasso, the 1946 collage *C 63 Old Picture* feels like his parting shot: fragments of Mannerist drapery combined with torn paper, more a papier collé than a montage. Among the layers of manila, dark brown and grey, there’s just one bit of text (‘Dr Scholl’s Foot Comfort Service’) and, in the top-right corner, a bright patch of blue.
