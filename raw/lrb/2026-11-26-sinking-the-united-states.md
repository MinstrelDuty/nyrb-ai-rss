# 正文

Earlier​ this month, the hulk of the Atlantic liner *United States* was towed from its mooring at Mobile, Alabama, and scuttled twenty miles off the Florida coast by the US Marine Corps, where it now forms the world’s largest artificial reef. Okaloosa County in Florida bought the ship in 2024 for $1 million in the hope that tourists would come to dive at the site. It also intends to build a small museum in the city of Destin featuring the liner’s twin smokestacks.

Three of the most famous Cunard liners – the *Queen Mary*, the *Queen Elizabeth* and the *QE2*, all built by John Brown’s shipyard in Clydebank – were used as troopships. In 1982, the *QE2* took three thousand soldiers from the 5th Infantry Brigade to the Falklands. The Cunard liners weren’t built as troop carriers, but the *United States* was built with a military purpose in mind. It was constructed in 1950 in a naval shipyard at Newport News, Virginia, as a joint venture between the US government and United States Lines (USL). The contract, signed in 1949, priced the liner at $80 million; USL paid 35 per cent and the government the rest. Its designer was William Francis Gibbs, an eccentric obsessive who had helped with the production of the Liberty ships – 2700 cargo ships constructed at great speed during the Second World War.

The US government wanted the *United States* to be able to transport an army division – fourteen thousand troops – across the Atlantic. The specification was for a ship capable of sailing anywhere in the world without refuelling, but the potential war imagined was in Europe. It wasn’t the biggest liner on the Atlantic route – in 1950 that was the *Queen Elizabeth* – but it was the fastest. Quite how fast it could go was a naval secret. Leaked reports after its sea trials indicated that under full power it might be capable of 44 knots, or a little over fifty miles per hour, but this seems unlikely. Its well-documented average speed of 35 knots, or 40 mph, remains remarkable and it still holds the Blue Riband as the fastest passenger liner in regular service across the Atlantic. Viewed side on, the ship is all hull, with two vast smokestacks above a beetling superstructure. It looks like a machine built for speed, sharp and streamlined, quite unlike the corpulent cruise ships of the present day. There is a lovely aerial shot of it in the opening sequence of *West Side Story* (1961), a slim dagger pointing at the West Side Highway.

The *United States* was also an exercise in soft power. It didn’t look like a battleship and the interior designs, by Dorothy Marckwald, were what we would now call mid-century modern, in deliberate contrast to the stuffy interiors of the Cunard liners. Gibbs’s brief was to make it ‘spacious, modern and uncluttered’, so Marckwald settled on something ‘quick and snappy’. In his book on the *United States*, Stephen Ujifusa noted that ‘nothing could appear frivolous’ because so much public money was involved. It helped that Gibbs seemed to think decoration a sin. He was also obsessed by marine fires, so the interiors had almost no wood. USL’s publicity material used a breezy sans serif, projecting a classless modernity.

I was a passenger on the *United States* in September 1967. I was only three months old, so I have no memory of the trip, but it became part of our family mythology. My father, an astrophysicist, was a postdoc at the Godard Space Flight Center in Maryland for two years, and I was born at the end of that period. We were offered return to the UK by sea for free, on condition that we sailed on an American liner. The *United States* was the only option. We used to look at my parents’ slides of the trip if there was nothing on TV, always the same ones in the same order. There I was in my father’s arms at the stern with Long Island City in the background. There was my mother holding me on the upper promenade deck in piercing sunlight, a great red smokestack behind us. There was the funny picture of French seagull shit on the cabin porthole (the boat called at Le Havre before arriving in England). There was the *Queen Mary* at Southampton, where it had just moored after completing its last commercial transatlantic voyage. We had inadvertently travelled just at the moment when airliners displaced ships as the primary means of transatlantic travel. We didn’t have a decent picture of the *United States* itself, but lots of ephemera: luridly colourised postcards, deck plans and cross sections, advertisements extolling the virtues of sea travel, luggage tags and menus. There was also an inexplicable photo of the three of us at dinner, my mother in a satin gown, my father in evening dress sporting an Abraham Lincoln beard and a wizard’s hat.

For my parents, the *United States* represented the ideal America. It was a generous and essentially benign polity, well engineered in all respects. It was also unfussy, bordering on austere – positive qualities in my parents’ eyes. It represented a country of public works, national parks, fine roads and clean public toilets, all things that were in short supply back home.

After we docked, the *United States* sailed for only one more complete season. Its career, terminated abruptly in 1969, had lasted sixteen years; its military capacities had never been deployed. Its French competitor, the slower but slightly larger and prettier *France*, managed only thirteen years. Competition from airlines did for them both. That said, remarkably few Atlantic liners spent longer in service. They lasted at most thirty years, if not sunk by icebergs or torpedoed by U-boats. The ships didn’t even have the durability of the airliners that replaced them: the Boeing jet I recently took to Newark was 35 years old, and the B-52s that bombed Iran early this year have been going for more than sixty years.

After being laid up in 1969, the liner was preserved, dehumidified and sealed back at Newport News while successive US governments debated what to do with it. After all, it was a military asset, and its supposedly secret hull design meant it couldn’t be sold. After it was finally declassified in 1977, a succession of owners tried and failed to make it viable as a floating condominium or a cruise ship. In 1992, it was towed back across the Atlantic, through the Mediterranean and the Black Sea to the Ukrainian port of Sevastopol, where it remained for some time while asbestos was removed and the remaining interior fittings stripped out, tasks too filthy and expensive to be undertaken in the US. In photographs it’s strange to see this American symbol of the Cold War moored near vessels from the Russian Black Sea fleet. Another image shows it arriving in Istanbul, where it was stormed by Greenpeace activists protesting at the asbestos.

After its then owners, Marmara Marine, ran out of cash, the US government repossessed the ship and towed it across the Atlantic again, this time to Philadelphia, where it spent the next 29 years, most of them at Pier 82 opposite the local branch of Ikea. The *United States* filled the view from the windows of its first-floor restaurant, with a wall text explaining the view to curious diners. When I went there in 2009, it was the first time I had seen the ship as an adult. It felt like seeing a ghost.

The liner was acquired in 2011 by the SS *United States* Conservancy Council, but it eventually ran out of money to pay the berthing fees, and so in February last year the *United States* was towed 1800 miles down the east coast, round the Florida Keys and into the Gulf of Mexico to Mobile, not far from Okaloosa County.

You might have thought that the present US administration would have wanted to preserve the *United States*. It would have made a splendid addition to the historic vessels on the Chelsea waterfront in New York, which include an aircraft carrier, the USS *Intrepid*, now a popular museum. (One of the *United States*’s four-bladed outboard propellers is at Pier 76, repurposed as nautical sculpture.) The liner seems a good example of the ‘greatness’ – industrial might, confidence, patriotism – that Trump wants to restore. Last December he proposed a new ‘Trump class’ battleship, which he claimed to have had a hand in designing. The SS *United States* Conservancy Council did in fact approach Trump during his first term, and early last year a rival body, the New York Coalition to Save the SS *United States,* also tried to get him involved. As far as I can tell, there was no official response to either request (maybe they should have pitched it as a piece of real estate).

There was a brief campaign against the plan to sink the liner. The New York Coalition to Save the SS *United States* was set up to prevent it and to lobby for the return of the hulk to New York City. As well as writing to Trump, it took legal action against Okaloosa County, claiming, among other things, that because of the *United States*’s aluminium-heavy construction, the proposed reef would be an environmental hazard. At the end of last year, when the *United States* was already half dismantled, New York City Council passed a resolution calling for its ‘restoration, and its relocation to the city of New York’. But these were quixotic gestures. Whatever it means to have sunk the *United States,* there was scarcely anything left of it worth preserving.
