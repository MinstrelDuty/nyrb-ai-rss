# 正文

Early one morning in May 1985, three Johannesburg session musicians – the bassist Bakithi Kumalo, the guitarist Ray Phiri and the drummer Isaac Mtshali – touched down at John F. Kennedy airport in New York City to record an album with Paul Simon. It was the first time any of them had left South Africa. At the gate, they were met by Simon and shown to a limousine by a white chauffeur. As they watched the sun rise over the Manhattan skyline from the Triborough Bridge, discussing all they would see and do in the city, what Simon called “the reality of life” suddenly intruded. “We weren’t going to begin work until the next day,” he recalled, “and Isaac Mtshali said to me in the midst of this excitement, ‘When do we have to go to register with the police?’”

This anecdote is one of many in Ashley Kahn’s _Days of Miracle and Wonder_ that underscores the gulf between Simon and his collaborators on the project that became _Graceland_. He was a middle-aged pop star in a creative slump, inspired by imported cassettes to seek out the scrappy bands who soundtracked the South African townships (the propulsive _Indestructible Beat of Soweto_, as a popular compilation was titled). They were working-class musicians, many of them accustomed to getting paid by the finished track, rather than by the hour. (Simon would pay them three times the standard US studio rate.) Apartheid had clipped all their wings, from journeymen like Kumalo, who used his first cheque to buy medicine for his ailing mother (and some new clothes for himself) to the most established act to appear on _Graceland_, the transcendental isicathamiya vocal group Ladysmith Black Mambazo.

Simon’s determination to bridge divides of geography, language, race and genre made _Graceland_ one of the most ambitious pop recording projects ever undertaken. The results were undeniable. A critical and commercial success, the record saved Simon’s career and cemented a western market for what became known as “world music”. His decision to record part of the album in Johannesburg, though, guaranteed international controversy. The local musicians’ union had voted to invite him, but his trip nonetheless violated the cultural boycott of South Africa called by the African National Congress and upheld by a UN resolution in 1980. Here lies the crux of _Graceland_: a cosmopolitan triumph, but at what cost?
