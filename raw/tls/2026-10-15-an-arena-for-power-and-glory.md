# 正文

Aristotle, that relentless ancient classifier, would have loved the modern _Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders_. Its cold and categorical list of symptoms gives professionals a common language for diagnosing mental health conditions. Some would like to add “Hubris syndrome” – a personality change arising from power and success, first described by the physician and politician David Owen. It owes its name to the Greek _hybris_, which meant something like “insolent pride indulged at someone else’s expense”. The modern version would add such symptoms as “a narcissistic propensity to see [one’s] world primarily as an arena in which to exercise power and glory”, or “loss of contact with reality”.

This volume brings classicists into conversation with neuroscientists, psychologists and management theorists. In the first section, on ancient Greece, four essays consider obvious cases of hubristic behaviour (Persia’s King Xerxes is the classic example) and less obvious ones: a hubristic vine, for example, luxuriating in producing shoots and leaves, but arrogantly refusing to bear fruit for its human tender.

The three chapters in the second part, on contemporary psychology and neuroscience, investigate the brain and psychological profiles of individuals who show traits of Hubris syndrome. The stress experienced by politicians and executives can affect the prefrontal cortex, potentially impairing decision-making and altering personality. The upside of this, however, might be a confidence that really does pay off – unlike ancient _hybris_, which had no real “bright side”.

The final section, with contributions from business and management experts, discusses the perils of hubris in corporate environments – sometimes drawing surprisingly on ancient _hybris_. One chapter points out that modern societies, unlike classical Athens, largely fail to recognize the harm done to victims of the hubris committed by powerful individuals. Another analyses “hubris narratives” in the (auto)biographies of executives – notably the former Apple CEOs Steve Jobs and John Sculley – which can seem to turn captains of industry into ancient hubristic kings.

Few readers will greet pages on Athenian tragedy, prefrontal cortex degradation and organizational management with equal enthusiasm. But the virtue of the book is bringing such different specialists into conversation at all. While it may not be worth pondering seriously whether Alcibiades had a Cluster B personality disorder, one enduring question remains: how best to check the overweening, erratic and destructive behaviour of society’s most powerful members.
