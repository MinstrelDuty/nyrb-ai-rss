# Death on campus

## A philosophical investigation into a murder

### In this review

Time Being

208pp. Salt. Paperback, £10.99.

Jude Cook

The second paragraph of Jude Cook’s third novel introduces a dead body. The end of the prologue, a few pages later, delivers a twist (the first of several), undermining much of what we have inferred about the death so far. _Time Being_ has the trappings of a murder-mystery, but it houses within the genre structure – as the echo of St Augustine in the title implies – a philosophical investigation.

The murder victim is Laura Cameron, forty-two, a professor of philosophy at a London university based on KCL. No one can make sense of her death, inflicted by a bullet shot through the door of a fifth-floor lavatory cubicle. Cameron was charismatic, inspiring; the possessor of a bright and eccentric wardrobe; much in demand for media appearances; the taker of male and female lovers; married with two children. The protagonist of the novel is her older colleague Esther Luck, “small, dark, undemanding” and somewhat austere. Esther “is jealous of those labelled larger-than-life”. She was once the lover of Laura’s widower, Jack, before unwisely taking up with Michel, an addict, petty criminal and “volcano of rage and unpredictability”.

As the novel opens, Esther is recovering from treatment for ovarian cancer, a process that Cook portrays in grim detail: the baldness, the persistent awful taste in her mouth, the weight gain brought about by steroids that turn her “into a frump overnight”, cystitis, diarrhoea, infection. But a miracle occurs. At a meeting at which she had expected to hear her death sentence, Esther’s oncologist tells her that she is cancer-free. She spends a recuperative week at a country hotel. She takes part in an environmental protest, meeting a younger woman, Jessica, who becomes her lover. In Jessica’s bed, she finds an escape from temporality: “As if she is the experience and the experience is her, with no mediation from the body or mind”.

Esther cannot, however, escape her consciousness of death: of her relatives in the Holocaust, of her mother, of Laura. And she cannot escape guilt. Readers may wonder why Esther seems not to recognize the value of truth and the necessity for justice. But this expertly crafted novel has another twist to come.
