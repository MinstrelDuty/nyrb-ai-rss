# 正文

Ospreys – the black and white raptors that crash into lochs and rise with fish flapping in their talons – became important symbols of Scottish nature conservation when, after many years of absence from Scotland, a pair nested at Loch Garten. But this book is not about the return of the ospreys; it is about the power of their absence. It is drawn from more than 500 days of observation over five years – and many more days of non-observation.

John Lister-Kaye, the protégé of the Scottish naturalist Gavin Maxwell and the author of many incandescent books about the natural world, first saw an osprey in 1972. It shuddered out of the water, shrouded in a “mist of its own creation”. Ospreys have remained in that self-created mist since then, haunting his imagination. He “harboured a visceral need to know them”, and in 2019 he erected an old telegraph pole by the loch on the estate in the Highlands where he lives, and placed a ready-made osprey nest on the top. It seemed to work. In April of that year, ospreys spotted the nest. Lister-Kaye was desperate for them to breed. But they taunted him. They mated on the nest, dropped sticks on it, but did not lay their eggs there. Despite his hopes that they would stay and lay, they were in charge – an understanding he comes to gradually. “Nature rules out here”, he writes. “I want to be subordinate for once, to let my suppressed animal-ness drift back to the surface and take over”. The ospreys rewilded him and, in so doing, humanized him.

The wild world rewards humility, and Lister-Kaye was rewarded royally. Sometimes he did see an osprey, and the day would be “transformed, sent spinning from flabby patience to a rush of spirited joy”. But very often he did not, meeting instead the other creatures that form the world of the wood, the hill and the loch. This tweedy baronet was forced literally to the ground by the splendour of it all, “cheek to the pine needles in an unplanned expression of reverence, or meditative engagement, or just plain, old-fashioned pleasure”.

The author probably knows more about ospreys than anyone else in the world. That knowledge is on exuberant display in this wonderful and wonder-full book – Lister-Kaye’s best yet.
