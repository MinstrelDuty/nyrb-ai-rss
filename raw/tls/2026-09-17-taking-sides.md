# 正文

What is your worst fear? Violence? Disease? Financial ruin? Societal collapse? Whatever it is, Emily St. John Mandel has a story for you. _Exit Party_, her seventh novel, actually has two. The first part of the book, “A-Side”, begins in 2031, following the fracture of the US into a region of warring city-states, territories and militias. The second part, “B-Side”, occurs in the same time, same place, but in a parallel universe. In that other world, society has “closed like a fist” to produce a country run by total surveillance and policed by agents known as the “Service”.

The novel’s two-handed structure pulls off exhilarating feats of narrative derring-do. It starts _in_ _medias res_, at a party where things take place that neither the characters nor the reader can understand. It then dives into two packed plotlines, each with sets of parallel characters and intertwined lives that span four decades. It is difficult to summarize and works beautifully. Mandel is a virtuoso of complication, catching the reader up in suspense, then providing answers slowly, thoroughly, satisfyingly.

Both parts of the novel follow the fortunes of one woman, who is and is not actually two women. In “A-Side”, Ari is a recently released would-be murderer navigating the ravaged country that fell during her imprisonment. Ibari, Ari’s self in the parallel world of “B-Side”, is a Service lifer. Both women have to do things they do not like. They make choices that at first seem different from one another’s, but arrive at surprisingly similar ends. Both are resourceful, smart and self-aware. Both come to have deep regrets about the lives they have led. They, and we, cannot easily say whether they could have been different from what they are. They are and are not what their worlds have made them.

One of the strangest things about _our_ world is that Mandel is a global bestseller in it. This is a compliment. It is often bewildering to see the company her novels keep on airport convenience-store shelves. From her first novel, _Last Night in Montreal_ (2009), she has written impeccably grounded, delicately metaphorical narratives that are at heart about ideas (time and space, fate and choice) and moral questions. What is corruption? Can it be avoided in a corrupt world? How much responsibility does an individual have when we are all born into circumstances we didn’t choose?
