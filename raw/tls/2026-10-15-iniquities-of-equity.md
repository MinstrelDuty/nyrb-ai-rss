# 正文

Andy Burnham used the word “control” more than two dozen times during his speech at Labour’s conference in Liverpool last month. He talked of “political control”, “economic control”, even the Brexiteers’ maxim “taking back control”. But if Burnham’s address had a leitmotif, it was the promise of “public control” of “the basics”: housing, water, energy and transport.

Setting aside the question of what “public control” actually means, there is no doubt that Britain’s new prime minister has tapped into a prevailing mood. Nationalization has seldom been so popular among voters. The sense that the UK has sold off its family silver – and needs to get it back – spans the political spectrum.

Against this backdrop, Hettie O’Brien’s and Kate Bayliss’s new books are particularly timely. Both, in different ways, try to expose what Brett Christophers calls the “asset manager society”, a world in which the basic building blocks of life in Britain, from the country’s polluted rivers to the power that heats its homes, are controlled by opaque funds and financiers, often on the other side of the globe.

O’Brien, a _Guardian_ journalist, opens _The_ _Asset Class: How private equity turned capitalism against itself_ in a railway arch behind her parents’ home in southeast London. Here we meet a textile artist whose rent has recently doubled. She is not alone. Railway arch occupants complaining about spiralling costs have become a staple of local newspapers and Reddit threads. But these are not just isolated incidents of avaricious landlords.

England and Wales’s roughly 5,000 railway arches used to be publicly owned, by Network Rail. Following the unexpected majority for the Conservatives at the general election of 2015, the chancellor, George Osborne, embarked on a mass sell-off of public land to ease the UK’s fiscal pressures. The arches were sold as a job lot for £1.5 billion. Today, they are owned in their entirety by Blackstone, the world’s largest private equity firm.

Blackstone’s empire stretches long beyond Britain’s railway arches. As of May 2025, the New York-headquartered firm had more than $1.3 trillion in assets under management. That’s roughly the GDP of Saudi Arabia. Its dizzying array of holdings includes the back catalogue of the pop singer Nelly Furtado, the clothing brand Spanx and more than 12,500 pieces of real estate around the world, including Legoland, the London Dungeon and Madame Tussauds.
