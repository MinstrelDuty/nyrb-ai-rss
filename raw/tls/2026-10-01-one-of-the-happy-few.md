# 正文

The Booker-winning novelist and art historian Anita Brookner (1928–2016) poses an unusual challenge to any biographer brave enough to tackle her life. During a prolific career – twenty-four novels produced with astonishing regularity from the early 1980s until 2009 – Brookner had a reputation for being aloof and austere. Outside her work she was, she declared, resolved to “leave no trace” of herself. She was as good as her word: when Hermione Lee began her research, she discovered that Brookner had taken pains to destroy as many of her private papers as possible.

This isn’t, perhaps, all that remarkable; other writers have done the same. As a biographer and critic, Lee has long been drawn to women authors who are difficult to disentangle from their public image: Edith Wharton, whose reputation as a prim _grande dame_ is belied by her secret erotic archive; Elizabeth Bowen, the outwardly poised creator of worlds steeped in unease; Willa Cather, already a cultural monument by the time of her death, and anxious to suppress letters that would expose the self-doubt behind the flinty façade. What distinguishes Brookner’s case is that much of this erasure concerns the years before she became a novelist at all, when there was, as yet, no “Anita Brookner” whose legend needed protecting. Her first novel did not appear until 1981, when she was fifty-two. By then, she had already perfected a persona – high-minded, thin, coiffed, handbag under her arm – that was notable for its _soignée_ self-possession. Frustratingly, the experiences that might seem most likely to explain the work belong to the part of her life whose traces Brookner was most determined to remove.

That this secrecy was less a case of literary estate management than a deep habit of self-concealment is further shown by Brookner’s reluctance to engage in the usual forms of writerly self-promotion. She didn’t throw book launches or attend literary festivals, and she avoided interviews. When these proved inescapable, she perfected off-the-peg if contradictory replies to the impertinent questions posed by journalists. Why had she never married? Well, she chose the wrong people. Or the wrong people chose her. Did she regret it? Yes, she was lonely and unfulfilled as a result. Or, no, she was glad not to have to share her well-run life with anyone else. Now you see her, now you don’t.

The unmarried, childless Brookner…
