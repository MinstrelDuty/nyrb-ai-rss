# 正文

Anne Carson trained as a classical scholar, receiving her PhD from the University of Toronto in 1981 with her dissertation on longing and lack, the pain inherent in desire, in Sappho and other Greek writers, _Odi et amo ergo sum_ (“I hate and I love, therefore I am”). This was reworked and published as her debut, _Eros the Bittersweet_ (1986), and was a popular and critical success – an unusual achievement for a book of essays about classical poetry. Lyrical as a critic, scholarly as a poet, Carson has since carved out a very particular yet well-known niche in contemporary poetry. At the heart of her work is the idea that writing is a way to have one’s cake and eat it. A negative statement contains the positive thing it negates: such a both-at-once paradox is, as she has pointed out in _Economy of the Unlost_ (1999), available only with language.

In _Anne Carson: The glass essayist_ (the title nods to Carson’s early discursive poem on her mother and Emily Brontë, “The Glass Essay”), Elizabeth Sarah Coles does a thorough and intelligent job of discussing Carson’s career. The monograph covers everything from _Eros the Bittersweet_ to _H of H Playbook_ (2021), a facsimile collage “playbook” – a term that suggests both a collection of strategies for a team playing a game and a playbook in the early modern theatrical sense – about Euripides’ _Herakles_. It is serious and erudite in its scholarship and includes, satisfyingly, hefty footnotes, a bibliography and an index. The cover image is typical Carson: the inscrutable poet, in shorts and walking shoes, sits in silhouette, but turns to face the photographer (and us), wearing glasses and a sphinx-like expression. (She has to be the only living writer who regularly uses author photos that seem calculatedly less attractive than she is in person, visual versions of her standard deadpan biography line: “Anne Carson was born in Canada and teaches ancient Greek.”)

The chapters address the range of Carson’s strategies in her various books: creative criticism, fictional essays, “speculative form” and“open translation” – where the translator essentially throws up her hands and gestures despairingly to the dictionary entries of individual words.
