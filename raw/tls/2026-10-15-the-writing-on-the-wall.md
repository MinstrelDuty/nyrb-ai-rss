# 正文

There can be few monuments from the ancient Greek and Roman worlds quite as peculiar as an inscription from the second century AD in the town of Oinoanda, just northeast of modern Fethiye in Turkey. The inscription was originally massive: probably about 80 metres long and more than 3 metres high, and estimated to have contained approximately 25,000 words. Its message, carved across a number of courses of stone and including a number of separate texts, is that true happiness can be acquired only through the philosophical teaching of the Hellenistic hedonist and atomist philosopher Epicurus.

We know nothing about the “Diogenes” who commissioned this inscription except what we can infer from the text, many fragments of which still lie close by or have been incorporated into later buildings. He was clearly a wealthy citizen with a passionate desire to improve the lives of his fellow Oinoandans. But, unlike other local benefactors, rather than build them a bathhouse or sponsor a festival, he offered them the truth about the universe and how they should arrange their desires and beliefs so as to live happy lives.

Much of our knowledge of Diogenes and his inscription is thanks to the work of Martin Ferguson Smith and his collaborators. Smith has been working on the site and the inscription since the 1960s. He published a significant edition of the text, with translation and commentary, in 1993, but further fragments have since come to light.

This new publication is a distillation of his current thinking, and is also available as a free ebook; doubtless, Diogenes would have approved of any means to publicize his message as widely as possible. Smith provides a concise but helpful introduction to the inscription and the history of work on it, and a translation that refines his earlier versions and adds the fragments discovered since 1993, now included where Smith thinks they belong among the previously known material. (Unfortunately, that means that there is a new numeration for the fragments, so a bit of page-turning or scrolling is needed to line up the different editions.) There are also explanatory notes for each one, though the earlier publications will still be necessary for more detailed arguments. This is a handy starting point for anyone curious about this peculiar inscription, and should prompt further inquiry into the social and political context in which it stood.
