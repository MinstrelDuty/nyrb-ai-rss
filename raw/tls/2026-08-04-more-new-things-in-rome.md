# More new things in Rome

*

![Image 2: The TLS front page from the issue dated More new things in Rome. Issue number ](https://www.the-tls.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/mb1.jpg)

© Alamy

One of the most extraordinary collections of ancient sculpture in Rome is the Albani-Torlonia collection – a combination of what is left of the eighteenth-century collection (now largely dispersed) assembled by [Cardinal Alessandro Albani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alessandro_Albani), with the advice of [J. J. Winckelmann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johann_Joachim_Winckelmann), and the nineteenth-century collection of the [Torlonia family](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torlonia), who bought up the splendid Villa Albani in the city in 1866 (hence the hybrid “Albani-Torlonia” – though I am simplifying a very complicated story here).

The Torlonia family had quite a lot of their material on show in a museum in Rome’s Trastevere until the Second World War, but it never reopened. For years this collection was locked up, hard to access and one of the lost jewels of Rome. But things are changing under the [Torlonia Foundation](https://www.fondazionetorlonia.org/), which was established a decade ago. They have sponsored a big travelling exhibition of works from the collection, which started in the [Capitoline Museums](https://www.burlington.org.uk/archive/exhibition-review/the-torlonia-marbles-collecting-masterpieces) in Rome in 2020, but has since gone to the Paris, Chicago, Texas and further afield.

As yet, there is no regular public access to the Villa Albani itself, built in the eighteenth century to be a grand living space combined with domestic museum – though you can [arrange group tours](https://www.fondazionetorlonia.org/visit) (a huge change from when I was a graduate student in Rome, when it was simply off-limits). And recently they have opened an excellent [free display](https://www.wantedinrome.com/news/exhibition-space-at-villa-albani-torlonia-in-rome-doubles-in-size.html) of some of the Torlonia sculptures in the old stables of the Villa (nicely incorporating the rather posh, aristocratic animal feeding troughs into the show!). It’s a small exhibition, but it gives you a great feel for the whole collection, and a glimpse of the Villa itself.

![Image 3](https://www.the-tls.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/Piranesi-16047.jpg?w=1024)

Courtesy Wikimedia Commons

This week I was lucky enough to get a sneak peek inside the Villa and made a beeline for what is probably the most famous remaining work of art acquired by the Cardinal: [the relief panel of Antinous](https://museum.classics.cam.ac.uk/collections/casts/deep-relief-antinous-silvanus), the emperor Hadrian’s enslaved boyfriend who drowned mysteriously in the Nile in 130 CE. This was found at Hadrian’s Villa at Tivoli in the early 1750s and was installed above a fireplace in one of the display rooms on the _piano nobile_.
