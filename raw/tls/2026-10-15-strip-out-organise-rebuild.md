# 正文

According to its blurb, _Drystone_ – by the writer and photographer Kristie De Garis – is about not just “overcoming hardship, but shaping life within it”. De Garis has certainly led a hard life. When she is eight years old, her single mother – a fiery, occasionally violent woman – moves the family from Jedburgh to Caithness, “one end of Scotland to the other”. They have little money, and De Garis is shunned at her new school for being poor.

She begins drinking heavily when she is a teenager, and notes that “early starts in alcohol were common in Caithness … I loved it”. A relationship with a cheating, abusive university boyfriend, Daniel, results in an abortion. “[He] destroyed me in every way”, De Garis writes. Self-harm, antidepressants and suicidal ideation follow. She embarks on, then drops out of, multiple college courses and finds it difficult to hold down low-skilled work. “Something always got in my way.” She marries a colleague, Luke, and they have a baby daughter, Jo. But De Garis struggles with motherhood, and suffers panic attacks and intrusive thoughts. Her drinking increases, and one evening she is date-raped in a bar.

After De Garis separates from Luke, she has a child with a second partner, Janek, and moves to rural Perthshire seeking to gain control of her life. She takes up numerous hobbies, including running and photography, and eventually completes a drystone walling course. It’s in this activity that she finds some peace: “To build with stone … changes your mental pace. Drystone works the impatience out of you … In a world I find overwhelming, it quietly absorbs the chaos”.

Luke, a waller himself, moves in with her and Janek to form an unconventional family unit. Luke’s injunction to “strip out, organise, rebuild” when working on a wall is an obvious metaphor for what De Garis must do with her life. It’s around this time that she discovers the “something” – the reason for her impulsivity, hyperfocus and reliance on alcohol. A casual remark in a text from a friend (“you’re def not neurotypical”) leads to a late ADHD diagnosis and the inevitable grief of reframing the last three and a half decades of her life through the lens of neurodiversity. “How much damage had been caused by not understanding – and not being understood?”, she asks.
