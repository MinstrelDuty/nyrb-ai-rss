# 正文

As the past three years have shown, many people hold strong views about the Middle East, though many of these opinions are based on little more than superficial knowledge of the region’s history. Simon Sebag Montefiore’s aim, in _The Cauldron: The making of the modern Middle East_, to give readers the background to form their own judgements is obvious, laudable and urgently necessary. His geographical definition runs from Morocco to Iran, including Turkey and Sudan, which is a broader canvas than most comparable histories attempt. For readers already interested in the history of the Middle East, _The Cauldron_ unquestionably earns its place on the shelf of books that have in their turn attempted to explain the whole region, wherever its boundaries are drawn. In scope, it may be considered the most ambitious.

The text is divided into two broad periods: “Empires: Sultans, Pashas, and Proconsuls (1900–1945)”; and “Independent States: Presidents, Kings, and Imams (1945–2026)”, the second act being given considerably more room than the first. One of the book’s most distinctive contributions is its inclusion and treatment of Iran. Where other recent histories tend towards the Arab-centric, presenting Iran as a powerful but peripheral force, Sebag Montefiore integrates it as a full co-protagonist in the region. This results in one of the book’s genuine contributions to the field. The author’s claim that the revolution in 1979 was an event of the order of 1789 or 1917 is entirely justified, while the case for Ayatollah Khomeini being a political strategist of the rank of Lenin or Mao is well argued, even if this reviewer is not entirely convinced.

Sebag Montefiore is a past master at finding just the right telling detail or revelatory anecdote to make the history both lively and memorable. For instance, he describes the mockery doled out by President Nasser of Egypt towards the Muslim Brotherhood and, perhaps astonishingly for modern readers, the hijab. Sebag Montefiore quotes one of his live speeches, which were broadcast nationwide to an audience of millions, in which Nasser ridiculed the head of the Brotherhood: “‘Sir, you have a daughter in the College of Medicine … _she’s_ not wearing a scarf. If you can’t make one girl – who’s your daughter – wear a scarf, how do you expect me to put a scarf on _10 million women_?’ This brought the house down.”

A thread of cosmopolitan elegy…
