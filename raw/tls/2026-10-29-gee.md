# 正文

Not so long ago (October 2) we wrote of poetry’s “usefulness”. Not just as reading matter, that is, but as reading matter that could “grow your confidence” or help you to “celebrate growing older” – such are the large claims made by two anthologies that happened to have come our way.

More have come our way since then. Top of the pile is _You Can Do It with a Broken Heart_ (First Ink, £14.99). “It turns out that all the best people have had their hearts cruelly broken”, this volume’s compiler, Ana Sampson, states in her introduction. Here are poems for “the days when the wheels are coming off and love feels like it’s slipping through our fingers”, as well as for “wallowing”, fury “that sweeps in like a forest fire” and the “slow and stately business” of picking up the pieces. We are not sure if the “relatable wisdom” of W. B. Yeats, Jackie Kay et al can live up to this therapeutic hard sell. The unlucky lover who leafs through this book may at least be led to investigate further and read all of George Meredith’s _Modern Love_ (in which the wheels most certainly come off) or more of Rosemary Tonks (“Criminal, you took a great piece of my life”).

Via “Story of a Hotel Room”, Tonks crops up again in _Vices_, one of the more vivid and various additions to the Poetry Poison series (Macmillan, £10 each), edited by Deborah Alma. Published last month alongside _Revenge_ and _Darkness_, _Vices_ belongs to the Poetry Pharmacy – an outfit that has already prescribed, in a more positive vein, anthologies for love, joy, inspiration and so forth. The idea here is to offer a “controlled dose of the less virtuous emotions” – as represented by the vicious pride of “Ozymandias” and the darkness of “Do Not Go Gentle into That Good Night”, apparently, although there are also some satisfying contemporary selections, especially in _Revenge_. Is this the kind of thing Alan Brownjohn meant when he proclaimed, “I write nothing without hoping it might make the world one grain better”? (“A pompous statement which, I suppose, makes me a moralist as a writer, a humanist one.”)

A new catalogue from Peter Harrington – which is now available in print and via peterharrington.co.uk – tells a familiar tale in rare detail. _World at War…_
