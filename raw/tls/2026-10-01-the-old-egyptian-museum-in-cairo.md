# 正文

The treasures of [Tutankhamun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tutankhamun) were removed from their [old museum](https://egyptianmuseumcairo.eg/) in central Cairo and splendidly re-displayed, just over a year ago, in the new [Grand Egyptian Museum](https://gem.eg/) near the pyramids. (In posts a few months ago, [I wrote about the GEM](https://www.the-tls.com/regular-features/mary-beard-a-dons-life/a-visit-to-the-grand-egyptian-museum-blog-post) and the wonderful, newly refurbished [Greco-Roman Museum in Alexandria](https://www.the-tls.com/regular-features/mary-beard-a-dons-life/a-five-star-museum-in-alexandria-blog-post).) So, what has happened to Tutankhamun’s former home, in its splendid nineteenth-century building, in Tahrir Square? I visited this week, partly to find out.

The answer is that it is still very much worth a visit, and it is undergoing a gradual process of renovation and re-display (some of this in collaboration with the British Museum and other European museums). Yes, it has lost its big selling point, and a few other important Egyptian antiquities have migrated too. But there is plenty left (a deal was done that nothing that was in the old museum at the date of its foundation would move) – and in a way, without Tutankhamun grabbing the headlines and attracting vast crowds, it is easier to look at some of the other great things in peace.

One high point for me was the gold funerary mask of [King Psusennes I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psusennes_I) (_c_.1000 BCE) plus his silver coffin. These weren’t found surrounded by the wealth of other treasure that is part of the draw of Tutankhamun, but the mask and coffin themselves are certainly rivals for what [Howard Carter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Howard_Carter) discovered – and you can have it almost to yourself, with no jostling from selfie takers.

Another was the wooden statue of the scribe [Kaaper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaaper), just over half life-size, dating to the middle of the third millennium BCE. It is made of pieces of sycamore wood, meticulously fitted together, and totally disposes of the notion that Egyptian art didn’t, or couldn’t, do naturalism. Some figures are as “naturalistic” as anything from classical Greece. Just look at Kaaper’s face and those eyes. He’s also a striking reminder of how some important genres of ancient art have barely survived: the tradition of large wooden sculpture is one – which I talked a bit about in that earlier post.
