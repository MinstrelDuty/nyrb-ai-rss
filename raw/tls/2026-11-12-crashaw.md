# 正文

During the Second World War, the British press frequently turned to historical, spiritual and theological anchors to navigate the trauma of suffering and loss. It was against this background that, in 1942, the _TLS_ published “Crashaw” by John Fethaland, the pen-name of the playwright and critic-turned-psychologist Leonard Inkster. He began his career writing Edwardian social drama and verse and turned later to commercial detective fiction. In 1933, however, he published _The Treatment of Functional Nerve Cases by the Method of Neuro-Induction_. This technique, which he pioneered at the Tavistock Square Clinic, bypassed conscious resistance by relaxing the physical nervous system in order to better allow the patient to process trauma or distress. The use of physical relaxation to repair a fractured nervous system might stand as a definition of the somatic effect of a lot of poetry, but Fethaland often considered it in a specifically religious light.

In “Crashaw”, he reflects a widespread mid-twentieth century revival of interest in seventeenth-century religious poetry – the poem appeared in the same year as T. S. Eliot’s “Little Gidding”, which was inspired by Nicholas Ferrar’s Anglican community, where Richard Crashaw (1612–1649) was a regular visitor. But Fethaland also finds in Crashaw a poet who maps out particularly clearly a spiritual equivalent of his own approach to dealing with emotional stress. In his “Hymn to St. Teresa of Avila”, Crashaw defines martyrdom not as heroic death in battle or at the stake, but as an internal “transverberation”, a piercing of the soul by divine love, which might be compared with how Fethaland’s patients surrendered their ego to a larger, life-changing power. Crashaw’s verse is, in Fethaland’s phrase, “Heav’n-ordained physic” (spiritual medicine) capable of treating a world that is “too sick”; it is also a painful shattering of the ego (“the fire that purgeth”) that leads to the birth of a new self. His poetic and spiritual method involves cauterizing and soothing the soul: “Purify: make by fire … then bathe … in that liquid light.” Suffering is not meaningless, but an agonizing renewal: poetry like this can reset a broken spirit, acting as a literal prescription for a world in shock.

**Crashaw**

Crashaw, thou luminous wit,  
At home with God, Familiar of Jesus,  
Epigrammatic Chorist of the Cross,  
Return; it is our loss,  
And thou these long three hundred years dost tease us,  
That thou art absent. _Redeas_, (and please us).
