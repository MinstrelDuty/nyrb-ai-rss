# 正文

I have just made a short video, aimed at amusing people waiting in the queue for the [Bayeux Tapestry at the British Museum](https://www.britishmuseum.org/exhibitions/bayeux-tapestry). The idea is that you will be able to scan a QR code when you get to the gallery, and there will be various “entertainment” options available, just in case you are getting bored in line. Mine is a quiz about the Latin on the tapestry. It presents six scenes from the embroidered narrative, each with their Latin caption, and asks you to pick out the correct translation – and then explains the gist of the original Latin, adding a bit of information about the scene concerned too. (Just in case you are planning to go, I won’t spoil the fun by giving away which scenes they are!)

The Bayeux Tapestry’s Latin is wonderfully simple, and part of the idea is to equip people with a few basic words (“hic” = “here”, “ubi” = “where”, etc) and then show that they can get quite a long way by themselves making sense of the language. There are all kinds of other more complicated questions that the Latin of the tapestry raises and which I explored in a slightly more technical [podcast](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-yjWKIOaII4&t=69s) (such as “can we detect allusions to Virgil?” or “does this look like Latin composed by Norman French or English speakers?”). But in the quiz, I’m just concerned with welcoming people into some simple translation.

I’ve been doing quite a lot of this sort of thing. I have [written before](https://www.the-tls.com/regular-features/mary-beard-a-dons-life/the-odyssey-in-latin-blog-post) on the blog about our Cheltenham gig in which Llewelyn Morgan, Peter Stothard and I explore in Latin a classical Latin poem, with an audience who may know little or nothing of the language. And in my [recent theatre tour](https://www.fane.co.uk/mary-beard) around the country, I’ve been encouraging people to get into reading Roman graffiti. One particular favourite example (which almost everyone understands before I have finished explaining it) is the famous graffito from the lavatory of the [House of the Gem](https://herculaneum.uk/Ins%20Or%201/Herculaneum%20Ins%20Or%201%2001.htm) in Herculaneum: “_Apollinaris medicus Titi imp(eratoris) hic cacavit bene_” (“Apollinaris the doctor of the emperor Titus had a good sh*t here”).
