# 正文

In March 1949, Kazimierz Moczarski was put into a cell in a prison in Poland with Jürgen Stroop, the SS general notorious for his role in the annihilation of the Warsaw ghetto. Moczarski, a member of the Polish Home Army who had fought in the Warsaw uprising in 1944, had been arrested, tortured and jailed at the end of the war by the Communist regime on false charges of collaboration. Stroop was awaiting trial and a death sentence.

The men spent 255 days together. Moczarski, a former journalist, decided to use the time to interview Stroop, who proved a willing subject, eager to describe in great detail his life, his rise through the ranks of the SS and the massacres he had overseen._Conversations with an Executioner_ is an extraordinary and horrifying book, put together by Moczarski when he was released in 1956, but finally published uncensored only after the fall of Communism, when it became a bestseller. Sean Gasper Bye’s translation from the Polish is fluent and unobtrusive.

There was a third man in the cell, an SS policeman called Gustav Schielke. As Stroop talked, the two others fed him questions. Moczarski had an excellent memory and later made more than 1,000 pages of notes. Stroop talked readily, not bragging, but clearly enjoying his audience, volunteering additional information and correcting himself. When contradicted, he grew red in the face and shouted.

For the first six months, Stroop built up a picture of his childhood with a disciplinarian father and a delight in military uniforms. Then came the First World War as a corporal, enjoying Prussian drilling and rides in the Carpathian Mountains. His Nazi promotions were rapid, since he shared their hatred of “verminous and subhuman” Jews, Communists and Gypsies. Heinrich Himmler became his mentor, other senior Nazis his friends. When the war came, Stroop served in Czechoslovakia, Poland, Ukraine, Greece and France. War, he told Moczarski, belonged to the higher realms of existence and was a “biological and psychological procedure of selection”. His brutality was chilling. Posted to Wiesbaden, with 130,000 men under him, he lived in a mansion stolen from a Jewish family and dressed his eight-year-old son Olaf in an SS uniform, with a loaded miniature rifle.
