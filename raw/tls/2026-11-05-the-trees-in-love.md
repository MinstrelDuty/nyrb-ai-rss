# 正文

Ovid’s story of Baucis and Philemon is perhaps the ultimate mythological example of trees harbouring emotion. The only couple to show hospitality to the gods Zeus and Hermes, who visited their village in disguise, they were granted their wish that they might die together. When the time came, Philemon was turned into an oak and Baucis into a linden tree, so that, branches intertwined, they could stay in each other’s arms forever.

The concept of trees being capable of feeling has been revisited recently by the German forester Peter Wohlleben. In his influential book _The Hidden Life of Trees_ (2015), he uses anthropomorphic language to describe how trees might be able to form friendships and experience loss and pain. Although accused by forest scientists of misrepresenting how and why trees interact, Wohlleben says his use of emotional language is an attempt to engage a public put off by accounts of nature as a coolly scientific force and eager for stories that depict it as a unified and resilient community.

“The Trees in Love” by Ashley Dukes (whose Mercury Theatre helped establish T. S. Eliot as a playwright) was published in the _TLS_ in 1942,when Ovid’s myth felt newly relevant for the way it captured values (piety, contentment, sympathy) under grave threat. Between 1938 and 1941, Bertolt Brecht wrote _The Good Woman of Setzuan_, transposing the story into a modern capitalist setting where it was almost impossible for anyone to practise Baucis and Philemon’s hospitality without being destroyed by it. And in 1972, Ernst Jünger published an essay (_Philemon und Baucis: der Tod in der mythischen und in der technischen welt;_“Philemon and Baucis: Death in the mythical and technical world”) which, drawing on the devastation of the Second World War, presented the pair as symbols of a world going up in flames. Duke’s poem, with its playful, song-like echoes of Robert Herrick, attempts instead to find hope in Ovid’s altogether happier ending.

**The Trees in Love**

“Which are both he and she”: Burton, _The Anatomy of Melancholy_

The trees in love  
Bend branches whispering  
Where no wind stirs:  
His bough to hers  
With murmuring  
Inclines like dove to dove.

Some waking air  

The scent from one,  
There is begun  
More dallying  
Between the forest pair.

Thus, their delight,  
Till, dawn illumining  
Each tree and fern,  
The lovers turn  
To mingling  
Their sense in mutual sight.

If she be hid  
By green embowering  
Too overgrown  
Then…
