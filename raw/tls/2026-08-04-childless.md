# 正文

Throughout her multi-decade career, Penelope Shuttle’s work has been informed by two incongruous touchstones: Cornwall, and the rhythms of the menstrual cycle. Cornwall’s sea-spittled cliffs have shaped the visual and emotional landscape of Shuttle’s poetry since _The Orchard Upstairs_ (1980) and remain an inspiration in recent collections such as _Lyonesse_ (2021),which is named after an ancient mythological Cornish kingdom. The elemental, mercurial imagery of Shuttle’s work could also be linked to her fascination with hormonal cycles. Shuttle suffered earlier in her life from a debilitating pre-menstrual mood disorder, and her interest in how the tides of hormones governed her life led her to write _The Wise Wound_ (1978) with her husband, the poet, writer and one-time _TLS_ staffer Peter Redgrove. Together, they considered the biological, mystical and cultural significance of menstruation, using Jungian theory to examine taboos in what became a landmark feminist text.

Shuttle brings the rhythms of the elements and the female body into “Childless”. The title perhaps gestures to the start of the menstrual cycle – the period’s bloody confirmation of childlessness. The “roots of trees / turning away from light / into darkness” feel equally bodily, recalling the hidden systems and processes of ovaries and bleeding. “Childless” is, however, materially rooted in the outside world. This is an outward-looking poem, more concerned with the vistas of nature than the dark interiorities of the human body. Shuttle’s speaker watches seabirds from her window, advancing “by inches towards October”; she picks and eats blackberries. The seasons turn. Hilary Davies said in this paper that Shuttle’s poetry “shows restraint without becoming prosaic”, and that she can “conjure and appreciate magic”. Indeed, with her berries suddenly turned “bony, equine, dark”, Shuttle eludes the prosaic entirely – these small, quotidian moments are suspended in dreamlike time and illuminated in full.

**Childless**

While picking these blackberries  
I think of the brilliant diseases  
of horses

I think of the roots of trees  
turning away from light  
into darkness, like hard lessons  
in a foreign tongue

Later, at the blue window,  
I advance by inches towards October  
The seabirds unwind their hoaxes  
in the sky

and the taste of blackberries  
turns to evening,  
bony, equine, dark

PENELOPE SHUTTLE (1980)
