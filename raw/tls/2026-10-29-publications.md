# 正文

In a note from the late 1950s or early 1960s, Elizabeth Bishop lists the “three qualities I admire in the poetry I like best”: “Accuracy, Spontaneity, Mystery”. Successful works of nonfiction have their own special qualities. To make it out of the starting gate, they need Expertise: the author needs something to say and the authority to communicate it in an effective way. To hold our attention, they need Narrative: an intelligent, perhaps surprising structure to guide us through the material. We need to believe in the reality of the world described – so you might find yourself thinking, for example, who knew that soap-making/archery/crop cycles in the reign of Charlemagne could bring us to tears? Finally, to really sing, a nonfiction author needs the ineffable element that is Style.

David Pearson’s history of bookbindings soars past the halfway point on its first page. Whatever bindings look like, he reminds us, “they all have a story to tell” and form a necessary “part of the whole”. One of his aims is to produce a guide that, “though grounded in solid scholarship” (nothing worse than academic liquid), will “be accessible to a broader audience”, and on those terms the book is a great success.

With illustrations on every other page, as well as a chronological approach and a meticulous index, it gives readers an immediate way in to the Bodleian’s collections of rare and handsomely bound books. Pearson is an amicable, unobtrusive guide. His style throughout is clear, free of jargon and well punctuated, with lively asides. It’s fun to learn that Umberto Eco missed a trick by leaving out the diligence of bookbinding monks in his otherwise well-researched novel _Il Nome della rosa_(1980; _The Name of the Rose_, 1983)_,_ and that a magnificent sixteenth-century Qur’an once belonged to Tipu Sultan (1751–99), “best known for [the] mechanical model of a tiger eating a British soldier” held by the V&A.

The non-specialist reader can treat the book almost like a pack of cards, dipping in to look at, say, an edition of John Toland’s _Life of John Milton_ from 1761, bound in red goatskin: “Milton was a particular favourite of [the publisher Thomas Hollis], not for his poetry but for his polemical writings supporting freedom of speech”. Literature, history, publishing, design: bookbindings are at once a part of and apart from these worlds.
