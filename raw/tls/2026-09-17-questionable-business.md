# 正文

In his latest book, the Nobel prize- winning economist Alvin Roth explores the morality of our modern economy, reflecting on how it underpins the type of shared ethics and trust Adam Smith wrote about in _The Theory of Moral Sentiments_. The author starts his intellectual journey in dramatic fashion by describing the death of one of his friends, the renowned psychologist Daniel Kahneman. Aged ninety, Kahneman decided that he had lived a good life and that his health was failing. And so, to avoid being a burden to his family, he made a decision – backed by his wife – to travel to Switzerland, following a final birthday meal in Paris, to use the services of Dignitas to end his life. The reason Kahneman had to travel is that this type of service is banned in his native United States.

Was the “transaction” with Dignitas, made at commercial rates, ethical? Or was it repugnant? Roth notes that opinions are sharply divided. “This book is about morally contested markets – like the market for medical aid in dying – that some people wouldn’t like to participate in but others think should be outlawed”, he writes. No clear answer can be found in an algorithm.

Of course, assisted suicide is a fairly small “business”. But other morally contested sectors are far more widespread and significant, at least in economic terms. One obvious case is the market for sex, which has been legalized via sanctioned brothels in some regions, such as Nevada and Amsterdam, clearly banned in many others, yet is often effectively tolerated in many societies, even if it is considered a “sin” under various religious laws.

Pornography is an adjacent case in point: for some people, this is an activity that represents personal freedom; for others, it is exploitative and corrosive, and should be banned. And what makes any debate doubly contentious from a “moral” perspective, Roth notes, is that there is widespread evidence that, when pornography and brothels are legalized, rates of rape in a population tend to fall. This implies that, even if people find the sex business repugnant, they might want to tolerate it if they consider rape and other forms of sexual assault to be even worse. The key point, then, is that morality can involve trade-offs – albeit ones that few people want to articulate.
