# 正文

Brazil’s president has come a long way from the bare-earth shack in which he grew up. The trajectory of Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva towards the country’s top job, a role to which he has now been elected three times, took its first turn in the early 1950s, when his family moved from a northeastern backwater to São Paulo. There, in the metal-thumping centre of Brazil’s industrial heartland, via trade union activism, Lula’s star gradually rose. A political apprenticeship served against a backdrop of military rule, left-wing radicalism, hyperinflation and economic adjustment packages resulted in Lula winning the presidency at his fourth attempt in 2002.

In the decades since, Lula’s off-on life as Brazilian president and global darling of the political left (Obama’s “my man”) has been no less eventful: attacked by radicals within his own party, pilloried by the populist right, imprisoned for corruption, widowed (for a second time), married (for a third), all the while bringing down poverty, curbing deforestation and strutting the world stage as a key face in the increasingly confident, and authoritarian, Brics bloc.

Richard Lapper, a former Marxist enthusiast who lost his love for revolution while covering South America for the _Financial Times_, skips over much of Lula’s personal and political biography. After all, the Brazilian president’s backstory is well documented. Moreover, despite his years spent reporting in Brazil, Lapper has few new secrets to divulge. Instead, his book focuses on the political and cultural ecosystem that frames Lula’s five-decade career and on his _entorno_, those around him. There’s a fascinating chapter, for instance, on the Social Democratic Party leader and president from 1995 until 2003, Fernando Henrique Cardoso, whose practical reformism arguably laid the groundwork for Brazil’s boom times during Lula’s first terms. Among others, Lula’s Communist-devotee brother Frei Chico, his bungling successor Dilma Rousseff, and his forest-defending environment minister Marina Silva receive similar treatment.

Few foreign commentators know Brasilia’s corridors of power as well as Lapper. That said, his penchant for in-depth policy discussions and the who-knows-who of politics can weigh the book down in parts. Where _Lula!_ really comes alive is at the end, when it shifts to the macro-trends that are currently defining “Lula 3”. Sections on the politics of self-interest, the rise of individualism (anathema to the old-school collectivism of Workers’ Party) and Brazil’s place in the world are fascinating.
