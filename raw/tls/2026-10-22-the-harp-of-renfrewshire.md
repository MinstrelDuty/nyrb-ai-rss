# 正文

“The only answer is to live quietly, miles away.” One of Douglas Dunn’s most striking lines, this captures the desire for rural seclusion which he once admitted in an interview: “by temperament I’m a quasi-mystical nature poet – I like nothing better than walking through the woods down by the shore … yet here I am writing … political verses. Dunn traces this tension to his early childhood. He was born in Inchinnon, a village in Renfrewshire: “because it was in sight of heavy industry and close to Glasgow, I sensed the vulnerability of the place … I don’t think it functions in my poetry so much as pervades it”.

The pull between what Dunn called “social responsibility and romantic sleep” is, according to Sean O’Brien, “brilliantly” negotiated in one of his finest poems, “The Harp of Renfrewshire”, first published in the _TLS_ in 1980 and then in _St. Kilda’s Parliament_(1981). Written after the death of his father, it uses an old map to recapture a world before Enclosure and the “rural argument” and “soft disputes” of the people who lived in it. Dialect words such as“darg” (work), “brose” (broth), and “davochs” (units of land measurement) help evoke the period but, more broadly, Dunn’s language _becomes_ the landscape as phonetic features dissolve into physical ones. The “trilled R” and “gently stroked L” mimic the rolling hills and running streams, while the “river’s burr” blurs the line between the sound of moving water and the “burr” of spoken Scots. In his rejection of political borders (“on my map is neither wall or fence”), Dunn insists that identity is not defined by who owns the land, but by who lives there and colonizes it with language.

_St. Kilda’s Parliament_(1981), in which the poem appeared, moves away from the urban focus of Dunn’s early work and looks back to his Scottish roots. The title poem was inspired by a photograph of the remote Hebridean islanders whose “parliament” (informal gatherings where the day’s tasks were distributed)stands in direct contrast to the distant, imperial, political machine of Westminster. Nonetheless, the eventual abandonment of the island is a reminder that pastoral escape is, by definition, impossible.
