# 正文

This book celebrates those “underrated stars of the English language”, verbs. Sarah L. Kaufman’s fascination with them derives from her former, longstanding role as the dance critic for the _Washington Post_. New to the job, she quickly realized that, since dance “exists only as action, vanishing moment to moment, leaving no physical trace behind”, the only way to interpret its “dynamic, wordless poetry” was through verbs.

_Verb Your Enthusiasm_ includes some lovely musings on the quicksilver, shapeshifting and intangible nature of verbs. “Boundless and new-forming”, they deal only with action and movement, leaving nothing behind – except, crucially, in the mind of the reader. “In verbs we catch the flash of life”, Kaufman writes. “We tease impressions from the inexorable roll of time.”

I would happily have read more of this kind of poetic reverie on the magic of verbs, but after a while the book settles down into being a general writing primer. Its advice – go for strong verbs over adjectives, don’t clutter up your writing, cut unnecessary words – is hardly original. Its occasionally overly breezy style (typified by that try-too-hard title) is interrupted by “style tips” and “fun facts”. But Kaufman knows her grammar, and there are clear and useful definitions of stative verbs, verbal nouns and verbs of manner (of which, we learn, there are many more in English than in other languages). There are well-chosen examples of verb-filled sentences from writers such as Sally Rooney, Virginia Woolf and the perhaps-too-obvious George Orwell. Kaufman also delivers some fighting talk on the blandness and banality of writing produced by large language models.

I am not sure, though, that I agree with this book’s blanket advice to replace low-energy stative verbs with verbs of action. Is “A storm loomed; we dashed home” necessarily better than “It looked like a storm was coming so we decided to go home in a hurry”? Surely it depends. Kaufman rightly praises the last sentence of _The Great Gatsby_ for its driving verbs: “So we beat on, boats against the current, borne back ceaselessly into the past.” But you only have to read the novel’s more sedate opening sentence (“In my younger and more vulnerable years my father gave me some advice that I’ve been turning over in my mind ever since”) to see that low-energy verbs have their place too.

This book is not…
