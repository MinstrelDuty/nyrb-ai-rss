# 正文

According to Margaret Atwood, the defining feature of Canadian literature is its preoccupation with survival, and survivors. In her aptly titled critical guide _Survival_(1972), she posits that the characters who populate the Canadian canon, including her own, are “victims”. She proposes these characters move through various stages to eventually become “creative non-victim[s]”, primarily by refusing to accept victimhood as an inevitability. “At Brute Point”, first published in the _TLS_ in October 2004 and later in Atwood’s collection _The Door_(2007)_,_ is a poem where nothing bad happens to anyone. This is not to say that the circumstances aren’t difficult – the hillside setting is one of “treacheries”. But the characters are “sprightly” as they “climb back up, / One claw and then the other”, resisting the traps of rolled ankles and defeatism.

The first novel in Atwood’s dystopian trilogy, _Oryx and Crake_ (2003), was released a year before “At Brute Point” appeared in the _TLS_, and the book’s polluted setting is also present here, though to less ominous effect. Plastic bottles sit among wildlife near the tidal pool that the couple descend towards, down an“eroded slope”. The molluscs are “small” and “few”, the glittery mica an afterthought. The bottles, which take up more space than any other objects in the surrounding terrain, are positioned as inanimate counterparts to the couple, who now merely observe a world they once participated in: they “talk about rocks” in the place where they once would have made love. Ultimately, however, this is still a love poem. In her introduction to _The Circle Game_(1998), Suzanne Buffam instructs readers to be cautious of “Atwood’s fathomless parentheticals”: here, they reveal the ageing couple quietly enjoying the familiarity of long-term companionship. They watch themselves back as if on a film reel, nudging and winking. “(Could it be that we are the old people / already? / Surely not. / Not with such hats.)”

**At Brute Point**

The old people descend the hill in slow motion.  
It’s a windy hill,  
a hill of treacheries and pebbles,  
and twisted ankles.

One has a stick, one not.  
Their clothing is bizarre,  
though wash-and-wear.

Foot over foot they go,  
Down the eroded slope,  
flapping like sails.  
They want to get down to the ocean,  
and they accomplish this.

(Could it be that we are the old people  
already?  
Surely not.  
Not with such hats.)

We may have been here before;  
at least it looks familiar,  
but we…
