# 正文

Bilingual pairs of poems, which are intended to be read and understood together rather than as alternatives, appear with some frequency in seventeenth-century books and manuscripts. One of the best-known pairs comprises Andrew Marvell’s “The Garden” (1681), an English-language work, and his Latin poem “Hortus”, published in the _TLS_ in this translation by Edmund Blunden in 1955. “Hortus”– although a classical ode in the manner of Horace– is not a direct imitation of a specific poem but rather a dispassionate tribute to the form. It is a fine replica of pagan idealism from which, however, writer and speaker seem curiously absent.

This may seem odd until we consider the similarities and differences between the poem and its English counterpart. While “Hortus” reminds us of the pastoral tradition to which “The Garden” belongs, it also seems radically incomplete without the spiritual resonance Marvell brings to it in English. For example, a brief comparison of the two poems reveals that although the first two sections of “Hortus” correspond roughly to verses one and two of “The Garden”, sections three, five and six, full of historical and mythological reference, are missing. Instead, Marvell moves from the repudiation of public ambition with which he begins in “Hortus” (What power so sways the hearts of human-kind / That with the laurel, palm, mere grass they bind / Their brows”) to delight. We discover a kind of sensuous Eden, and from there, salvation and spiritual ecstasy. Any reader familiar with “The Garden” who senses this absence in “Hortus” is in good company: the printer of the poem’s first folio volume wrote _desunt multa_: “many things are lacking”, or, perhaps, “there should be more to it than this”.

**Hortus**

_It was noted by Sir Herbert Grierson that Andrew Marvell’s “The Garden”, though obviously connected with his “Hortus” in Latin hexameters, is not a translation of that piece. The following is an attempt at a plain version of “Hortus”._

What power so sways the hearts of human-kind  
That with the laurel, palm, mere grass they bind  
Their brows and but one tree (almost) crowns all  
Their mighty deeds – such grudging coronal  
Leaves temples bear; meanwhile together move  
For Quiet’s garlands, flowers to be inwove  
Of every kind, and with them all the grove.

Sweet spirit, quiet, thee I clasp, and thee  
Sister of Quiet, our Simplicity.  
Through the great city, through the stately church,  
The courts of Kings, long…
