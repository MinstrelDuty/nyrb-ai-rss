# 正文

_Treasure Island_, _Dr Jekyll and Mr Hyde_ and _Kidnapped_ are the books for which Robert Louis Stevenson (1850–94) is remembered today. Mostly forgotten are his thirty-five volumes of fiction, poetry, letters, essays, history, criticism and travel. “There has never been a more headlong fall in a writer’s reputation than there was in Stevenson’s after his death”, Leonard Woolf observed.

Stevenson’s life, Glenda Norquay writes, was “impossibly romantic”. There was the sickly childhood in Edinburgh, the loss of faith, the travels through Europe, the emergence as a bohemian essayist in the 1870s, the success as a popular novelist in the 1880s, the debates on literature with Henry James and the position as a luminary of letters that he had attained by the 1890s. He was almost continuously ill, but remained peripatetic, jumping from Edinburgh to Paris and from London to Samoa, where he spent his last years writing feverishly until a cerebral haemorrhage killed him at the age of forty-four.

Norquay seems to have read everything by or about Stevenson, and makes the case well for works such as the unfinished novel _Weir of Hermiston_ and the playful essays of _Virginibus Puerisque_. She is especially alert to the roles played by the various women in his life: his childhood nurse, Cummy; his first love, Fanny Sitwell; his future wife, Fanny Osbourne. As a biography, this is an admirable book. It relies more on Stevenson’s own writings than on previous biographies, of which there are several, and its distinguishing merit is brevity, describing in 170 pages a life that Claire Harman, for example, described in several hundred.

A problem arises as soon as Norquay departs from biography into criticism, from concrete facts into interpretation. It takes a paragraph simply to say that the child is father of the man:

As he was moving on from the world of child’s play into the more complicated dynamics of adulthood, these first years of childhood and adolescence provided him with memories – of friends, landscapes and activities – that would become both part of his writing and objects of study.
