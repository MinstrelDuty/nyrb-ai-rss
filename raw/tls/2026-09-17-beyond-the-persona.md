# 正文

Sylvia Plath wrote her first poem when she was five years old, and her last poem a week before her suicide at the age of thirty. In the years between, she honed her craft relentlessly, driven by her conviction in her calling – perhaps the surest belief she ever possessed. In _The Bell Jar_(1963), Esther Greenwood famously compares her myriad future career paths to the branches of a fig tree. But Plath herself experienced no such ambivalence. She was steely in her determination to become, as she wrote in her journal, “The Poetess of America”.

From a young age, Plath was ambitious, disciplined and diligent about her work. As a child, she mastered limericks, then iambic and dactylic meter. In high school and college, she perfected sonnets and villanelles. She embraced the work of Sara Teasdale, Edna St Vincent Millay, Dylan Thomas and W. B. Yeats before moving on to W. H. Auden, D. H. Lawrence, Wallace Stevens and Marianne Moore. She also absorbed the influences and examples of her contemporaries Anne Sexton, Adrienne Rich and Robert Lowell, and was inevitably also influenced by Ted Hughes, whom she met at Cambridge and married in 1956. For Plath, Hughes was more than a husband – he was an ally and creative partner who took her writing seriously. Plath had discovered that such men were rare in Cold War America, a nation obsessed with traditional gender roles, where women’s artistic ambitions were discouraged. Plath and Hughes understood each other’s genius from the start, and challenged each other to write better poems. Plath also gave Hughes practical help by becoming his agent and secretary, then a depressingly common role for writers’ wives – even wives who were writers themselves. Hughes might never have published _The Hawk in the Rain_(1957), his first masterpiece, if Plath had not entered his manuscript in a prestigious contest run by Harper and Brothers. (She decided not to enter her own unpublished poetry collection.) He won, and the prize kick-started his career. Hughes called Plath his “secretary in chief”, and later suggested, in his _Birthday Letters_ (1998) poem “Ouija”, that if not for her belief in him, “I’d be fishing off a rock / In Western Australia”.
