# 正文

“I hope this book provides a makeover for some species that could use one”, writes Marlene Zuk, referring to those animals we view as living on the margins, often encroaching on our lives and occupying “an uneasy place in human thought”.

She begins with raccoons, gulls, cowbirds and common mynas before extolling the virtues of rats and cockroaches. Along the way, we encounter a good deal of interesting research, which Zuk renders pleasingly accessible to readers from an engaged but non-scientific background, without flattening complexity. Are urban raccoons, dining out on discarded takeaways, less healthy than their rural counterparts? How do young cowbirds (raised, like cuckoos, by parents of an entirely different species) recognize other cowbirds? What can laughing rats tell us about the origins of humour? And – stretching the idea of a “makeover” somewhat – have snakes that spit venom evolved to disable great apes like ourselves by blinding them?

The reasoning behind Zuk’s choice of subjects, however, is haphazard. “Most of them are North American, mainly because that’s the part of the world with which I’m most familiar, but several global characters will be included”, she writes. “In some chapters I consider just one species, in others, an entire group like rats or snakes.” At times, her categorization of “outsider animals” felt confused. “Raccoons can become tame, but they haven’t been domesticated, and no outsider animal has been”, she writes, before devoting a chapter to rats – which, in their lab and indeed pet forms, are, as she herself notes, among the clearest examples of domestication you can find.

As an evolutionary biologist, Zuk is at her strongest when she writes about the forces of evolution, but the book lacks the strength of argument found in titles such as Menno Schilthuizen’s captivating _Darwin Comes to Town_– a whistle-stop tour of how the urban environment is changing species across the globe – or the intellectual ambition of Christine Webb’s _The Arrogant Ape_, which challenges our long-held belief in human superiority over other species.

Zuk’s book, then, reads more like a cabinet of curiosities. I left with a newfound appreciation for a handful of apparently extraordinary creatures – but unconvinced that raccoons, cabbage white butterflies and coyotes belong in quite the same story.
