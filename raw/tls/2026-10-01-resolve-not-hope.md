# 正文

“War is a myopic seamstress”, Iya Kiva wrote in 2021, a year before the start of Russia’s full-scale invasion of Ukraine, “who keeps cutting language after language from our tongue”. These lines, beautifully translated into English by Amelia Glaser and Yuliya Ilchuk, are especially resonant for those who, like Kiva, know both Ukrainian, the language in which they were composed, and Russian, the language the poet grew up speaking in eastern Ukraine, but in which she now refuses to write. Russian uses the same word for “tongue” and “language”, _yazyk_, while in Ukrainian the words are different, _yazyk_ and _mova_ respectively. The very expression of Kiva’s thought in Ukrainian enacts war’s surgery, severing her mother tongue from her own tongue.

As Glaser explains in her helpful introduction to _Silence Dressed in Cyrillic Letters_, a generous trilingual selection of Kiva’s poems, the poet “shifted toward writing in Ukrainian after the outbreak of the Donbas war in 2014”. Moreover, although the poems were written in Ukrainian and Russian, they include snippets of Polish and Belarusian: “Language, translation, and occasional slippages are ways of mapping”, the translators point out, “the cultural landscape of Ukraine, a country still coming to own its rich and diverse heritage”.

This heritage is intricately scarred. A daughter of Russophone parents, Kiva has twice been displaced by Russian aggression – first to Kyiv, then to Lviv, which was, until 1939, under Polish rule. “You read the inscriptions in Polish”, she writes in Ukrainian about a visit to a cemetery in Lviv,

hear the hum of Polish tourists  
a tomb a tomb grobowiec  
searching for somebody’s death in Polish  
you search for somebody’s death in Ukrainian  
your ancestors could be buried here  
were they not forced to be echoes  
to roam Donbas searching for death in Russian

Death, in this book, is never hard to find. In a Ukrainian poem dated April 3, 2022, “death’s iron water jingles beneath your eyelids”. In one written the following day, refugees at a train station, with their “death faces [watching] empty lives”, try to ward it off with empty threats:

just one step, death, and we’ll eat you for dinner  
our rusty tin-can lives will break your teeth  
just one step, death, and you’ll stay at this table  
like a cracked tray strewn with free people’s hair
