# 正文

Of all the historic moments surrounding the end of the Cold War, the afternoon of August 21, 1991, in Moscow was central, not least for its symbolism. On the third grim day after communist hardliners attempted to prop up the tottering USSR by arresting the reformist Soviet leader, Mikhail Gorbachev, and staging a _coup d’état_, I was standing among the protesters and their flimsy barricades near the so-called White House. The office tower that housed the Russian Soviet republic’s government was now serving as a base of operations for the resistance. Suddenly, shouts came from a few small groups huddled around radios, announcing that the plotters had fled the city. As the tension lifted, rain clouds that had been blanketing the city parted as if by miracle, and the sun shone down on the beginning of a tremendous celebration.

In _The August Coup: The destruction of the Soviet Union and the making of new Russia 1985-1991,_ the veteran historian Robert Service provides critical context for what led up to the coup and how it unfolded. Beginning with Gorbachev’s rise to the top of the Communist Party in 1985, he shows that, although the ensuing reforms aimed to modernize the USSR by loosening economic restrictions and enabling free speech, they also prompted conflicts over the pace and scale of a transition to capitalism and, crucially, a new union treaty. Service vividly details how the plot’s eight ringleaders tried to turn back the clock, despite a mounting financial crisis and the emergence of independence movements in some of the Soviet Union’s fifteen republics. The abortive putsch ultimately discredited Gorbachev and opened the way for his main rival – the Russian republic’s leader and tank-striding resistance hero, Boris Yeltsin – to launch an era of radical democratization and free-market reform in a newly independent Russia.

Service’s deep perspective as a scholar and first-hand Russia observer makes _The August Coup_ a valuable addition to the wide-ranging literature about the Soviet collapse. The author of biographies of Lenin, Trotsky and Stalin, he presents a nuanced account of the main characters and draws on many sources, including testimony from an official investigation into the coup attempt. Of the often drunk and sometimes depressive Yeltsin, and the waffling Gorbachev, he writes, “I recall having regarded both… as exceptional leaders even though each had unmistakable flaws.”
