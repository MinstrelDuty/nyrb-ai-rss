# 正文

Georgia’s Menshevik-led first republic was, Stephen F. Jones writes in this comprehensive history, an “oddity”. From 1918 to 1921, this mostly peasant country on the periphery of Europe saw the emergence of a parliamentary democracy despite having had no meaningful history of statehood in modern times.

Jones covers this three-year story with a sure grasp of both the changing international context and local detail. This should come as no surprise: the book is the culmination of decades of work on Georgia and a sequel to his volume _Socialism in Georgian Colours: The European road to social democracy, 1883-1917, published in 2005_. Jones’s mastery of the Georgian language and expert navigation of the country’s archives make _The First Social Democracy_ an indispensable work.

The title is a deliberate lure to the general reader, but also misleading. The Georgian republic never managed to establish itself as a functioning state, let alone implement social democratic policies. Fighting for its life from the start, it could not form a working economy, draw recognized borders or receive _de jure_ recognition from the great powers of the age.

The would-be state was born in May 1918 more or less by accident. When the Russian Empire collapsed, all of its provinces south of the Caucasus Mountains were left ungoverned. The Georgians tried to build a Transcaucasian federation with Armenia and Azerbaijan, but that dissolved as each national group responded differently to the threat of invasion by the Ottoman Empire, at which point Georgia decided to go it alone.

British efforts to contain the spread of Bolshevism were divided. Lord Curzon, the foreign secretary, made the case that the South Caucasus was a “middle wall” worth defending against Russian expansionism. But this did little to change the prevailing view that the Reds could best be opposed through support of the Whites, specifically General Anton Denikin’s Volunteer Army. In hindsight, it is difficult not to conclude that British resources would have been better spent on the fragile Georgian republic.

Some parts of this history have a familiar ring for the modern reader, as when the new Georgian authorities brutally suppressed Abkhaz and Ossetian attempts to win autonomy or unite with their ethnic kin, interpreting these as Bolshevik-backed efforts to destroy the new state. Jones rather downplays the awfulness of this story by telling it mainly from Georgian sources.
