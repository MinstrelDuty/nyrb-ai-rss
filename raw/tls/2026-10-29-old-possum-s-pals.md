# 正文

N _octes Binanianae_ (1939) – “Bina Nights” – comprises the proceedings, and the proceeds, of a poetry symposium held over a period of several months between 1936 and 1939 by T. S. Eliot and three of his dearest friends: his publishing colleagues Geoffrey Faber and Frank Vigor Morley, and their younger associate, the editor, critic and bibliographer John Hayward. Since Hayward was confined by illness to a wheelchair – he could not even leave his apartment and get onto the street without one or more assistants to manhandle it – the friends would spontaneously gather from time to time for a drinking and poetry party at Hayward’s large rented room not far from Gloucester Road in South Kensington. Eliot spoke appreciatively of such gatherings with his coterie as “the Kensington Kulture Club”.

The full edition of _Noctes Binanianae_ is soon to be published on [the website of the Eliot Estate](https://tseliot.com/). To date, Eliot’s contributions to this unitary volume of nonsense verses have been extrapolated, with reports of the other contributions, in _The Poems of T. S. Eliot_, edited by Christopher Ricks and Jim McCue. Yet Eliot himself knew that _Noctes Binanianae_ was a collaborative work, not to be dissevered; he scarcely thought to print his own verses separately from the group production. The full text of _Noctes Binanianae_ – this interinanimated series of light verses, centos and pastiches – provides a grand opportunity for his readers, including devotees of _Old Possum’s Bookof Practical Cats_ and the worldwide audience for the musical _Cats_, to appreciate to a fuller extent his comic genius – his kindliness, his playfulness, his mischievousness – with his bantering verses put back into their full context, their original occasion, alongside the provocative rejoinders of the other players. Much recent literary-critical labour has gone towards assessing Eliot as mimic, parodist, pasticheur and dandy – his sources, his indebtedness, his creative transformations – but few readers have been able to estimate the true scale of his achievements in light verse. Hayward, who was one of Eliot’s longest-standing friends, aptly described him as:

a satirical wit … a connoisseur of the ridiculous, an ironical commentator on human folly, a lover of allusive quotation (particularly from the dicta of Sherlock Holmes and other eminent detectives), and a passionate observer of what may be called the _Bouvard et Pécuchet_ way of life.
