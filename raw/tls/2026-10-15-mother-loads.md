# 正文

Where does motherhood fit in the women’s movement? Are pregnancy, birth and babies part of the main feminist argument that women should have the same rights and freedoms as men? Or do these bodily experiences belong to some other agenda?

Two new books by Lara Feigel and Elinor Cleghorn, both of whom are authors and mothers, answer these questions in contrasting ways. Feigel’s _Custody: The secret history of mothers_ is inspired by her dispute with her ex-husband, and uses the true stories of seven famous women who fought for custody of their children to make an argument about sexism and family law. Cleghorn’s scope is broader. _A Woman’s Work: Reclaiming the radical history of mothering_ blends the history of mothering and midwifery with polemic on women’s reproductive rights today.

Feigel announces straightaway her intention to confront the issue of whether “celebrating women as care-givers returns us to essentialist, patriarchal ideas about gender”. Like Rachel Cusk, who also wrote about the bitter struggle over children that followed her divorce, Feigel found herself in visceral revolt against the idea that her children’s lives could be fairly divided up. All the women in her book asserted what she calls “a raw cry of ownership”, and her emotional investment in the topic gives instant force to her inquiries.

These start in the nineteenth century with the aristocratic social reformer Caroline Norton, whose contribution to the early Victorian women’s movement ought to be better known. Caroline was a Sheridan, granddaughter of the playwright, and her Whiggish views were one reason why her marriage, at the age of nineteen, to the Tory MP George Norton, was a disaster. He was physically abusive and in 1836, after they separated, brought a court case against the prime minister, Lord Melbourne, accusing him of adultery, or “criminal conversation”, with his wife. Since married women had no legal standing, Caroline had no voice in the case; only Melbourne had the right to a defence. It was, Feigel drily says, as though he “was a thief who had stolen George’s horse”.

Norton lost, but Caroline’s reputation was savaged and, having taken charge of their three young sons, as he was entitled to, he refused to let her see them.
