# 正文

On June 8, 1708, a Spanish galleon sank off the coast of present-day Colombia. The _San José_, which had been ambushed by a British squadron, plummeted in minutes, sending billions of dollars’ worth of gold, silver and jewels onto the Caribbean floor.

Dubbed “the Holy Grail of shipwrecks”, the _San José_ developed a mythical reputation among treasure hunters and marine archaeologists, and became part of the lore of the Colombian Caribbean. In Gabriel García Márquez’s novel _Love in the Time of Cholera_(1985), the romantic hero, Florentino Ariza, studies the navigation charts of Spanish galleons and tries to find the wreck with the help of a twelve-year-old amateur diver.

The _San José_ was discovered in 2015 by a man with Florentino Ariza’s passion, plus the support of the surveyors who found the Titanic. Roger Dooley, an American Cuban marine archaeologist who had received funds from a British hedge-fund manager, had pursued clues in maritime archives for more than thirty years. Dooley, whose wife is a well-connected Colombian, secured permission to search for the wreck after he insisted that the country’s then president, Juan Manuel Santos, look at his annotated antique map.

Claims to the _San José_’s treasure came from across the world. The dispute included the Colombian state, Dooley’s British patron and a US company that had previously searched the site (and then claimed it had been hoodwinked). The Spanish government demanded its imperial property; and indigenous people in Peru and Bolivia, whose forebears had been forced to extract the minerals from their ancestral lands, also launched petitions.

In _Neptune’s Fortune_, Julian Sancton, author of _Madhouse at the End of the Earth_(2021), which described Adrien de Gerlache’s perilous expedition to Antarctica in 1897, tells the story of the _San José_ from its final voyage to its discovery and afterlife. _Neptune’s Fortune_ is also a portrait of Dooley – from his youth watching Fidel Castro preparing late-night food in the kitchen of the Havana Hilton to the fulfilment of his life’s dream and his wait for public recognition. However, though Sancton takes the story up to 2025, most of the striking details can be read in a longform article that he wrote three years earlier for _Vanity Fair_.
