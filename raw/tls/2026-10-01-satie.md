# 正文

It has been said that listening to Erik Satie’s _Trois Gymnopédies_ is like looking at the same sculpture from three different angles. The bassline – a single note followed by a two-beat chord, repeated in almost every measure – provides just enough momentum to simulate a slow walk. But rather than creating a sense of forward movement through sequences that anticipate a return to the home chord, Satie’s melodies rarely resolve. The music is not so much a series of ideas as a physical space, one where storytelling is replaced with architectural stillness.

For the music critic Constant Lambert, Satie’s habit of writing pieces in groups of three (_\_Trois Gymnopédies\__, _Trois Gnossiennes_, _Trois Morceaux en forme de poire_) “took the place in his art of dramatic development”. In _Erik Satie: Three Piece Suite_ (2025), Ian Penman even wonders whether Satie’s predilection for the number three is not fundamentally theological. Satie’s friend Debussy saw in his attachment to the principle of ritual repetition evidence of his early fascination with plainsong: he was “a gentle medieval musician who has somehow strayed into our century”. But Satie’s first job was as a pianist at Le Chat Noir, a nightclub in Montmartre. As Penman reminds us, three is also at the heart of all dialectic: “Thesis: high classical tradition. Antithesis: low cabaret songs. Synthesis: Erik Satie.”

In his poem “Satie”, first published in the _TLS_ in 1989 and then in _Waiting for the Storm_ (1990), the Glasgow-born poet, artist and musician Gerald Mangan respects Satie’s fondness for threes: he uses tercets, a three-beat anapaestic foot and terza rima. But he also captures something of Satie’s obsessive routine: travelling into Paris from his tiny room in the suburbs, calling at his usual café where ‘”he clock has stopped to admire its reflection”, noticing the infinite reduplication of a mirror facing a mirrored wall “frame within frame”. And it ends, of course, exactly where it started, with a barman opening up for the day “like the river changing its waters again”, different and the same.

**Satie**

It’s the hour when blue becomes mother-of-pearl.  
Chairs are still up-ended on tables,  
and pigeons are gurgling on window-sills,

when he stops for coffee at the usual place.  
Vendors are hanging out waterfalls  
of lace and polishing cairns of apples.

He’s pedalled from his suburb into town  
with his brolly rolled, at a tightrope pace.  
Rain or shine,…
