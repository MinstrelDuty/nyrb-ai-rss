# 正文

“There is a hole at the bottom of the lake where the movies come from,” the reclusive former scream queen Billy Presley announces in Jane Schoenbrun’s newest film, *Teenage Sex and Death at Camp Miasma*. Maybe so—Schoenbrun’s astonishing creative output over the past several years certainly suggests some such occult source. In addition to three feature films—*We’re All Going to the World’s Fair* (2021), *I Saw the TV Glow* (2024), and *Teenage Sex*—Schoenbrun has now written a nearly six-hundred-page novel, *Public Access Afterworld*. Individually, each work is, to varying degrees, a success. Taken together, they form the kind of ambitious, labyrinthine corpus that most artists require a lifetime to create.[\*](#fn-*)

Schoenbrun’s three features, sometimes referred to as the “screen trilogy,” all involve strikingly similar characters and plot elements that are revisited to wildly different tonal effect: a depressed, dark-haired young woman with a subdued demeanor and wide, shattered eyes disappears into a fantasy world of uncertain epistemic status, closely observed by a masculine figure whose motivations are difficult to parse.

In *We’re All Going to the World’s Fair*,the young woman is a teenage girl named Casey, and the world is a collaborative online horror “challenge,” an ostensibly occult ritual played out via shared YouTube videos and Skype calls. Casey, who lives alone with her father, catches the attention of JLB, an adult man who participates in the game. (In 2018 Schoenbrun made a documentary, *A Self-Induced Hallucination*, consisting entirely of stitched-together clips from YouTube inspired by the horror phenomenon Slender Man, and *World’s Fair* demonstrates a correspondingly intimate knowledge of the messy DIY landscape of online horror, as well as the quirky, creative, youthful community that gave rise to it.) Structured and marketed as a horror movie, as all of Schoenbrun’s films have been, *World’s Fair* is the one that comes closest to actually meeting the demands of the genre: a clean setup, building tension, gross-out moments, the occasional jump scare. Yet the primary emotion the film evokes is not fear but the crushing loneliness, akin to grief, that is the hallmark of emotional neglect.

In *I Saw the TV Glow*, meanwhile, the young woman is Maddy, and the fantasy world is a TV show she loves called *The Pink Opaque*. Her observer is a teenage schoolmate named Owen, and his motives are less ominous than JLB’s but in some ways equally, well, opaque—he desires Maddy’s friendship, but with the yearning, painful intensity of a teenage crush. Their relationship is complicated by the fact that Maddy is queer, while Owen does not understand—or cannot accept—himself as trans. Via the fantasy world of *The Pink Opaque*,Maddy and Owen can relate to each other as two women, with a shared, straightforward destiny; in the real world, that’s too painful for Owen to contemplate. He tells Maddy, “When I think about that stuff [i.e., sex] it feels like someone took a shovel and dug out all my insides, and I know there’s nothing in there, but I’m still too nervous to open myself up and check.”

The same month *The Pink Opaque* is canceled, Maddy runs away, and Owen is too frightened to follow her. Maddy returns, years later, to make an astonishing claim: *The Pink Opaque* is real, and it’s *this* world—the world of the suburbs, of jobs and family and grinding sameness—that is the illusion. She entreats Owen to follow her into *The Pink Opaque*, but again he refuses, caught by both his fear and his allegiance to the people he’d be forced to abandon. He is left only with a crushing despair and fantasies of what could have been:

> What if she was right?… What if I really was someone else? Someone beautiful and powerful? Someone buried alive and suffocating to death, very far away, on the other side of a television screen?

The most straightforward reading of the movie is one in which *The Pink Opaque* stands in for the possibility of authenticity: transition, queerness, heroism, and art as the true reality, counterposed with the stifling lie of the conventional, closeted life. Both *World’s Fair* and *TV Glow*, though studded with flashes of beauty and wit, are on the whole emotionally punishing—even devastating—films. They mimic the slippery, dissociative fugue states in which their protagonists are trapped: all the characters are deeply isolated and, despite their best efforts at connection, can’t help talking past one another. The rules always seem to be shifting, the passage of time feels muddy and irrational, and it’s hard to tell what’s real.

*Teenage Sex and Death at Camp Miasma*,Schoenbrun’s most recent film, at first seems to break that mold. The main characters are two women: Gillian Anderson’s Billy Presley and Hannah Einbinder’s Kris Williams, an up-and-coming queer filmmaker who visits Billy at her remote movie-set-turned-country-house. The film is joyful, warm, funny, extremely sexy, and endlessly self-referential: a horror fan’s delight, minus any actual scares. Watching it, one is tempted to imagine Kris’s life as the one Maddy might have had after she escaped from the suburbs. No longer isolated or punished for her queerness, she is instead celebrated for it. Despite having made only one tiny art house film (a remake of *Psycho* “from the perspective of the shower curtain”), she’s been hired to reboot the “zombie IP” of the once popular, now problematic *Camp Miasma* slasher franchise—that is, to make it acceptably woke. She has an open relationship with her girlfriend, who is, in turn, dating a bisexual man named Thor. Her mission is straightforward, and her enemies (sexism, racism, transphobia) clearly identifiable. The only problem is…none of it feels quite real.

Enter, once again, a dark-haired young woman with wide, shattered eyes. This time she is the character played by a young Billy Presley (Amanda Fix), the protagonist of the first *Camp Miasma* film, who is stalked and murdered by its recurring villain, Little Death. Little Death is the ghost of a child who was bullied and then drowned by other campers because of his ambiguous gender, and the bond between him and Billy is heightened, and made strange, by the fact that though Little Death murders her, her experience of the encounter is one of erotic fulfillment: “little death” (*la petite mort*) is a euphemism for “orgasm,” and Billy has her first one while imagining herself through the eyes of Little Death as he kills her character. The wide-eyed girl and the one who obsessively watches her: there they are again, that mysterious pair.

Toward the end of the film, on a call with a bunch of soulless movie execs, Kris tries to explain what she’s discovered from Billy, the truth of *Camp Miasma*: that though the filmmakers thought they invented Little Death, in fact Little Death is the one who created them. “You all are characters in a movie!” she cries. “We are a movie for him to edit, and he has final cut.” It’s the same message that Maddy fought her way back to Owen to deliver: your understanding of the world is upside down; your reality is fantasy; the fantasy is realer than real.

At the climax (ha) of the movie, after Kris moves through a dreamlike scenario in which Little Death slaughters all the people who irritated her, the adult Billy gently but firmly coaxes Kris through a sexual encounter in which Kris lets go of her hang-ups and allows herself to experience the desire underneath the shame. Then Little Death turns his murderous gaze (and weapon) on Billy and Kris. The film suggests that feelings that begin in trauma—whether discomfort with the body you were born in or an imagined identification with the person who has harmed you—can be revisited and reclaimed in fantasy. This discovery, though frightening at first, ultimately allows Billy and Kris the freedom to slip back and forth between worlds at will, playfully weaving fantasy and reality together in a way that brings them genuine happiness.

For all the blood and guts and screaming, *Teenage Sex* is an exuberant, life-affirming film, a joyful celebration of queer sexuality and a sharp-eyed but loving tribute to the imperfect cultural ephemera that have helped shape it. Beneath the pop sensibility, there is a serious argument about the primacy of desire, the way it bursts through logical, moral, and aesthetic barriers. The body pushes beyond what the mind considers acceptable, overwhelming and dissolving the thinking self, and bringing with it a capacity for renewal, generation, and rebirth. Desire always comes first; the intellect trails behind it like a shadow. And only when the mind surrenders to the body, and the critic to the artist, can satisfaction be achieved—and new art be born.

The trilogy, it seems, has a happy ending. But then here comes *Public Access Afterworld*, throwing everything into confusion, like Jason surfacing to drag the final girl underwater right before the credits roll in *Friday the 13th*.

*Public Access Afterworld* is closer in tone to *Teenage Sex* than to Schoenbrun’s previous films. It’s a fast-paced, enjoyable read, full of twists and jokes; like all of Schoenbrun’s work, it has a close, affectionate, reference-dense relationship to the horror genre yet contains few outright scares. The premise is now almost uncannily familiar: it is 2009, and a teenage girl, Erin Morrison, becomes obsessed with the idea of a “secret” TV channel called Public Access Afterworld, rumored to broadcast beneath the static of ordinary television. In the fall of her senior year, Erin disappears into the world of the show, pursued by her friend and occasional lover, David Sawyer. As the years go by, David pulls a wide assortment of characters into the search, uncovering the deep history of the strange world into which Erin has vanished. All the familiar elements are here: disappearing teenage girls, fantasy worlds of uncertain status, dissociation, nostalgia, obsession.

Schoenbrun’s films are notable for their impeccable pop cultural replicas, the accuracy of their worlds-within-worlds. The realism with which Schoenbrun depicts this fake pop culture is a crucial, destabilizing element of the experience; the false and the real weave together so tightly that it’s hard to keep hold of any meaningful difference between the two. Schoenbrun’s work demonstrates how deadening this timeless, frictionless reproduction of ephemeral culture can be (the impossibility of finding anything new to say about the millionth iteration of the *Camp Miasma* franchise is a running joke in *Teenage Sex*) while remaining attuned to the freedom it offers—the alluring promise of endless play.

As a novelist, Schoenbrun has moments of equally virtuosic world-building.The pop culture trailhead that opens into the world of *Public Access Afterworld* reads like a slice of gruesome Internet ephemera, something you could easily convince yourself you’d stayed up late reading about one night on Wikipedia and then forgotten:

> Local weatherman Ray “Can You Say Sunshine” Davino killed himself live on air at 5:35 P.M. on September 3, 1988, on Dallas–Fort Worth ABC Channel 8 WFAA, pointing a gun at his head in the middle of the five-day forecast, rambling for forty-eight seconds in a half-coherent final monologue about the absurdity of reality, then pulling the trigger as if it was all part of his nightly routine.

This path also winds through several “forgotten” episodes of nonexistent trashy television that Schoenbrun captures with deadpan accuracy, as though tuning into the reality of a universe closely adjacent to ours:

> Do *you* remember the TV show *Short Stack*?…

> *Short Stack* was a family sitcom that aired for eight seasons on ABC from 1977 to 1985, Saturday nights at 8:30 Eastern / 7:30 Central….

> Maybe you remember Lil’ Angus, the show’s breakout fan favorite character….

> If you’re still drawing a blank on *Short Stack*, maybe this will jog your memory: “*I dono about that!*”

> Lil Angus’s iconic catchphrase, which you had to live under a rock not to hear ten times a day during the show’s initial run.

Navigating the elaborate alternate universe of pop culture Schoenbrun has constructed is pleasurably uncanny, a bit like visiting a wax museum of the entire world.

But much of the emotional power of Schoenbrun’s filmmaking has gone missing. Instead of concentrating on a single, intense relationship, as the films do, *Public Access Afterworld* takes up the perspectives of several different characters, in multiple timelines. The book is presented as a series of TV “episodes” contained within a frame narrative. In the opening pages, an unnamed narrator says they heard the episodes told, “like campfire stories,” by someone going by the name David Sawyer, a fellow patient at a youth rehabilitation center who “claimed there was a fake TV show stuck inside their brain, and that they desperately needed to get it out.” And indeed, in a note included in advance copies of the book, Schoenbrun says that they initially conceived and wrote it as “1,600 pages of television screenplays,” before rewriting it as a novel when they realized that such a show would never get made. Whether this is true or another metafictional game, there is an action-packed quality to the book, a privileging of plot over character, that does over time start to feel wearying. After all, a novel is not a summary of a TV show; it has its own pleasures. *Public Access Afterworld* has these, too, but one has to look a bit harder to find them.

After focusing on Erin and David in the first “episode,” Schoenbrun jumps ahead to the present day and introduces Bethany Peters, “a twenty-seven-year-old trans girl playing chicken with the poverty line down in Texas,” who is employed as a content moderator for a YouTube-esque tech company called GlobalVill. The narrator declares, “Every TV show has a hero. *Buffy* had Buffy, *Xena* had Xena, and *Dr. Quinn, Medicine Woman* had Dr. Quinn, Medicine Woman. My TV show has Bethany Peters.” Bethany is special, and we know she is special, not only because the narrator tells us so but because the plot revolves around her. She has unique powers, everyone is after her, and everyone is also a little bit in love with her. As the novel progresses, its simplistic morality starts to feel like fan fiction: Bethany is the chosen one, the “receiver,” possessing an inherent specialness. She is literally “one in ten billion,” the person on whom the survival of the entire universe depends. David Sawyer, meanwhile, unable to let go of his obsessive pursuit of Erin, becomes one of the most notorious serial killers in American history and ultimately makes the FBI’s Ten Most Wanted list.

It’s almost too much—that is, until you remember that the “episodes” are being recounted by someone named David Sawyer, “the same name as one of the characters in their stories.” David the storyteller and David the character are not the same person. We know this in part because David the storyteller, the narrator implies, has transitioned (they have a new name and use they/them pronouns). David the character, however, never does. You might even say that telling this story is in part what *allows* David the storyteller to transition; after telling it, they get the TV show out of their head and disappear.

One way of interpreting the novel, then, is that in order to survive, David has to tell themself a story, one in which they’re divided neatly into two parts: a bad, selfish cis straight man who causes harm to the people he loves, and a heroic, beloved, too-perfect trans woman. Both the vilification of David and the idolization of Bethany have a slightly flat, unreal quality, which is what makes the story feel more like television than real life. And yet through this crack in reality something new emerges. The story serves its purpose. David the storyteller slips into a new reality, and is free.

Toward the end of the novel, Bethany wakes up in Public Access Afterworld, which takes the form of a kind of supernatural asylum. She sees, written on her door, the name “David Sawyer,” before it shifts and is replaced by her old pre-transition name. Recalling her childhood, she remembers, pre-transition, being a small boy with an “imaginary friend” named Bethany. Depressed and hospitalized, the child decides to make Bethany “real”—to become Bethany: “*[Bethany] was brave and strong and resolved, just like she had always been when she was someone else’s imaginary friend. And she was beautiful. And she was real.*” But in return Bethany has to exile the child, to make him as “imaginary” as Bethany once was:

> *As long as Bethany was alive herself, he still would be as well, scared and shattered in his childhood bedroom. So he lived with her. And now he was the imaginary one inside her head. And yet she knew he was still very real at the same time.*

To create anything new, we have to pull it out of the realm of the imagination: every work of art, every invention, every new identity, every major life choice must be conceived before it can be made real. In that sense, imagination precedes reality: that is why it’s realer than real. But the relationship between the real and the imaginary is not a simple one. The boundary is porous and must be carefully patrolled.

In *I Saw the TV Glow*, Maddy slips into *The Pink Opaque* while Owen remains, suffocating and miserable, in the suburbs. He is her shadow. He is her fantasy as much as she is his—the story she tells herself about how terrible her life would have been had she never escaped. She needs him, and he needs her; they are bound together, and the tension between them is the gap (or the lake, if you will) out of which the story flows. As I walked out of the theater after seeing that movie, I remember feeling a huge wash of gratitude for my life—a life in the city, where I am free to be queer, a life where I can determine my own gender expression, a life where I am an artist. But this was followed by a strange, flickering image, almost a vision, of a life in a different reality, a life in which I had made none of those choices, in which I lived in my hometown, owned a house there, and saw my family every day.

The life I live now, which I love, has exiled that life to the realm of the imagination. By necessity, in the stories I tell, that version of myself is lonely, suffocated, and sad. Her imagined suffering helps make my real life possible. But, I wonder, if she were to catch a glimpse of my life, *would* she envy it? Would she think of my life as a fantasy, and try to chase it down and make it real? Or would she tell herself the kinds of stories about me that would help make her life bearable? Would I be an inspiration, or a cautionary tale?
