# 正文

Late last year a team of scientists published a study in *Nature* comparing the biomass of wild mammals on Earth with the biomass of humans and our domesticated mammals—cattle, pigs, sheep, etc. Biomass in this sense—totaling the weight of organisms by species or habitat—is a relatively crude measure, and estimating it gets harder and harder as you go back in time and the observational record thins. The study cautiously proposes the year 1850 as a point of parity, when the biomass of humans and our domesticates roughly equaled that of wild mammals.

No one in 1850 could have sensed this conjectural state of balance, of course, and not only because the data was missing. That year came and went as most years do: humans doing their humanly thing with vanishingly little regard for other species.[1](#fn-1) Which brings us to the present. The proportion of mammalian biomass on this planet is no longer fifty-fifty. It’s now 95 percent humans and our domesticated mammals—and only 5 percent wild mammals. How did we get here? Ever-multiplying numbers of humans[2](#fn-2) and livestock and, in the wild, plummeting numbers in every class of species, including mammals.

If this were science fiction, we might imagine Earth’s human inhabitants coming together, long before it was far too late, to consider ways of protecting and sharing the planet’s riches with all the other organisms. The truly fictional element in a tale like that would be humans actually committing to global, equitable interspecies sharing—and then sticking to it from generation to generation. But such things simply aren’t in the “nature” of Capitalist Earth *Homo sapiens*, whose nature it is to believe that only *our* nature matters.

How large a share of Earth’s natural resources are humans entitled to? That’s an ecological question. But it’s also a philosophical and ethical one, and it was thoughtfully articulated by the fourth American president, James Madison. (This was once upon a time, when a president could be articulate, philosophical, and ethical all at once.) In May 1818, a year after he left office, Madison gave a talk to the Albemarle, Virginia, Agricultural Society titled “The Role of Agriculture in Civilization.” In it he explains what must have seemed evident to his audience once it was stated: civilization and agriculture are inextricably and beneficially linked. And then he adds these remarkable words. Their formality may sound unfamiliar, but they contain a powerful and prescient thought:

> We can scarcely be warranted in supposing that all productive powers of [Earth’s] surface can be made subservient to the use of man, in exclusion of all the plants and animals not entering into his stock of sustenance; that all the elements & combinations of elements, in the earth, the atmosphere, and the water, which now support such various & such numerous descriptions of created beings animate and inanimate, could be…appropriated to the exclusive [support] and increase of the human part of the creation; so that the whole habitable earth [should] be as full of people as the spots most crowded now are, or might be made; & as destitute as those spots are of the plants & animals not used by man.

In other words, humans are *not* warranted to do the very thing we’re doing: stripping the planet for the apparent benefit of our species alone. Madison seems to speak with an implicit understanding that all other organisms have a right to their vital share of the world habitat.[3](#fn-3) It’s almost as though he were reading the pattern of history and the explosive growth of his new nation through the eyes of other creatures.

Madison has something else on his mind as well: the surprisingly porous border—in one direction only—between “civilization” and the lifeways of Native Americans. He points out a persistent irony in the America of 1818: how easily “civilized” people, i.e., white and of European descent, succumbed to “the charms of that personal independence which belongs to the uncivilized State”—how willingly, in other words, they abandoned “civilization” for what Madison calls “the indolence enjoyed by those who subsist on the mere bounties of nature.”[4](#fn-4) He argues, in fact, that there’s “a disinclination in human nature, to exchange the savage for the Civilized State.”

These very themes are central to the work of James C. Scott, the brilliant Yale anthropologist and political scientist who died, age eighty-seven, in July 2024, a few months before the publication of his final book, *In Praise of Floods: The Untamed River and the Life It Brings*. Scott, too, is interested in the “Civilized State.”[5](#fn-5) In Madison’s phrase, “state” means the condition of being civilized. But to Scott it means *the state*: the structures of power and governance that bind a governed people—and the source of “both our freedoms and our unfreedoms.”

As for agriculture, it gave rise, in Scott’s view, not to civilization broadly, as Madison uses the word, but to early versions of the state, which strove (as it still strives, in different ways)

> to create a legible, measured, and fairly uniform landscape of taxable grain crops and to hold on this land a large population available for corvée labor, conscription, and, of course, grain production.

In contrast, the yields from “pastoralism or shifting agriculture” were much harder to count or commandeer, as were the “mobile peoples” who engaged in them and who preferred a peripheral relationship with the state. “Putting it epigraphically,” Scott writes, “there is sedentary grain growing *without* the state but there is no early state without sedentary farming.” Scott encourages the reader to wonder, as he does, just how we humans came to be “cereal-growing, livestock-rearing subjects governed by the novel institution we now call the state”—an institution that in Scott’s accurate view is “determined to refashion nature for the benefit of a single species.”

Like Madison, Scott discerns a counternarrative to the historical story so often told: the familiar march of progress “upwards” from “primitive” hunter-gatherers living in small kinship groups to inhabitants (free and unfree) of an early grain-fed state to, well, us. The rise of the first Mesopotamian city-states circa 3500 BCE  
[6](#fn-6) surely didn’t look like “progress.” Compared with the lifeways of hunter-gatherers, Scott writes, the shift to sedentary agriculture led to “a contraction of our species’ attention to and practical knowledge of the natural world, a contraction of diet, a contraction of space, and perhaps a contraction, as well, in the breadth of ritual life.”

An autonomous, non-state existence—Madison would have called it uncivilized or “savage”—had much more to offer than personal independence and an escape from the drudgery and “unremitting toil of plough agriculture and livestock rearing.” It provided a more diverse diet as well as freedom from the diseases, zoonotic and otherwise, that emerged when humans and their domestic animals began crowding together in fixed settlements. It was as if, to use Scott’s image, humans had abandoned a rich biological and nutritional “library” for an impoverished one—as if, in fact, humans had been domesticated by the plants and animals they had domesticated.

The early rise of agriculture, Scott argues, did more “to depress standards of human welfare than to raise them.” Going over to the barbarians[7](#fn-7)—living outside the state and adapting to what Scott calls “agro-ecology”—was “far more common than any of the standard civilizational narratives allow for.” That’s one of the problems with “hegemonic human-centered narratives.” They intentionally ignore counternarratives that don’t culminate in the status quo, while also ignoring “the fate of the myriad of nonhuman life forms.”

A river runs through Scott’s “long essay on rivers,” *In Praise of Floods*. It is the Ayeyarwady,[8](#fn-8) which flows some 1,350 miles through Scott’s beloved Burma[9](#fn-9) (where he spent two years after college and returned often between 2010 and 2020) before debouching into the Andaman Sea through distributaries in its delta. The Ayeyarwady is *not* the untamed river of Scott’s subtitle, for the human impact on the river, like so many others around the globe, has been immense and deeply unfortunate. Its floodplains have been deforested, causing unchecked erosion. Its water is being contaminated by runoff from fertilizer and pesticides, poisoned by the chemicals used in gold mining, and disrupted by turbulence and warming caused by other forms of unregulated mining. The riverbed is being commercially dredged for sand and gravel, with appalling consequences for riverine habitats. Its waters are pumped and siphoned for irrigation, and its floodplains have been drained, contributing to what some environmental scientists call “the Great Drying,” a widespread phenomenon that seems to occur wherever humans have encountered wetlands. Dikes and embankments and irrigation dams have “penned in the river,” Scott writes, and the military junta is even now reviving plans to build a massive hydroelectric dam, sponsored by China, at the confluence that forms the Ayeyarwady[10](#fn-10)—a project blocked in 2011 by public outcry.

And yet the Ayeyarwady lives, allowing Scott to summon “a parliament” of unexpected voices in a chapter called “Nonhuman Species.” Rising, metaphorically, one by one to speak to the reader are members of several of the species most at risk in the river. They include the Ayeyarwady river dolphin (the interlocutor), two kinds of fish—the snow carp and the hilsa—the Burmese roofed turtle, the oriental darter, the Asian hairy-nosed otter, the mollusk, and even the white ginger plant. Scott doesn’t devise an individual idiom for each of these “riverine citizens”—they all speak with his precision. But the effect is powerful. It creates what I think of as interspecies parallax—a sudden shift in perspective from the perceptual (and ethical) world of one species to the perceptual (and ethical) world of another.

One after another, these nonhuman speakers give first-person voice to fears and complaints that closely echo those of the Burmese people who work and live along the Ayeyarwady: loss of habitat, rapidly worsening pollution, overexploitation, the inanition of the stream itself. At the root of everything they say lies the threat of extinction: loss of habitat, loss of a way of life, loss of life itself. Their complaint against our species is fundamental and well founded. Says the dolphin about humans, “You declared the territory aqua nullius and terra nullius—water and land belonging to no one and therefore open to your claim of exclusive sovereignty over all forms of life.”

In a way, the nonhuman voices speaking from the Ayeyarwady resemble the animal voices that speak in the remarkable tenth-century Arabic text called *The Case of the Animals Versus Man Before the King of the Jinn*. The animals in that fable bring humankind to trial for our endless depredations of the natural world. As they speak they indict *Homo sapiens* and at the same time present an encyclopedic natural history examining the character and diversity of the animal creation. What’s surprising in both Scott’s book and *The Case of the Animals* is the reasonableness, the inherent wisdom, the patience and understanding of the speakers—from all classes of animals—as they discuss the problem of our abusive species. They sound perhaps the way animals wish humans sounded but rarely do. As the trial in *The Case of the Animals* nears its end, for instance, a human orator “from Mecca and Medina” sums up the defense of *Homo* *sapiens* by saying flatly, as if it were beyond question, “These animals are slaves to us and we are their masters and owners.” To which the nightingale says, simply, no: “You stand on equal footing with us.”

On the Ayeyarwady, there’s also another class of beings to consider, the *nats*: spirits that belong to and inhabit the riverscape. The nats were once humans who over time became “revered spirits,” each with its own highly localized, often tragic, sometimes heroic story. Both “benign and malicious,” the nats “exert enormous power over the fate of all riverine creatures.” These aren’t quaint spiritual emanations, rising like mist from the Ayeyarwady. They say little or nothing, but they act, and act in ways that demand from river dwellers attention and propitiation. As Scott says, they’re “worshiped, placated, evaded, and invoked.” They’ve also played an important part in local traditions that “were vital in maintaining the ecological balance of the river and ensuring a healthy fish population,” traditions that under the pressure of “economic hardships and political instability” are themselves becoming extinct, with dismaying effect.

When its turn comes to speak in *In Praise of Floods*, the white ginger plant (*Curcuma candida*) makes an important point. “We are creatures of the in-between,” it says, referring to its species’ love of swamps and marshes. Scott, who calls himself “a student of resistance,” is also, necessarily, a student of the connection between resistance and the geography and ecology of the in-between. In the binary view of the mapmaker or state official, what isn’t dry land must be water, and vice versa. But what fascinates Scott are the places that are sometimes land, sometimes water, and often mud: floodplains, alluvial islands in the Ayeyarwady, the Ayeyarwady Delta, and wetlands of every kind. These are places of extraordinary biological diversity, which declines rapidly as you move away from them. They’re also places of biological and geomorphic resistance, defying the human desire to create a vastly simplified “state-serving habitat.” In times past they were refugia for people who chose for their own reasons to become or remain illegible to the state, creating “potential sites of resistance and rebellion.” All of these in-betweens are replenished, along untamed rivers, by the bounty of periodic flooding.

Reading *In Praise of Floods*, I recalled a passage from Mark Twain’s *Life on the Mississippi*. In his opening pages, Twain describes the river’s habit of making “prodigious jumps” and finding new channels. The Mississippi, he writes, “is always changing its habitat *bodily*—is always moving bodily *sidewise*.” We don’t tend to talk of planetary features like rivers as having habitats. We say that they *are* habitats or provide or create them. They are, you might say, the ground of habitat itself. But thinking of a river as *having* a habitat helps us reimagine its natural dynamism and its complex relation to the terrain it rises from and through which it flows. It helps us see a river as a living being[11](#fn-11) which, in its natural state, explores its habitat and alters it, as all organisms do. This is Scott’s task in his final opus: describing the natural habitat of rivers and “the assemblage of life-forms that depend on the river for their existence and well-being.” As it happens, only one of those life-forms is us.

We think of rivers as having a unitary, linear course, however sinuous they may be, as if a river could be nothing more than its main channel. But a river is *not* merely its main channel, and certainly not its main channel as delineated on a map. In a radical sense, a river can’t be mapped definitively because a map projects the present into the future. As Scott, echoing Twain, notes, there’s no assuming “that the present course of a river is an adequate description of its future.” Nor is a river merely water flowing downhill along its bed from a single source—a vision of fluvial geography that sparked the mid-nineteenth-century search for the origin of the Nile. A river doesn’t simply flow from a single source through a landscape on its way to somewhere else. It shapes the landscape and gathers it. Its sources are innumerable. It draws moisture of every kind—from “wetlands, damp soils, tiny puddles, ice, dew, and groundwater”—to itself, connecting them as “elements of a single hydrological system.” “Properly viewed,” writes Scott, “it is the entire *waterscape* that comprises what we conventionally call a river.”

The most important element in that waterscape is periodic inundation—flooding—which redistributes the waters a river has gathered and benefits every organism it touches. As the white ginger says, its species needs “the rich, nutritious mud brought to us annually by the flood pulse.”[12](#fn-12) The ginger might well be speaking on behalf of every organism in the floodplain, including the very earliest farmers, who practiced “flood-retreat” agriculture. As the water receded, they found in the floodplain the richest possible soil—“nutritious mud”—renewed by the river year after year without any effort on their part until at last the floodplain was taken over for other purposes and lost its character.

To every form of control, a river offers resistance, unless it’s been functionally extinguished, like the lower stretches of the Rio Grande and the Colorado, which no longer flow all the way to their deltas. To borrow a word from Dilip da Cunha’s *The Invention of Rivers* (2018), a river *dissents*. No matter how energetically humans pursue their war to prevent flooding and “*exterminate mud*” (Scott’s italics), the kinetic energy we borrow to generate hydropower—at immense cost to riverine ecology—is always pushing at the boundaries humans erect to keep a river in what we believe should be its place. As the always illuminating Ellen Wohl writes in *Following the Bend: How to Read a River and Understand Its Nature* (2025), we’ve “been remarkably persistent, thorough, and in some respects, successful at transforming rivers into something unnatural and unsustainable.” But confined is just what a river will not be, not for long.

Trying to prevent all flooding by means of “flow regulation”—the ancient, endless, universal, and unceasing building of dikes and levees and dams—has always come “*at the cost of laying the groundwork for more catastrophic floods*” (Scott’s italics). Flood prevention creates the kind of feedback loop plainly visible in the historic American effort to prevent *all* wildfires—a practice that merely amasses the fuel for more damaging future burns. There’s an important point here. If humans were to try to prevent earthquakes, it’s hard to imagine we might make them worse than they are. Same for typhoons or tornadoes or tsunamis. But nearly everything we’ve done—and are still doing—to prevent flooding makes flooding worse, makes the flood pulse more violent. Floods are now almost entirely anthropogenic. This isn’t only a function of anthropogenic climate change and its worrying effects on the hydrological cycle.[13](#fn-13) Our desire to control a river’s course always conflicts with a river’s determination to shape its own.

Movement, variation, instability, disturbance, *resistance*—these are the very qualities Scott emphasizes in the behavior of rivers and in riverine ecology, in contrast to the official state desire to convert rivers into pipelines or aqueducts incapable of flooding. His goal in *In Praise of* *Floods* is “to disturb any static understanding of the river.” Scott’s approach differs radically from the way we conventionally understand rivers. It’s as though millennia ago our species adopted a riverine policy best expressed in words attributed to Joseph Stalin: “Water which is allowed to enter the sea is wasted.” To this we seem to have added a corollary: water that’s allowed to enter the sea *unpolluted* is doubly wasted. If Stalin’s words seem too harsh, consider instead a statement by Herbert Hoover in 1926, when he was secretary of commerce. The purpose of federal oversight of American rivers, he wrote, was the “coordinated long-view development of each river system to its maximum utilization.” This is the bureaucratic version of Stalin’s anti-ecological dogmatism. Putting it epigraphically (as Scott might say), it’s hard to beat these words from the US Bureau of Reclamation in the 1930s: “Total control for greater Wealth.”

“What we have done to rivers,” Scott writes, “seems a powerful metaphor for understanding the troubled and possibly cataclysmic relationship between humans and nature generally.” He quotes Theodor Schwenk, a water researcher and the author of *Sensitive Chaos: The Creation of Flowing Forms in Water and Air* (1962). Schwenk registers the fact that humans can no longer look “at the *being* of water but merely at its physical value.” And he has this to say about our planetary predicament: “A way of thinking that is directed solely to what is profitable cannot perceive the vital coherence of all things in nature.” The fact that in rivers this vital coherence emerges in the midst of movement, variation, instability, and disturbance makes our efforts to impose “stasis, regularity, and predictability” upon them—upon all the world’s natural systems—look even more pathetic and tragic. As Scott writes, the landscapes we now live in “are engineered for a single purpose: to satisfy the taste and needs of a single species.”

Very few of us have ever seen a wild or “untamed” river, undammed, unsiphoned, unleveed, free flowing. They still do flow—a few of them—in relatively inaccessible places like the far north and parts of Central Africa and South America. And though the United States was once rich in in-between places (“approximately 221 million acres of wetlands—an area more than twice the size of California—at the time of European settlement”), very few of us have ever seen what might be called a living floodplain. So why go on talking about these things? Why make such an effort to understand a world none of us has ever lived in? Why try to remember what isn’t part of our personal memory?

Let me suggest two reasons. Trying to understand the richness, the biotic wealth of a former world makes it plain that our species, especially in the last few centuries, has been conducting a brutal, undeclared war on diversity. We can pretend that the social and cultural diversity humans have created—itself now under threat—can somehow substitute for biological diversity. Nothing could be further from the truth. The second reason is this: I believe we owe a nearly infinite debt to nature—a debt to the natural world in which we evolved. This is a debt we can only try to repay by our actions. Yet we’ve acted—and still act—as though the natural world has somehow contracted an irredeemable debt to us, giving us the right and duty to extract repayment in every way we can.

Reading *In Praise of Floods*, you’ll inevitably find yourself thinking about resistance. It may occur to you that there came a point, ages ago, when we began to treat humans—peoples—the same way we treat biotic communities in the natural world. As Scott makes plain, resistance is fundamental to life, fundamental to holding open a space for living. You may never have expected to feel the need to resist. But here we are.
