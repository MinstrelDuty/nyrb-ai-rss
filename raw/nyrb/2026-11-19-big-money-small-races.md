# 正文

At the end of June the Supreme Court decided, in *National Republican Senatorial Committee* v. *Federal Election Commission*, that federal limits on political parties’ campaign spending violated the First Amendment—in effect overruling a twenty-five-year-old campaign finance precedent. Now parties are free to spend unlimited sums in coordination with candidates. Since donors may give a national party committee twelve times more than they can contribute to a candidate directly, these larger checks can now find their way to the campaigns the donor favors.

The decision was one more attack by the Court on long-standing rules to limit money in politics. The effort began in earnest with the Court’s landmark 2010 ruling in *Citizens United* v. *FEC*, which legalized unlimited independent political spending by corporations, labor unions, and other associations. In a little more than fifteen years, the Roberts Court has radically remade the role of money in American politics, allowing vast amounts to be spent to influence US elections. Between 2008 and 2024 outside spending in federal elections rose from $144 million to nearly $4.4 billion.

Much of that money has been funneled through super PACs and other independent expenditure groups, which can accept unlimited contributions to support or oppose candidates for political office, as long as they do not directly coordinate their activities with any candidate. Donors to independent expenditure groups are overwhelmingly white, often male, and largely drawn from a limited number of industries: finance, energy, technology, real estate, entertainment, and health.

The richest donors dominate outside spending. Billionaires contributed around $31 million in the 2010 elections. Ten years later, in the 2020 presidential cycle, they gave $1.2 billion. Elon Musk, whose super PAC spent more than $250 million to help Donald Trump and his fellow Republicans in 2024, spent roughly $366 million in the 2026 election cycle, as of September 30. And Reuters reported that by late September, some two months after the Court’s ruling in *National Republican*, the National Republican Senatorial Committee and the National Republican Congressional Committee had “poured a total of nearly $56 million of coordinated spending into Senate and House races across the country,” whereas their Democratic counterparts had spent approximately $6 million.

In the aftermath of *Citizens United*, much of the scholarship on outside money in American elections has focused on the federal level, where standardized data is plentiful and easier to analyze. Yet donors have not ignored local elections, in which their spending can arguably have a bigger impact. Voter turnout is lower in city council, mayoral, and school board races, which are often held off-cycle to federal elections, so every vote matters more. And since there is less money in local races, every dollar matters more as well. The reward can also be more immediate. Local officials regularly make decisions that shape daily life, touching on zoning, land use, public safety, education, transportation, and environmental protection.

Campaign finance at the local level is not a miniature of what occurs at the federal level, Henrik M. Schatzinger, a political scientist at Ripon College in Wisconsin, explains in *Super PACs in the City*. (The publisher calls it the first book-length study of independent campaign spending in local elections since *Citizens United*.) In local elections, party structure can be weak, candidate information scant, and governmental authority over political activities fragmented. Outside money exploits those weaknesses, but the book suggests that voters have more leverage than the spending totals alone would imply.

Schatzinger’s strongest material focuses on New York City between 2013 and 2021 and Los Angeles between 2011 and 2023; assembling new sets of data on candidate-level independent expenditures in the nation’s two largest cities, he examines when and where outside money had the biggest impact. He also explores mayoral and school board races, and he ends his analysis with a review of the reforms that are still feasible under the legal regime created by the Roberts Court.

His chief insight involves what he calls “the conditional information theory of independent expenditures,” or CITIE. It argues that the effectiveness of independent expenditures in local elections depends on various factors—including the visibility of the race, the strength of partisan cues, and the robustness of existing civic and media institutions—that help determine how voters interpret and respond to independent campaign spending.

His theory offers a glimmer of hope for those who worry about the influence of money in politics. Schatzinger has discovered that putting more PAC money into local contests does not reliably buy the outcome donors desire. Instead, he writes, the power of money comes not from “shouting louder” but from “speaking into ambiguity.” Outside campaign spending is most effective when voters don’t have alternative sources of information on candidates and issues.

The first citywide election in New York after *Citizens United* was in 2013, the year Bill de Blasio was elected mayor. Because roughly two-thirds of the city’s registered voters are Democrats, the primary, not the general, was the election that mattered. There were thirty-nine contested city council primaries that year, in addition to the mayoral race. Special interest groups seized the opportunity afforded by the Roberts Court: eighteen city council candidates received more support from independent expenditure groups than the candidates spent themselves, and six candidates received at least double the amount they spent on their own campaigns. The biggest outside spender was a group that called itself Jobs for New York, which accounted for 78 percent of the $6.3 million in outside money spent on city council races. Jobs for New York focused much of its spending on political advertising aimed at sixteen open council seats, spending an average of $251,000 in each race.

Jobs for New York looked like a “flash PAC,” New York City’s most prevalent independent expenditure model: a group created for high-intensity spending in a single election cycle. However, it continued on. Flash PACs are often funded by billionaires and prefer to target crowded open primaries, spending on political advertising to define issues and candidates with scant name recognition.

Of the $6.9 million raised by Jobs for New York, 90 percent came from more than 120 LLCs and partnerships associated with specific real estate development projects underway in the city. The Real Estate Board of New York was the group’s lead organizer. The largest contributor to the board that cycle was Leonard Litwin’s Glenwood Management. (In 2014 Glenwood contributed more than $1 million to then governor Andrew Cuomo’s reelection campaign.)

Buying influence doesn’t require changing electoral outcomes. Jobs for New York devoted resources to thirteen of the nineteen city council races in which independent expenditures played a significant part; nine of its preferred candidates won, and four lost. In most cases it supported candidates who were already front-runners. An analysis by Common Cause found that money from Jobs for New York was arguably decisive in only one race. Schatzinger concludes that the group focused not on buying victories but on “signaling support in hopes of future reciprocity regarding the projects of real estate developers that would require the city council’s approval.”

The next two years were indeed kind to the donors. In 2014 the New York State Housing Finance Agency approved a $260 million state-supported low-interest loan to Glenwood. Three donors who kicked in more than $500,000 each to the Real Estate Board of New York’s 2013 effort received the 421-a tax exemption, a state-authorized break on New York City property taxes. Then in 2015 Litwin was identified as “Developer 1” in federal charges against Sheldon Silver, the former speaker of the New York State Assembly, over alleged real estate kickbacks. (Silver was eventually sentenced to six and a half years in prison; Litwin was not charged with any crime and died in 2017.)

Los Angeles, unlike New York, features nonpartisan primaries with a top-two runoff: all candidates share one ballot, and unless someone wins a majority outright, the top two advance to a runoff, even if both are Democrats. Schatzinger found that this changed how independent expenditure groups behaved. During the period studied in Los Angeles, independent expenditures were dominated by the Los Angeles County Federation of Labor (AFL–CIO), the California Apartment Association, and the Los Angeles Police Protective League. Entrenched interests and durable coalitions determined which candidates were viable. And since these groups had institutional reputations to protect and memberships to satisfy, they were reluctant to bomb races with too much negative advertising.

Both cities retain functioning, if diminished, media ecosystems that, according to Schatzinger’s theory, should blunt the effect of independent expenditure groups by providing countervailing information and exposing the identities and connections of those behind political spending. But he offers scant evidence for that in either city, characterizing their media environments without measuring them. He describes the media in Los Angeles as “patchy but active,” with voters often turning to social media and “campaign-driven content” for information. New York’s media landscape, he writes, is “dense but uneven in its coverage of local politics.”

Nonetheless, the scope of media coverage in these two cities is the exception nationwide. Some 47 million Americans live in counties with a single local news source or none at all, and digital outlets, which cluster almost entirely in metro areas, have not filled the void, according to Northwestern’s Medill Local News Initiative.

The venerable *Rocky Mountain News* shuttered in 2009. By the 2023 mayoral race in Denver, independent expenditures accounted for over 70 percent of all campaign spending in the contest, Schatzinger reports. More than two thirds of it backed the eventual winner, Mike Johnston, and came from just five individuals, four of whom didn’t live in Colorado—among them New York’s Michael Bloomberg and the Texas philanthropists John and Laura Arnold. (All three likely hoped the mayor would champion gun reform in the Mile High City.)

Since 2018 there has been a marked increase in independent expenditures in mayoral elections. According to Schatzinger, between January 2010 and June 2024, across ten cities, the mayoral candidates who received the most outside support, including in the form of spending against their opponents, won in 64 percent of primaries and 76 percent of general elections. But a full accounting is more complex, as he admits: for starters, the ten cities have wildly uneven rules about the disclosure of outside spending, making data hard to standardize. More importantly, independent expenditure groups “are usually pragmatic, directing their support toward candidates who are widely perceived as frontrunners in a race.”

Enter “endogeneity,” a wonky econometrics term for when a factor used to explain an outcome is also influenced by that outcome, leading to biased estimates. Competitive races attract spending. Independent expenditure groups often back front-runners, who may well have won without their backing. It is difficult to measure what impact independent expenditures have, if any—a fact often mistakenly used to argue that money doesn’t matter. And there is no way to count the potential candidates who declined to run, fearful of being crushed beneath a tidal wave of negative ads funded by independent expenditures.

Still, the correlation between independent expenditures and electoral success is undeniable, and its size is likely underestimated, particularly when the sample narrows to open-seat races. Schatzinger’s appraisal, for instance, does not include expenditures from 501(c)(4) groups that promote “issue advocacy”—as long as an ad doesn’t contain so-called magic words like “vote for” or “elect,” the spending need not be reported at all. In the 2024 federal cycle, the Brennan Center found at least $1.9 billion in such “dark money,” a “dramatic increase” from the previous record of $1 billion in 2020.

Dark money is increasing in local elections, but by how much is unclear. Most spending on issue advocacy at the federal level is measured through TV ad buys, but local campaign spending is often on direct mail. Cloaked in anonymity, dark money, like independent expenditures, thrives in low-information environments. If dark money flows into the same races as the disclosed kind, as it likely does, the problem is much larger than Schatzinger shows.

Independent expenditure groups have posed as local media outlets to fill news vacuums with disinformation. Schatzinger describes such an effort in the 2022 gubernatorial race in Illinois, when groups masquerading as legitimate news sources with titles like *Chicago City Wire* and *DuPage Policy Journal* spread disinformation about new criminal justice laws under the incumbent, Democratic governor J.B. Pritzker, in hopes of boosting his opponent, the Republican state senator Darren Bailey.

This is Schatzinger’s only mention of the spending slugfest around Bailey and Pritzker, but there is more to the story. One of Bailey’s opponents in the GOP primary that year was the mayor of Aurora, Richard Irvin, on whose behalf the hedge fund billionaire Ken Griffin, at the time the state’s wealthiest resident and an implacable foe of his fellow billionaire Pritzker, spent $50 million. Irvin finished third. Bailey had a wealthy backer, too: Richard Uihlein, the Uline billionaire. Bailey also improbably had the Democratic Governors Association on his side, which ran ads in the Republican primary attacking him for being *too* conservative in order to boost his candidacy and provide Pritzker with an easier opponent. Pritzker then defeated Bailey, exactly as Democrats had hoped. *Super PACs in the City* sorts every dollar into support or opposition, but in this case the meddling exploited the fact that a helpful message in a primary may be damaging in a general election.

The campaign finance ecosystem is more fluid than Schatzinger’s model allows. It’s an adaptive game between donors, consultants, candidates, and voters. Even the legal terrain can shift.

Consultants determine everything from the creation of PACs to how the money is spent. But consultant relationships are difficult to track, although some scholarship on the subject exists. While Schatzinger is concerned about consultant coordination between PACs and candidates violating the PAC independence requirement, he doesn’t touch on consultants’ economic motivations, or on how abuses such as scam PACs, which prioritize the enrichment of campaign operatives over political impact, can skew spending numbers.

Voters themselves are not static variables in this equation either. Schatzinger notes that many of the city council candidates who had money spent on their behalf in 2013 by Jobs for New York publicly denounced the group’s spending, well aware that voters had negative views about outside money and might punish any association with it. And just as voters keep evolving, so do PACs. The political mailers that clog our mailboxes are tossed into the trash unread, so PACs have turned to digital advertising and microtargeting on platforms such as Facebook and YouTube to try to break through. In 2013, 71 percent of independent expenditures went to mail in New York City. By 2021 that had fallen to 55 percent.

This variability is highlighted by Schatzinger’s “punctuated culture war,” the idea that “sudden cultural flashpoints” can trigger nationalized funding, ideological backlash, and rapid shifts in electoral power. He points to school board battles during the first few years of Covid-19. National ideological groups spent heavily at the time to boost right-wing cultural fixations on issues such as restroom policies and book bans. The money focused not on defining candidates, as his independent expenditure theory would predict, but on reinforcing their messages and activating base voters. In the next few cycles, grievances—over things like masks, remote learning, and critical race theory—succumbed to more lasting concerns about cutbacks and teacher retention, and many of the candidates backed by national groups lost. Opportunistic money is agile precisely because it lacks roots, but its rootlessness is also why it struggles to hold ground.

*Super PACs in the City* largely treats donors as pragmatic and has little to say about whether the electorate has adapted to or become desensitized to outside money in the decade and a half since *Citizens United*. It assumes that low-turnout primaries keep refilling with newcomers who haven’t wised up, so the information deficit regenerates each cycle, waiting for a super PAC to exploit it.

The 2025 New York City mayoral cycle was a record breaker for independent expenditures, with nearly $83 million spent. Airbnb was the single largest spender on city council primaries, giving $2 million in support of twelve candidates through its group, Affordable New York, hoping to secure a reversal of short-term rental restrictions. Affordable New York also spent just over $1.3 million opposing the mayoral candidates Zohran Mamdani, Brad Lander, and Scott Stringer, all three of whom were hostile to short-term rentals. DoorDash also spent heavily. It was one of the top three donors, alongside Bloomberg and the oil industry heir John Hess, to Fix the City, the independent expenditure committee that spent more than $20 million trying to help Cuomo during the primary.

Of the $35.3 million in outside money spent in the general election, 83 percent supported Andrew Cuomo, who ran as an independent after Mamdani soundly defeated him in the Democratic primary. This wasn’t the kind of pragmatic investment in city hall that Schatzinger highlights; it was panic capital.

Mamdani did something that Schatzinger’s framework doesn’t touch. He turned the PAC spending against him into some of his most persuasive campaigning. Each escalation by the billionaires and corporations opposing him was transformed within hours into social media clips and online fundraising. Suddenly PAC spending came with a risk that matched the prominence of the race, reinforcing Mamdani’s message that the opposition of big money showed why he should be elected.

The outcome, though, comports with Schatzinger’s independent expenditure theory. Mamdani is a singularly gifted communicator who benefited from movement infrastructure predating him, built by unions and the Democratic Socialists of America. New York City is the country’s largest media market. Cuomo was a scandal-damaged opponent. Under Schatzinger’s theory, independent expenditures would be expected to fail in that situation. Most local races don’t receive that kind of attention. Even if they did, imagine if the nearly $22 million in outside spending against Mamdani across the primary and general elections had attacked a merely competent progressive facing a clean establishment rival. The winner might have been different.

Wealthy donors are always seeking ways to counter the risk of the rise of candidates opposed to their interests. They can deploy independent expenditure money earlier to deter such candidates, or they can own media platforms outright to control the information environment directly. But Mamdani’s campaign suggests a response to outside money.

Independent expenditure money has certain built-in weaknesses. It can’t buy virality, which requires authenticity. It can’t create an army of enthusiastic canvassers as Mamdani did. It loses its power when voters find out who is behind it. All of these weaknesses can be the strengths of a citizen-driven campaign. The Supreme Court, for the time being, has made it difficult to ban outside money. But the power of the money shrinks as informational clarity increases.

*Super PACs in the City* ends with some basic prescriptions. Public matching funds for candidates broaden participation—New York City’s eight-to-one public financing match plus Mamdani’s viral fundraising enabled him to hit his fundraising caps quickly, allowing his campaign to focus on door knocking and persuasion rather than wooing donors. New York State recently moved to on-cycle elections for most local races outside New York City. It’s an unglamorous change, but it promises to boost turnout and alter campaign dynamics. Ranked-choice voting can make it harder for candidates to act as spoilers, a dynamic independent expenditure groups have exploited, and it allows for cross-endorsements, which build coalitions. Requiring top-donor disclosures on advertisements, as New York City does, helps strip away the anonymity PACs operate behind. Local media can be rebuilt through nonprofit newsrooms.

Schatzinger highlights the New York City Campaign Finance Board’s Follow the Money portal, which tracks fundraising and expenditures for both candidates and outside groups, as an institutional way to spotlight outside spending. In the age of AI, in-depth voter guidance by a respected nonpartisan nongovernmental sponsor like the League of Women Voters could go much further in educating the electorate.

*Super PACs in the City* argues that outside money is especially powerful at the local level, where voters know the least. By dismantling the rules that once policed political money, the Supreme Court has handed the wealthy a megaphone. We can no longer trust that democracy is self-executing. The system won’t hum along while we attend to our private lives. The machine runs on citizen power, or it doesn’t run.
