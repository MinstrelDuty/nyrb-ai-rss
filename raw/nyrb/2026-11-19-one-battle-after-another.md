# 正文

Shortly after I moved to Italy in early January 1980, someone assassinated the governor of Sicily. It was eventually found to be a Mafia killing, but at the time everyone I met wondered whether the Brigate Rosse (Red Brigades), the country’s main left-wing terrorist group, had begun operating in Sicily. The next day I went to visit a friend of my father’s, an old journalist recovering from throat cancer. While we sat talking, there was a knock at the door, followed by several seconds of panicked silence, since the journalist’s name had turned up on a Red Brigades list of potential targets. It turned out to be a young woman to whom my hosts had wanted to introduce me. The journalist’s wife asked if she had been in Lotta Continua (Continuous Struggle) in high school, referring to a far-left group that had been especially active in the early 1970s.

For young Italians of our visitor’s generation, adherence to one of a number of radical student groups had been an almost obligatory rite of passage, like belonging to a college fraternity in the United States. Two days after the governor of Sicily was murdered, the Red Brigades killed three police agents in the center of Milan, where I was living. The head of the publishing house where I worked had a bodyguard and a bulletproof car. Police armed with submachine guns combed the subways, checking people’s documents. I met a young man whose mother had been kidnapped and others whose older siblings had not been seen in a few years; it was feared that they had disappeared into the underground world of armed struggle.

In Milan at that time, it was obvious that, despite all the political violence, Italy was becoming an extremely wealthy society. The cities and towns of central and northern Italy, where terrorist groups thrived, had a standard of living the rest of the world could envy. I remember visiting Bergamo, a prosperous medium-sized industrial city, and seeing the symbol of the Red Brigades painted on a wall; it was a Sunday, the streets were deserted, and you could hear the clatter of plates and forks as the *bergamaschi* sat down to what were no doubt delicious meals. How had terrorism taken hold in the wealthiest parts of a country with one of the world’s fastest-growing economies? John Foot’s *The Red Brigades* and Sergio Luzzatto’s *Dolore e furore* offer explanations as well as gripping narratives of Italian terrorism.

Foot, a professor of modern Italian history at the University of Bristol, begins *The Red Brigades* with brief sketches of the group’s founding members. Renato Curcio was born into a working-class family in 1941, the illegitimate son of a single mother. One of his uncles, a partisan during the war, was killed by the fascists; when Curcio needed a code name in his clandestine life in the Red Brigades he chose Armando, his uncle’s first name. In his twenties he enrolled in the University of Trento’s sociology department, which became something of an incubator of left-wing radicals. Like many Italian universities, Trento was caught up in the fervor of the 1960s student movement fueled by Vietnam War protest and the popularity of China’s Cultural Revolution. “By 1967 Curcio was calling himself a Marxist and a Maoist,” Foot writes. That year

> he co-authored one of the most influential documents to emerge from the student movement—a *Manifesto for a Negative Universit*y. This manifesto went beyond a radical critique of university to a rejection of society in general. It argued that “repression and violence are the connective tissue” of Italian democracy.

The following year students occupied the university for sixty-seven days.

Curcio fell in love with a fellow Trento sociology student, Margherita (Mara) Cagol, who like him came from a devout Catholic family. The young couple fused the tradition of Catholic social activism with a form of radical Marxism; they were driven by a moral imperative to fight for the oppressed and bring about a Communist revolution. Curcio and Cagol married and moved in 1969 from provincial Trento to Milan, which was one of the centers of Italian industry. There they became close friends with Alberto Franceschini, who became the third founding member of the Red Brigades.

Franceschini was part of a militant strain within the Italian Communist Party (Partito Comunista Italiano, or PCI). He came from the city of Reggio Emilia—part of Italy’s Red Belt. His grandfather had been a founding member of the party, and the area was a stronghold of partisan resistance to fascism during and after World War II. As Communist partisans had formed the largest and strongest resistance groups, many of them expected that their struggle would lead to armed revolution. Instead the leadership of the PCI embraced Italian democracy: the party governed scores of cities across the center-north and focused on improving social services such as childcare, schools, transportation, and support for the elderly. The PCI was evolving into a reformist social democratic force, but it still paid lip service to the idea of Communist revolution.

Franceschini’s dissident group in Emilia-Romagna included former partisans who kept their old guns oiled for when they might be needed. In Foot’s formulation, Italian terrorism was something of a three-legged stool, combining the student radicalism of 1968, the increasingly militant mood in Italy’s factories, and the frustrated revolutionary elements of the Italian left who felt that the antifascist resistance had been betrayed. These three traditions came together when Curcio, Cagol, and Franceschini began working with the radicals in the Milan factories and became active members of a new organization, the Comitato Unitario di Base (Unitary Base Committee),which brought together workers, students, militants, and local residents and operated outside the official unions at the Pirelli tire factory.

“These activities were noisy, boisterous, occasionally joyful, undisciplined, angry and often violent,” Foot writes.

> Workers brought production lines to a halt without warning and were difficult to control. Demonstrations on the factory floor, heralded by the banging of drums, whistles and the smashing of metal on metal, would arrive from nowhere and shut down work. Those who disobeyed would often be threatened, or chased away, or forced to join in, sometimes with ropes which literally pulled people in.

Strikes spread across northern Italy in what became known as the Hot Autumn of 1969, with more radical groups that identified with the Viet Cong promising to turn the country’s factories into “one, two, three Vietnams.”

The combination of student protest and worker militancy was proving incendiary. In this overheated situation, someone planted a bomb in the lobby of a bank in Milan’s Piazza Fontana on December 12, 1969, killing seventeen people and injuring nearly ninety. Police began a roundup of hundreds of left-wing militants and arrested an anarchist railroad worker, Giuseppe Pinelli. Three days after the bombing, Pinelli fell or was pushed out the window of the room where he was being interrogated. The authorities insisted that he had committed suicide after confessing his guilt.

Eventually the militants were found to be innocent, and evidence pointed toward a neofascist group based in Padua that may have had murky ties with members of the Italian secret service. The Piazza Fontana bombing was the most glaring example of what came to be known as “the strategy of tension,” in which elements of the far right committed acts meant to create an atmosphere of chaos and violence that would push the country in an authoritarian direction or even provoke a military coup. It was the point at which many Italians claimed to have “lost their innocence.”

Weeks after the bombing, the book publisher Giangiacomo Feltrinelli wrote an open letter announcing that he was leaving the country. “He claimed,” Foot writes,

> that the bomb in Piazza Fontana signalled “the end of democratic illusions,” complained about judicial investigations against him, and said that Piazza Fontana was Italy’s “little Reichstag,” a reference to the events that facilitated the intensification of Nazism in Germany and its crackdown on freedoms and rights.

Feltrinelli—the heir to one of the largest fortunes in Italy—was a friend and admirer of Fidel Castro and considered himself a fighter against imperialism. Along with serious works of literature, he had published scores of revolutionary pamphlets, books by Che Guevara, books exalting the Tupamaros—the Uruguayan urban terrorist group—and even a practical manual for urban guerrillas by the Brazilian revolutionary Carlos Marighella, which became one of the bibles of groups like the Red Brigades.Rather than leave Italy, Feltrinelli went underground, began going by the code name Osvaldo, and tried to organize armed revolution.

In 1970Curcio, Cagol, and Franceschini formed the Red Brigades. Their symbol—a five-pointed yellow star inside a red circle—was modeled on that of the Tupamaros. From the beginning, what distinguished the Red Brigades from the dozens of other groups in what was called the “extraparliamentary left” was their single-minded focus on political violence. Other groups (such as Lotta Continua and Potere Operaio) had small armed contingents and preached revolution, but their leaders remained public figures, holding conventions, publishing newspapers, and in some cases participating in elections. They were divided over the place of violence in the revolution. In March 1972 Feltrinelli blew himself up while trying to place a bomb on a pylon outside Milan in order to sabotage the city’s electrical grid. Two months later what were likely militants of Lotta Continua assassinated Luigi Calabresi, the police commissioner who had interrogated Giuseppe Pinelli. Disagreements over violence (among other things) eventually led to the group’s breakup in 1976. In 1973 members of Potere Operaio firebombed the apartment of a neofascist, accidentally killing his two sons, aged twenty-two and eight. The crime horrified many in the group and contributed to its dissolution later that year.

The Red Brigades were much more professional, disciplined, and rigorous in following Marighella’s manual of urban warfare. They bought guns and trained themselves to use them. When members went underground, they cut all ties with friends and family and assumed new identities. These full-time “regular members,” who carried out military operations, were flanked by a larger number of irregular members who provided various kinds of logistical support: renting hideout apartments, supplying legal and medical help, and gathering information to assist with the activities of the regular members. Following the Leninist model of Communist cells, contact between members was kept to the minimum necessary so that if any were arrested, their knowledge of the group was limited.

The Red Brigades’ early success was partly the result of the state’s lack of preparation. Italy was then trying to reform and liberalize aspects of its judicial system. It had no laws to deal adequately with this new kind of threat. If a Red Brigades member was arrested on suspicion of participating in a crime, he would be released awaiting trial and then disappear underground. In 1974 Curcio was arrested and a year later was brought to a small prison in Casale Monferrato in Piedmont. Cagol and another comrade turned up on visiting day, forced their way into the prison with guns, and freed him. This made Cagol and Curcio into outlaw folk heroes, the Bonnie and Clyde of the revolutionary left. When Cagol was killed four months later during a police raid, she became a martyr to the cause.

Both *The Red Brigades* and *Dolore e furore* cover the events that helped shape Italy’s so-called Years of Lead: from the Piazza Fontana bombing in 1969, the death of Feltrinelli, the Calabresi killing, and the gradual escalation of violence to the group’s demise in the early-to-mid-1980s. The Red Brigades’ initial actions focused on factory life: burning the cars of unpopular managers or taking them prisoner for brief periods. In these early kidnappings, they generally did not harm their victims; instead they interrogated them and put them on trial, symbolically substituting proletarian justice for the repression of the state. From there they moved to kneecapping or shooting their victims in the legs and then to longer-term kidnappings and assassinations. As the Italian state began to react more vigorously, the Red Brigades retaliated by killing police officers and kidnapping or killing judges.

These attacks on the “heart of the state” culminated in the spectacular kidnapping of five-time prime minister Aldo Moro, the president of the ruling Christian Democratic Party (Democrazia Cristiana, or DC). Moro was the primary architect of the “historic compromise,” a rapprochement between the conservative DC and the PCI. The PCI at this point enjoyed the support of about 34 percent of the Italian electorate and was close to overtaking the DC’s 38 percent, but its leader, Enrico Berlinguer, felt that it would be dangerous to form a government without a solid majority. In Chile the socialist leader Salvador Allende had come to power with only a plurality and had been overthrown in a violent right-wing coup in 1973. The solution, Berlinguer and Moro concluded, was cooperation between left and right.

Moro was kidnapped in March 1978 as he was on his way to the Italian Parliament to inaugurate the first government headed by the DC and supported by the PCI. The ambush of his motorcade was carried out flawlessly: all five members of his security detail were killed, and Moro was taken unharmed. The Red Brigades managed to keep him for fifty-five days despite an enormous police manhunt. During that time the entire nation held its breath as his captors issued communiqués and allowed Moro to write numerous letters to family, friends, and fellow politicians in which he begged them to negotiate in order to spare his life.

The terrorists hoped to divide the government and force it into some humiliating compromise that would further damage the credibility of the state and facilitate the cause of revolution, but the leaders of both the DC and the PCI were united in what was called *la linea della fermezza* (the hard line) against negotiation; it was difficult to make concessions to a group that had murdered the five men guarding Moro. When negotiations failed, the Red Brigades killed Moro and left his body in the trunk of a car parked in the center of Rome—despite all the police roadblocks—roughly equidistant between the headquarters of the DC and the PCI.

These increasingly bold actions made the Red Brigades front-page news but also isolated them from the proletariat in whose name they claimed to be acting. The more professional and militarized they became, the more they had to guard against outside contact and infiltration. The locus of their actions had shifted from the factory to the state. Many of their early victims had associations with the far right, but now they began targeting reformists of the center and the left.

The Red Brigades’ war against the reformist left culminated in the murder in Genoa in 1979 of a Communist unionist, Guido Rossa, who had reported a fellow worker for distributing the group’s leaflets in the factory. In Luzzatto’s view it was the Rossa killing rather than the Moro kidnapping that marked the beginning of the end for the Red Brigades, the moment at which the revolution began devouring its own. The Moro case stunned the Italian public, but the Rossa murder brought hundreds of thousands of ordinary Italians, mostly workers, into the streets to mourn him.

Foot’s book is the best single-volume history in English of the Red Brigades. He makes sound judgments about the relative importance of events, avoids many of the fanciful conspiracy theories that have arisen around the group, and gives a brisk and solid account of its actions. But by focusing on one group in the constellation of the far left in Italy, he inevitably leaves a great deal out. After a while, the book feels like a dutiful chronicle of one Red Brigades battle after another. We never quite get inside the heads of the men and women who were prepared to kill for their cause. Foot relies mainly on the large library of books in Italian on terrorism and the memoirs of former members. He quotes from the group’s many communiqués, which make them sound like ideological fanatics parroting unnuanced Communist propaganda. As a result we don’t fully understand why university professors, medical doctors and lawyers, former antifascist partisans, and factory workers were drawn by the siren call of revolution.

In an afterword, Foot explains why he did not try to interview former *brigatisti*:

> This wasn’t for any moral reason: it was a choice born from reading the many accounts a number of them have produced over the years. In brief, I very much doubted whether they would say anything new to me; I thought that any such meetings would be a waste of time. Perhaps this was a mistake.

I think this was indeed a missed opportunity. In the early 2000s I interviewed several former terrorists and leaders of the revolutionary left, many of whom had been released from prison after serving long sentences. They were almost uniformly smart and interesting people with a complex and nuanced understanding of their experiences and mistakes. “I think it’s probably good we didn’t win, because we had no idea what we would have done,” Franco Piperno, a physics professor and former leader of Potere Operaio, told me.

Luzzatto, an Italian historian at the University of Connecticut, takes a different approach. He focuses on the Red Brigades in one city, his native Genoa, and drills very deep: there doesn’t appear to be an archive he hasn’t scoured; he interviewed dozens of former terrorists as well as their friends and relatives; we learn about the topography of Genoese neighborhoods and the temperature and rainfall on important dates. The book contains scores of portraits of central and peripheral figures, with fascinating asides and digressions, curious scenes, and telling anecdotes and details. At times the level of granular detail can seem overwhelming: the index lists more than a thousand names. But the book manages, quite brilliantly, to be both a microhistory of the Red Brigades and a macrohistory of Italian terrorism.

Perhaps the biggest difference between the two books is that Luzzatto gives a much fuller sense of the fertile soil out of which Italian radicalism grew. He does not discuss the Red Brigades in detail until page 184. In that long prologue, we learn a lot about post–World War II Genoa and some of the archaic institutions and social conditions that caused many of its citizens to rebel: for example, the Nave Scuola Garaventa, a floating reform school docked in the port of Genoa where juvenile delinquents were educated. An antiquated institution that began operation in 1883 and was finally closed in 1977, it was a harsh and punitive place of physical and sexual abuse. A few of its former inmates ended up in the Red Brigades. One of Luzzatto’s main characters, Giovanni Senzani, a professor of criminology, visited the floating reformatory as part of a major study of Italy’s reform schools, and that pushed him toward supporting revolution.

Luzzatto describes the difficult conditions in Genoa’s poorer neighborhoods built into the hills above the city center, which were subject to frequent flooding and landslides. Radical youth groups like Lotta Continua were extremely present in these neighborhoods, offering help and recruiting new members. Luzzatto includes a map of thirty-one different headquarters of various far-left groups operating in Genoa in the 1970s, among them Lotta Continua, Potere Operaio, and Avanguardia Operaia*,* which served as social centers for young people. They offered classes and services, recruited new members, sold pamphlets and newspapers, were present in high schools and universities, and competed to lead the revolution. It is impossible to understand Italian terrorism without first grasping the activities of these groups, many of whose members later found their way into the ranks of the Red Brigades.

Luzzatto takes us inside the humanities department of the University of Genoa, which also acted as an incubator for local radicalism. One of its most charismatic professors, the historian Gianfranco Faina, briefly teamed up with the Red Brigades and then, perhaps for reasons of personal ego, formed his own (failed) competing armed group, Azione Rivoluzionaria. The department also produced Enrico Fenzi, Senzani’s brother-in-law and a refined scholar of fourteenth-century Italian poetry, who joined the Red Brigades. The weaving together of this dense web of connections begins to make sense of this fraught, heady period. These are people who were reading Michel Foucault’s *Discipline and Punish: The Birth of the Prison* and who saw the development of Italy’s supposedly generous welfare state as a more subtle form of imprisonment. In the world of the radical left, they were making common cause with working-class people who had experienced the daily violence of factory life and fantasized about beating up their bosses and shitting on their desks. One proletarian *brigatista* said to Fenzi, “You will never be ferocious. You’ve never been in the factory.”

Riccardo Dura, another of Luzzatto’s main characters, was the child of Sicilian immigrants who moved to northern Italy as part of the migration from the south during Italy’s postwar economic boom. His father disappeared. He had a stormy relationship with his mother, ended up on the reform school ship, served in the army and the merchant marines, then found something of a home in the Genoa chapter of Lotta Continua. He became part of the group of young radicals who sat at the feet of Faina, who then recruited him into the Red Brigades. “Riccardo was somebody who had nothing…. He was the classic case right out of the Marxist doctrine of having nothing to lose but his chains,” one ex-*brigatista* said of him. “When they asked him: join us and go underground, he suddenly had an apartment, a salary, if small, but more importantly a purpose in life.”

Intellectuals were now joining forces with the disenfranchised people they had been reading and writing about, as if their theories were being proved true and coming to life before their eyes. Someone like Dura had the pent-up anger to do the actual killing that the armed revolution required. Fenzi told Luzzatto:

> He was one of those desperate types, not really bad but really angry, with a fire inside….

> He inveighed against the shitty Italian people who needed to be treated with a blow torch….

> He said to me once: if we ever win, I don’t want honors or a position. One thing: the job of killing all our enemies, all those who need to be taken care of. It will be a big job because millions of people should be eliminated.

Dura appears to have let his anger get the better of him. In the attack on Guido Rossa, the plan was apparently only to shoot him in the legs as punishment for informing on a fellow worker, but Dura ended up killing him instead. Fellow *brigatisti* considered that a serious mistake, and in Luzzatto’s view it shifted public opinion sharply against the group.

This nihilistic act of proletarian self-destruction was indicative of the blind alley that the Red Brigades were heading into. Their dream of revolution was turning into a purely military battle with the state—a battle they were destined to lose. Dura was killed in a shoot-out with police in 1980. By 1982 virtually all the high-level leaders had been rounded up and sent to prison. Luzzatto correctly describes the Years of Lead as the catastrophe of a generation.

There were already signs, when I lived in Milan in the early 1980s, that the tide of revolution was turning and being replaced by something Italians called *il riflusso*—“the ebbing of the tide.” Thousands of syringes began to litter Italian parks as a generation of young people shipwrecked by revolution turned to drugs or fled to ashrams in India. In November 1980 the real estate developer Silvio Berlusconi began broadcasts on Italy’s first private national TV network, Canale 5, from offices in the American-style gated community he had created just outside Milan. Showing programs like the American soap opera *Dallas*, it offered a future entirely different from the revolutionary fervor of the 1960s and 1970s, one of material wealth and pleasure.

The radicalism and extreme politicization of Italy in the 1970s pushed the country toward the right and, perhaps more importantly, toward an increased political apathy. Giovanni Senzani was struck by the change when he emerged after some twenty years of near-total isolation in a maximum-security prison. When I met him in 2000, he explained that he was struck and saddened by how narrow and modest the ambitions of the young Italians of his daughter’s generation were. They had virtually no notion—or hopes—of changing the world. By contrast, his own generation had erred in the opposite direction. “We tried to touch the sky,” he said, and fell to earth.
