# 正文

A singular, underrecognized talent who excelled in many different performing idioms, Tally Brown graced stages of all sizes and delighted audiences of all types. The actress and classically trained singer had a major role in the 1955–1957 US tour of *The Pajama Game*; her Broadway credits include *Mame* and *Medea*. She sang in strip clubs, jazz joints, and Vegas lounges. In 1958 she released her lone album, *A Torch for Tally*, a collection interspersed with blues covers that showcases a then-crystalline mezzo-soprano. Andy Warhol, whom she met in 1964, gave Brown her first film “roles.” In his silent, black-and-white goth extravaganza *Batman Dracula* (1964), she stands out for lying down: while hams of all genders gyrate and gesticulate around her, this zaftig woman, then entering middle age, reclines on the Factory couch, seeming to drift off to sleep. (Seeing herself onscreen, Brown joked that she “looked like a petulant baby whale.”)

Brown is more active in Warhol’s *Camp* (1965), a cracked send-up of the variety show. Soignée in an off-the-shoulder dress, she steps up to a microphone and, tongue in cheek, takes issue with the ostensible subject of the film:

> I don’t happen to believe in the existence of camp. So I’m going to do things for you that are absolutely serious, as I believe indeed everyone before me has done. I don’t think anybody’s camping. I think we’re all doing ourselves. Here are a few aspects of myself.

Imitating Yma Sumac, the so-called Queen of Exotica, Brown lets loose with a series of ululations.

More than a decade after *Camp*, Brown revealed abundant aspects of herself in Rosa von Praunheim’s extraordinary 1979 documentary *Tally Brown, New York*, recently restored by the Deutsche Kinemathek. The film’s revival is a fitting tribute not only to the little-known Brown but to Praunheim, the prolific queer German provocateur who died late last year. (Born Holger Radtke in 1942, the director took Rosa as his first name both for gender ambiguity and as a remembrance of the pink triangle—*rosa Winkel*—that gays were forced to wear in concentration camps; his surname referred to the neighborhood in Frankfurt where he grew up.)

Before his collaboration with Brown, Praunheim had earned instant notoriety with his second feature-length project, the incomparably titled *It Is Not the Homosexual Who Is Perverse, But the Society in Which He Lives*. Premiering at the Berlin Film Festival in 1971, this highly stylized, insurgent essay film, taking aim at the self-destructive habits of gay men and their pathetic attempts to replicate bourgeois heterosexual culture, was West Germany’s equivalent of Stonewall, instigating the modern LGBTQ movement there. (The gay rights group Homosexual Action West Berlin, for instance, was formed largely in response to *It Is Not*.)

Yet when Praunheim presented his film to New York audiences in 1972—first at MoMA and then at the Gay Activists Alliance headquarters in SoHo—several spectators savaged the documentary. “You depicted the absolute worst aspects of gay life,” one spat. The hostile reception did not sour the German filmmaker on Gotham; he later made several nonfiction films in and about New York, a city that fascinated him. One of the earliest is *Underground and Emigrants* (1976)—a survey of NYC avant-gardists, mostly working in theater—in which Brown appears fleetingly, saluted by the director as an “underground star.”

In that documentary Praunheim, microphone in hand, often appears in front of the camera, earnestly posing questions to a host of demimonde deities. In *Tally Brown, New York*, his queries to his subject exhibit a warmth and intimacy not found in *Underground and Emigrants*. They also take place entirely offscreen, the filmmaker’s conversations with Brown prompting her to reminisce, opine, exalt, lament—and occasionally grow tetchy with her interlocutor. (Asked “When did you come into the underground?” Brown sharply responds, “Which *one*, sweetheart?”) The recollections of the gifted raconteuse—her crisp elocution, sometimes shaded by a snooty, faux-British accent, calls to mind an early talkies star—are intercut with her mesmerizing covers of songs by David Bowie, Bessie Smith, the Rolling Stones, Johnny Mercer, and Bertolt Brecht and Kurt Weill and with the appearances of her pals (and fellow members of Warhol’s coterie) Taylor Mead and Holly Woodlawn, among others.

As the title of Praunheim’s film makes clear, a chronicle of Tally Brown also doubles as a chronicle of her hometown, where she was born in 1924, and rough-hewn footage of the metropolis constitutes another visual element of the documentary. *Tally Brown, New York* opens with a shaky, swooping long take of Times Square. The camera first captures a billboard high above street level—a sign that looks like a Lite-Brite display, emblazoned with the film’s title and a childlike facsimile of the city skyline—before descending on various neighborhood landmarks, long since vanished: the Playland arcade, the Rialto adult movie theater (then showing *Virgin Dreams*), Souvla King. The sidewalks teem with people as sunset nears; sirens wail.

With a smash cut, this visual and aural overstimulation cedes to the stately calm of Brown on a small bare stage. Accompanied by an off-camera piano, she performs Bowie’s “Heroes,” partly in English, partly in German. Seated on a stool, she is an indelible figure: short and rotund, outrageously bewigged and sporting a black tunic (baring one fleshy arm) over black leggings. Slowly the camera zooms in so that Brown’s visage, accentuated by gapped teeth and mile-long false eyelashes, fills the frame. Her cover of Bowie’s heavily produced, melodramatic ode to lovers in a bisected Berlin soars with even more emotion than the original—the result of not only her tobacco-deepened alto and its touch of tremolo but also her theatrical gestures and wildly expressive face.

Contrasting with Brown the flamboyant performer is Brown the matter-of-fact, clear-eyed interviewee, eloquently answering Praunheim’s questions about her life with piquant detail but without a trace of sentimentality—a considerable feat, considering that New York seems to breed more nostalgists than any other metropolis. She is a captivating cicerone, recalling her time working at places that had recently faded away, like the Continental Baths, the gay bathhouse located in the basement of the Upper West Side’s Ansonia Hotel that, somewhat incongruously, boasted a stage for live musical acts. (Closed in 1976, it’s where Bette Midler, accompanied on the piano by Barry Manilow, first developed her core audience.) “I love real decadence,” Brown declares, her words illustrated by footage of a tattier gay sauna, the Broadway Arms Baths (shuttered decades ago).

> To walk through a huge space full of naked bodies with just towels or no towels and sing, crowded by these bodies in this steamy, literally underground atmosphere…. The feeling, the intensity of ready feeling was, for me, the biggest turn-on as a performer that there is.

A silver-tongued sybarite, she emerges as open and alive to every possible sensation.

Near the end of her tenure at the Continental Baths, Brown also began performing at Reno Sweeney, a West Village cabaret that opened in 1973, taking its name from Ethel Merman’s character in Cole Porter’s 1934 musical *Anything Goes*. Praunheim’s documentary incorporates several of Brown’s performances there, including her jaunty take on Mercer’s “Goody Goody” and a fiery interpretation of Brecht and Weill’s “Surabaya Johnny.” Brown vivifies these standards of Tin Pan Alley and Berliner Kabarett with such élan that they are transformed into bewitching one-act plays.

Reno Sweeney is yet another Manhattan gay redoubt captured by Praunheim that now belongs to an ancient era. (It closed in 1980.) Although New York’s LGBT Community Center—established in 1983 on West 13th Street, less than a block away from where the nightclub was located—still stands as a reminder of the lavender, bohemian life that long defined the West Village, it can be disorienting to reconcile the neighborhood as it was then with what it has become: a stomping ground for uniformly attired, wealthy Gen Z women (with or without their financial analyst boyfriends) whose knowledge of New York begins and ends with *Sex and the City*.

The film is also a capsule of New York at the very end of the pre-AIDS era, and it is difficult not to be consumed by grim thoughts as we watch the stylish, youngish men—and they are almost all men—watching Brown at Reno Sweeney. How many would outlive the performer, who was felled by a stroke at age sixty-four in 1989? Other specters haunt the documentary. Movingly, Brown reflects on “the little girls who were [Warhol] Superstars who did not survive,” among them Edie Sedgwick, Andrea “Whips” Feldman, and Candy Darling, none of whom made it to thirty. Brown exhibits particular affection and admiration for Darling, who died in 1974 of lymphoma, likely caused by the potentially carcinogenic female hormones she’d been taking off and on since 1965. “Candy was not just an exhibitionist. Candy was someone who was making, constantly, the step over into being accepted as a legitimate *actress* in the legitimate world,” Brown says. Her words typify her compassion, that of a showbiz veteran—she was then in her early fifties—for younger performers struggling to prove their talent, to be taken seriously.

While Brown alludes more than once to her various excesses—going days without sleep before the filming of *Batman Dracula*, “the continual enticements” during her months in Vegas—she comes across in Praunheim’s film as a sensible hedonist, one whose lust for life stopped short of an appetite for destruction. Or whose bad habits, at the very least, were tempered by more salutary tastes: filmed at a sidewalk café in Little Italy enjoying dessert, Brown holds a cigarette in one hand and a glass of milk in the other.

Her joie de vivre made her irresistible to admirers like the visual artist Ching Ho Cheng, a former lover twenty-two years her junior and eventually a close friend. Over footage of Brown, regal in a lavender muumuu, visiting Cheng at his lodging in the Chelsea Hotel, he recounts the effect she had on him during their initial encounter at a party in SoHo in 1970: “What startled me was that here was someone who was completely unconcerned about her weight…but was completely sensuous and beautiful.” Though Cheng is the only ex-paramour to be interviewed, Brown makes clear that all of them would have kind words for her: “One of the most glorious things that I have to say about all the men who have been my lovers…is that, with the exception of one who’s dead, they’re still either all lovers, depending on geography, or friends.” She is as adept at transforming the amatory into amity, it would seem, as she is at reconceptualizing others’ songs—turning the twangy, druggy, languid bombast of the Rolling Stones’ “Sweet Virginia,” for example, into a zippy, puckish ditty.

Yet it is Brown’s unshakable ardor for her hometown, despite (or perhaps because of) its hardships, that most distinguishes Praunheim’s portrait of her. Then a resident of Washington Heights, a neighborhood she loves for its “schizoid” qualities—it is a place of “decrepitudes and inconveniences” that also affords majestic views of the Hudson River and the Palisades—Brown beautifully establishes her bona fides as one of the metropolis’s savviest partisans:

> If I didn’t love New York with the kind of passion that keeps me here through life getting rougher, tougher, more dangerous, more difficult, more hazardous, dirtier, funkier—if I didn’t have that kind of passion, I wouldn’t feel entitled to…make a film called *Tally Brown, New York*.

And her life was rougher, tougher at the time of filming, owing to a severe knee injury she suffered onstage during the opening night of the 1973 Broadway revival of *Medea*. The accident necessitated the use of a cane and forced her to all but abandon further stage work, including a tantalizing offer to play Mrs. Peachum in *The Threepenny Opera*.

But Brown did not abandon New York. (She died at Mount Sinai Hospital in Manhattan.) The city’s “soul comes from the fact of its difficulty,” she explains.

> The fact that it is the place where everybody makes the struggle to make it. And that therefore at any given point in its history, it is a hotbed of creative people who know that if they meet this challenge, they can do anything. That’s what holds me.

New York may be cleaner and shinier in the near half-century since Brown uttered those words, but it has lost none of its difficulty. For too many, in fact, the city is now an impossibility, so expensive that simply making rent poses an insurmountable obstacle to countless artists who aren’t independently wealthy. But her statement of municipal pride still inspires with its suggestion of a communitarian ethos, one amplified by her last performance in the film, a rousing version of Bowie’s “Rock ’n’ Roll Suicide” at Reno Sweeney. “Give me your hands!” she exults as she moves through the admiring crowd, the lyrics now a stirring reminder. “You’re not alone!”
