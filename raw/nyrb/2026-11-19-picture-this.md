# 正文

Henry James loved pictures, but not when they threatened to supplement his words. For James, “responsible prose” should be “pictorial enough, above all *in itself*”—the phrase comes from his preface to *The Golden Bowl* (1909)—and anything that offered to relieve it of that responsibility risked short-circuiting the process by which good writers should stimulate the imaginative capacity of their readers. “As regards illustrations you will of course please yourself,” he observed resignedly to the editor of *The* *Century Magazine*, Robert Underwood Johnson, in 1887. “I frankly confess that I hate them when attached to my own prose—& like them only as an incident of that of others.”

James lost that particular battle when *The Century* published the travel essay in question, “London,” with thirteen illustrations by the American artist Joseph Pennell the following year. But with the partial exception of the frontispieces that Scribner’s commissioned from the photographer Alvin Langdon Coburn for the so-called New York Edition of 1907–1909, James mostly succeeded in keeping his novels and tales free of illustration. Writing about visual art was one thing—the narrator of a story also submitted to *The Century* in 1887 happens to be a portrait painter—but James preferred that the magazine print “The Liar” “*pictureless*,” as he put it with characteristic emphasis to Johnson. “Ah, your illustrations—your illustrations,” he exclaimed wryly, “how, as a writer, one hates ’em; & how their being as good as they are makes one hate ’em more!”

James’s resistance to illustration doesn’t figure in D.B. Dowd’s new history of the practice, *Reading Pictures*, though the writer who liked such images well enough when they accompanied the work of others does make two brief appearances, both quotations from his 1893 collection of essays on the subject, *Picture and Text.* Though the evidence Dowd has assembled suggests that James was clearly exaggerating when he announced at the outset that “the illustration of books, and even more of magazines, may be said to have been born in our time,” the novelist wasn’t wrong to think that he lived in a century when words and images could be combined in increasingly profitable ways and that publishers were eager to take advantage of the opportunity.

According to the *Oxford English Dictionary*,it was also in the nineteenth century that the word “illustration”—which originally meant “spiritual enlightenment” or “illumination”—took on the modern sense of a pictorial representation. One of the dictionary’s earliest examples of this new usage, dated 1817, would have resonated with James, since it refers to the engravings after Richard Westall that served as frontispieces for the collected works of Sir Walter Scott, a publishing milestone that in turn provided a principal model for James’s New York Edition and its very different frontispieces almost a hundred years later. It was in James’s time, too, that the professional illustrator was first named as such, at least in English, which Dowd dates to the middle of the century.

Dowd’s book ranges chronologically from the ninth century to the present and geographically from East Asia to America; its more than four hundred images include everything from Buddhist scrolls and illuminated manuscripts to advertising calendars, album covers, and comic strips. Despite its subtitle, this history of illustrationisn’t so much a sequential narrative as a series of loosely arranged mini-histories and thematic excurses, some more sharply focused than others. But central to *Reading Pictures*,both literally and figuratively, is what Dowd calls “the high season of illustrated mass publishing,” which began in the late nineteenth century and continued well into the twentieth. “Illustrations,” he begins by stipulating, are “printed, by definition,” and though a few of the images in his study appear to violate that rule—an oil painting of a fight scene by George Bellows from 1909, for instance, or a drawing by Molly Crabapple of construction workers in Gaza that was apparently published online in 2015—the vast majority of the illustrations in *Reading Pictures* were first disseminated in print.

What that meant, however, differed markedly over time and from one culture to another. Dowd’s earliest example is dated 868 CE and comes from a seventeen-foot-long Buddhist scroll whose elegant ideographs, like its images, were printed from fixed wooden blocks rather than the movable type that European broadsides and books typically began to combine with woodcuts in the aftermath of Gutenberg’s invention nearly six centuries later. (Such single-block printing is known as xylography, from the Greek *xylon*, meaning wood or timber.) Though the Gutenberg Bible was not illustrated, a German publisher quickly took advantage of the new technology to issue a book entitled *Der Edelstein* (*The Gemstone*, 1461), in which illustrated fables from classical sources were retold in the vernacular by a Dominican monk named Ulrich Boner. As if to advertise the possibilities of the new medium, the monk’s woodcut figure appeared at the beginning of each fable, helpfully pointing to the accompanying image.

Around the same time, European printmakers also began to exploit the potential of copper engraving, a practice that seems to have originated with an anonymous artist known as the Master of the Playing Cards before largely migrating to the reproduction of paintings and sculptures in the seventeenth century. Though the use of chemicals rather than a burin to cut through the plate made the process of etching comparatively faster and more fluid, both of these so-called intaglio methods were more laborious than woodcutting, and both produced prints that had to be tipped or bound into letterpress books, since neither could be simultaneously employed with movable type. Eighteenth-century publishers might supplement their editions of poets and dramatists with engraved illustrations, but such volumes typically remained luxury items. The development of white-line engraving, a technique first perfected by the British artist Thomas Bewick, eventually helped to bring down costs by allowing engravers to incise refined marks directly into durable wooden blocks that could then be used for relief printing.

Bewick is probably most familiar to modern readers as the artist whose depictions of birds and their far-flung origins so fascinate the young heroine in the opening paragraphs of Charlotte Brontë’s *Jane Eyre* (1847). But his technique really came into its own a few years before the novel was published, when an enterprising editor named Herbert Ingram launched *The* *Illustrated London News* (1842),a weekly broadside whose combination of pictures and text—thirty-two wood engravings in each sixteen-page issue—proved especially popular for war reporting. Within a year of its founding, there were French and German equivalents, both also with versions of the word “illustrated” in their titles, and they were followed in the next decade by *Frank Leslie’s Illustrated Newspaper* (1855) and *Harper’s Weekly* (1857), among others, in the US.

Like the Crimean War, the American Civil War afforded ample opportunities for the men known as “special artists” to ply their trade, transmitting images directly from the battlefield to their subscribers—or more precisely to the engravers who helped translate their drawings and watercolors into print. (Illustration, as Dowd emphasizes, is a “team sport.”) Though we might imagine that such work was soon overtaken by the camera, that does not appear to have been the case: readers seem to have grown accustomed to the clarity and interpretive power of engraved images, and “for decades after it was possible to print halftone photographs in newspapers,” Dowd reports, “editors declined to do so.”

Indeed, one of the reasons Dowd’s history can sometimes be hard to follow is the unevenness of the phenomena he analyzes. While the visual culture on which he focuses clearly depended on technological changes, these didn’t necessarily succeed one another in chronological order, nor did they migrate consistently from place to place. Even in the West, xylographic books didn’t die out with Gutenberg, as the multiplying word bubbles in two scenes from *Life and Acts of St. John the Evangelist* (circa 1465–1470) richly demonstrate, while Japanese publishers soon abandoned the movable type imported by the Jesuits in the late sixteenth century and reverted to the use of single blocks that printed calligraphic marks and images simultaneously. In China, too, single-block printing didn’t give way until the introduction of modern lithography in the 1870s, and despite the widespread use of wood engraving in Europe and North America, the Chinese skipped that stage altogether. In both countries the preference for the single block was as much aesthetic as practical: the reflection of a culture in which the appearance of the writing constituted part of the image, and readers had learned to look at the words as well as through them.

Printed images travel easily, however, even if texts await translation. The impact of Japanese prints on nineteenth-century Europe is an old story, but Dowd’s juxtaposition of a poster that Pierre Bonnard designed to advertise Debray’s champagne in 1891 with a ukiyo-e print of a Kabuki actor that Toyohara Kunichika created twenty-five years earlier provides an eye-catching reminder of just how much Western artists learned from their Eastern counterparts. The large black characters of “France-Champagne” that curve boldly across the top of the poster might almost be said to speak Japanese, as they echo the calligraphic outlines of the curly-headed woman and the champagne bubbles that spill from her glass. At virtually the same time, an elaborate print from 1894 celebrating Japan’s victory in the first land battle of the Sino-Japanese War suggests that Western style was also leaving its mark on the East, as several journalists wearing European dress cluster in one corner, two of them busily recording the action live on their drawing boards. Meanwhile the creator of the print, Mizuno Toshikata, apparently remained in Tokyo, working the whole thing up from published accounts, photographs, and his own imagination.

East Asian cartooning, too, absorbed Western influences, from the variations on the satirical weekly *Punch* that nineteenth-century British expats launched in Japan and briefly in China to the syndicated cartoons from the US that poured into Japan in the 1920s and 1930s. We’ve grown accustomed to thinking of Japanese manga as a booming import—according to Wikipedia, it has been “massively outselling” American comics since the late 2010s—but *Reading Pictures* suggests that the influence has gone both ways, as modern versions of an older Japanese tradition of graphic narrative were catalyzed by the translated reprints of American strips that readers began to consume almost a hundred years earlier.

An evocative juxtaposition of a 1931 travel poster inviting tourists to explore the romance and beauty of French Indochina with a Vietnamese propaganda poster produced thirty-five years later suggests how tangled these lines of visual transmission could become, as the Japanese-inflected style of Bonnard and others made its way into the curriculum of the colonial art school in Hanoi, where the designer of the travel poster, Jos-Henri Ponchin, taught Đường Ngọc Cảnh, the Vietnamese artist whose arresting image of a young girl with a lotus and an M16 exhorted her compatriots to “Look after the Land, Look after the Youth.” The young woman in Ponchin’s travel poster holds an umbrella rather than an M16, and she carries it at a slightly more relaxed angle than does her counterpart, but the echoes of color and pattern between the two images are nonetheless striking.

Such cross-cultural comparisons are among the rewards of *Reading Pictures*, even if they threaten to compound the rather dizzying effect of the whole, with its profusion of images and multiplying narratives. No history, of course, really has a singular origin, but Dowd can’t quite seem to decide which of his many chronologies he wishes to highlight. After an initial survey of early modern printing and publishing, he opens his chapter on the visualization of literature with a familiar episode in the history of the British novel: the serendipitous sequence of events by which Charles Dickens came to write *The Pickwick Papers* (1836–1837)and stumbled on the formula for illustrated fiction that he and his publishers continued to exploit for the rest of his career.

The story, in more than one sense, began when Dickens was invited to compose the text for a series of sporting prints by the caricaturist Robert Seymour, to be issued monthly on the model of Pierce Egan’s best-selling *Life in London* (1821), a picaresque narrative that had been printed alongside engravings by George and Robert Cruikshank. Dickens was then a little-known journalist and parliamentary reporter—not yet one to class himself among the “important novelists,” as Dowd seems to think—and the assignment was basically hackwork. Seymour took for granted that his was the principal responsibility in the enterprise, but the new recruit had other ideas, and by the second issue he was requesting significant alterations to one of the artist’s designs. Already at risk of bankruptcy and now facing this additional threat to his autonomy, Seymour redrew the plate in question and promptly shot himself through the heart.

The result was not only that Hablot Knight Browne (better known as “Phiz”) was eventually hired in his place but that Dickens seized control of the project, significantly altering the ratio of text to images and turning a series of episodic sketches into a novel. With the introduction in the fourth issue of the hero’s irresistible sidekick, Sam Weller, the work really took off, and *Pickwick* became a best seller. The format Dickens had adopted—thirty-two-page monthly numbers with two illustrations each—became standard for Victorian fiction through the middle of the century. Seymour’s “fatal surrender,” as Dowd puts it, also marked a change in the relation between writers and illustrators, with writers now firmly in charge, while illustrators—of literary works at least—were understood as “valuable, fundamentally responsive contributors, decisively bound to an authorial text.”

That understanding of illustration probably remains the dominant one among readers of literature, even if they no longer expect to consume their fiction in monthly issues. It’s also the understanding that governs Dowd’s appealing chapter on children’s books, whose illustrations include a page from a fifteenth-century Chinese primer and a spread from *Nu Dang and His Kite* (1959)—a sophisticated picture book about a boy in Thailand created by the textile and fashion designer Jacqueline Ayer—as well as some prime examples from what’s been called the golden age of children’s literature: John Tenniel’s frontispiece for Lewis Carroll’s *Alice’s Adventures in Wonderland* (1865) and a stunning two-page spread of Walter Crane’s chromolithograph for *Beauty and the Beast* (1875) among them.

Nothing otherwise links Tenniel’s frontispiece to Toshikata’s print of the Japanese army’s victory some three decades later, but it’s nonetheless striking how both men register their self-consciousness about art making by including figures of artists at work within their images. For Tenniel, it’s a frog slyly posed at the right-hand edge of the frame, where he’s apparently serving as the courtroom artist for the King and Queen of Hearts and the bird-headed judges arranged below.

Variations on this conceit seem to have appealed to other illustrators too. Dowd reproduces a cover created by Norman Rockwell for *The* *Saturday Evening Post* in 1961, for instance, that pictures thegraphic designer Herb Lubalin at work on the magazine’s new logo, as discarded attempts dot the drawing board and old *Post* covers fan out behind it. Rockwell’s image of the designer at work in turn recalls a cover entitled “Blank Canvas (Deadline)” that he had produced for the same magazine more than two decades earlier, in which the artist pictured himself in the act of inventing—or rather, failing to invent—the cover in question. Yet another mischievous cover, this one by George Lois for a 1963 issue of *Esquire*,depicts a large billboard of Elizabeth Taylor and Richard Burton in *Cleopatra—*itself, Dowd notes, an illustration from the film’s poster—with two Lilliputian men perched on a scaffold in front of it. The men are soberly dressed, which adds to the comedy, since they also prove to be working artists, one of whom consults a reference image and draws a further line down Taylor’s cleavage.

Publications like these figure prominently in Dowd’s story. By the turn of the twentieth century the serial format popularized by Dickens had largely died out in Britain and America, and most serious writers of literature seem to have shared James’s desire to keep their pages free of other people’s images. But illustrated fiction for adults remained a significant feature of the general interest magazines that began to invade the market in the 1890s, their success buoyed by the advertisements that increasingly provided their chief source of revenue. Recent advances in photomechanical reproduction also contributed to their popularity, by enabling publishers to swap out engravings for more visually alluring halftones. Employing many of the same artists who also worked anonymously for their advertisers, magazines like *McClure’s*, *Cosmopolitan*,and their descendants in the mid-twentieth century encouraged readers to move seamlessly between both kinds of image, especially since “full-page ads,” as Dowd observes, “often included columns of copy and looked like stories.” It may be an accident that Dowd’s own eye seems to have been drawn more to the ads and magazine covers than to the pictures that accompanied the fiction inside, but his preference is also a reminder of how brief, comparatively speaking, was the era when illustrators took their marching orders from the writers of literature. Though Dowd occasionally writes as if illustration were by definition a subordinate activity—most notably, if oddly, when he refers in passing to “the practice of writers illustrating engravings with textual compositions” or adopts the same idiom for the captioning of the cartoons on the back page of *The New Yorker*—the majority of illustrations in *Reading Pictures* appear to argue otherwise.

In titling another chapter of his book “Illustrator as Author,” however, Dowd further complicates his narrative, even as he partly revises the chronology he has previously traced by beginning with Dickens. Rather than Phiz’s illustrations for *Pickwick*,this account opens with William Hogarth and his successful campaign to extend British copyright protection to printed multiples of visual art. The creator of *A Harlot’s Progress* (1732) had already turned author in Dowd’s sense by telling the tale of a young woman’s seduction and ruin through a sequence of elaborately captioned prints, themselves derived from oil paintings. But engravings like these were vulnerable to counterfeiters, and it was only with the passage of the Engraving Copyright Act of 1734 that Hogarth felt reasonably in control of his market—an achievement that constitutes a seminal moment in the history of printed images and that the artist soon celebrated by issuing another of his famous sequences, *The Rake’s Progress* (1735).[\*](#fn-*)

Abundantly supplemented by text and filled with emblematic details that require decoding, Hogarth’s prints are clearly pictures to be read. Whether most people would identify them as illustrations is more debatable, but it is this expansive sense of the term that appears to account for many of the other examples in Dowd’s book, from newspaper cartoons and comic strips to manga and graphic novels. Or perhaps it would be more accurate to say that the title *Reading Pictures* is finally a better guide to Dowd’s project than his subtitle, particularly since he acknowledges at one point that “freestanding” images like cartoons or caricatures don’t really qualify, even as he continues to reproduce a number of these, together with other freestanding images, from posters to the occasional oil painting.

Though Hogarth chiefly figures here as a champion of illustrators’ intellectual property, his visual legacy is abundantly evident as well. Among the most strikingly Hogarth-like of the images in Dowd’s book is a full-page reproduction of a cartoon feature by R.F. Outcault entitled “What they did to the dog-catcher in Hogan’s Alley” that appeared in Joseph Pulitzer’s *New York World* in 1896 (see illustration at top of article). The cartoon’s action takes place on New York’s Lower East Side rather than in Georgian London, but its raucous urban scene still owes an obvious debt to the eighteenth-century satirist. So, too, does the sheer proliferation of detail—the flames bursting from the dogcatcher’s van, the parrot squawking in a cage suspended from the bars of a fire escape, the boy tumbling headlong into the crowd—as well as the verbal glosses that Outcault scatters, as Hogarth did before him, throughout his image. The advertisement for “McSwat’s New Gold Cure” affixed to the tenement wall is pure Hogarth, as are the lyrics of “The Park Row Songster” that lie on the ground between two dogs and spell out “Its Funny How They Roast Us When We’er Gone [*sic*],” especially since the dogs’ eager postures and gaping jaws make them look as if they were singing along. We might almost be looking at Hogarth’s *Beer Street* (1751), *Gin Lane* (1751),or one of *The Four Stages of Cruelty* (1751), though the youthful denizens of Hogan’s Alley vigorously beating the dogcatcher to keep him from turning his catch into sausage are likely to evoke far more sympathy than the children torturing dogs and other animals in the first plate of the *Four Stages of Cruelty*. Outcault is a less sophisticated artist than Hogarth, and his lines lack the other’s refinement, but he clearly belongs in the great printmaker’s lineage.

Dowd also highlights Outcault’s role in the evolution of the comic strip, especially after the cartoonist left Pulitzer’s paper for William Randolph Hearst’s *New York Journal*, where he began experimenting with telling stories in multiple panels and supplying the parrot—a favorite character, apparently—with speech bubbles. If this practice of narrating primarily through images turns the creators of comic strips and graphic novels into “author-illustrators,” in Dowd’s coinage, then the illustrations they produce are no longer the handmaidens of text but something more like its master.

One of the last examples in *Reading Pictures* is a page from the graphic novel *It’s a Good Life, If You Don’t Weaken* (1996)by the Canadian cartoonist known as Seth that virtually does away with words altogether. There’s a speech bubble in the upper left-hand panel, but apart from a street sign spelling out “Milner” at the bottom, the other five panels are wordless: a device the artist employs repeatedly in the course of the narrative, sometimes for several pages at a stretch. The effect is not quite as stark, admittedly, as that produced by one of Dowd’s earliest examples, an initial miniature from a fifteenth-century antiphony in the Siena Cathedral whose vividly painted image of Jesus healing the leper visually overwhelms the capital *R* of its frame.

But the graphic novel, too, plays with the possibility that “looking,” as Dowd says of the miniature, “is more important than reading,” or at least that looking can itself be a means of reading—as of course it always is. Though Dowd doesn’t explicitly register the fact, the hand lettering of a graphic novel like *It’s a Good Life* also hearkens back to earlier practices. The comparatively simple strokes with which Seth forms his letters may seem a long way from the fluidity and sophistication of the ideograms in the *Diamond Sutra*, but both the calligrapher and the cartoonist know that the writer’s mark making can also be part of the picture.
