# 正文

In 1949 Ithell Colquhoun—artist, writer, and occultist—produced a manifesto, titled “The Mantic Stain,” in which she called on her readers to follow French Surrealism’s practice of “pure psychic automatism.” It was, Breton wrote, “the real working of thought. Dictation of thought, without any control exercised by reason, and beyond any preoccupation, aesthetic or moral.” “Mantic” was a characteristically esoteric flourish on Colquhoun’s part; it’s derived from the ancient Greek for “prophet” or “seer,” as in the arts of divination (by card reading—cartomancy; by dream interpretation—oneiromancy), and alludes to Manto, the daughter of Tiresias, the seer who experienced existence as both male and female. It also pays homage to the praying mantis, an erotic symbol beloved of the Surrealists.

Oracular methods such as automatism and tarot cards would lead Colquhoun down many paths in her search for knowledge, while the duality of the androgyne fascinated her in her art and writing. Mantic staining, including automatic processes like blotting and doodling, was a way anyone could access hidden orders of existence and liberate the inner self and its creative energies:

> To a beginner the fact that he is faced not with an empty white rectangle…but with a suggestive chiaroscuro or a colour combination with potentialities will give him confidence. This self-confidence is the first step towards revealing the mind’s profound imaginative riches.

Colquhoun first became aware of the Surrealists in Paris, where she was spending time in the early Thirties, and sat for Man Ray, who portrayed her as a corn goddess, with a sheaf of ripe wheat in her hands and her skirt parted. But it was the first major Surrealist exhibition in London, in 1936, that turned her into a disciple and fervent proselytizer for the movement. She was present at the famous opening when Salvador Dalí, wearing a deep-sea diving suit and holding two wolfhounds on a leash, began giving a lecture and had to be rescued from suffocating to death in the process. Later, back in Paris, Colquhoun met Breton, and in 1939, on the eve of World War II, joined a gathering at the château of Chemillieu, where the artists Roberto Matta and Gordon Onslow Ford took the discussions in a more esoteric direction, which appealed strongly to Colquhoun. She was already taking a serious interest in ritual magic, ancient and novel.

The 1949 essay on the mantic stain reappeared in another version two years later as “Children of the Mantic Stain,” an enthusiastic inventory of the many processes Surrealists had devised to set free the imagination. It includes decalcomania—the folding or pressing of paper onto a pour or splash of ink or paint, a process brilliantly explored by Max Ernst in his apocalyptic landscape paintings of the 1940s, for example *Europe after the Rain II* (1940–1942). She also describes frottage (taking a rubbing—another method pioneered by Ernst), *fumage* (holding paper to a flame to scry the traces it leaves), and *parsemage*, her own invention, which involves sprinkling powdered charcoal or pigment onto water and then passing a sheet of paper under the surface to lift off the image.

The psychologist Hermann Rorschach famously asked patients to “read” inkblots and then analyze their responses. Ernst, Dalí, and other Surrealists took the method further and proposed intervening, heightening with brush and pen what they discerned in the random marks, drawing out dramatic scenarios and dreamscapes. This approach, a modern variation on the ancient divinatory techniques of seeing the future in clouds, animal entrails, or tea leaves, was most passionately adapted by Dalí with his “paranoiac-critical method.” He was a virtuoso at applying it to produce complex—and lasting—phantasmagorias such as *The Persistence of Memory* (1931) and *Metamorphosis of Narcissus* (1937).

Colquhoun had a penchant for pedagogy, and in l948 she demonstrated several of these avant-garde methods for a very polite early BBC television documentary, telling viewers how to set free their artistic potential in the same manner as she might have shown how to make an omelet. In her own work, she improvised with them to powerful effect, allowing her vivid pigments to spill and ooze in paintings such as *Gorgon* (1946), *Decalcomania counterpart for “Gorgon”* (1946), and *Woman of Beare* (1950). She also took a significant step when she extended these Daliesque methods to her writing—and in this lies her true originality.

In the novels *Goose of Hermogenes* and *I Saw Water*, Colquhoun practices a literary form of mantic staining: fugues of images, sudden gaps and non sequiturs, unexplained exits and entrances, and abolition of cause and effect intensify the strangeness of her female protagonists’ journeys toward transfiguration. Surreal landscapes, as if created by decalcomania, are live with fantastic phenomena and bright with hallucinatory mineral colors. In *Goose of Hermogenes*, the heroine records:

> I now discovered fish-like flowers growing directly from the stone without leaves. I could hardly get foot-hold or hand-hold without crushing or gripping these cold petals, which spread their cherry and blue-grey all about the ascent, a salty deposit covering them with a dusty grape-like bloom.

Later, during the final stages of her apotheosis, she finds, “In this water were strange living creatures, like sea-anemones…. They were of various jewel-colours, ruby, sapphire, emerald, some of them spotted with white.”

Throughout her life Colquhoun kept a dream journal and pored over her records to plan her fictions, tracing patterns to uncover the dreams’ meaning. One particular dream journey in which she found herself in a cave in Nicaragua, a country she had never visited, inspired her to hunt for corroboration from its geography. This katabasis, or descent into the underworld, inspired a striking section of *I Saw Water*.

From the Surrealist perspective, dreams weren’t the debris of consciousness, and dreaming wasn’t waste disposal performed by the brain in readiness for the light of day. As Breton’s hero Baudelaire wrote in a letter of 1856:

> Since you enjoy dreams, here’s one that I’m sure won’t disappoint you. It’s 5 in the morning, so it’s still fresh. Note that this is only one sample from the thousands of dreams that constantly besiege me, and I don’t need to tell you that their complete singularity, and the fact that they are entirely unrelated to my daily activities or my passionate adventures always leads me to believe that they are a kind of hieroglyphic language to which I don’t hold the key.[1](#fn-1)

Colquhoun believed there was a key and she could find it. She researched cultures in which dreams delivered prophetic truth-telling messages from a higher plane. Like splashes and blots, dreams emanated from hidden forces, perhaps of the human mind, perhaps not. She had a metaphysical belief in their potential to put her in touch with supernatural beings, not angels or demons in any religious sense, nor the fairies or phantoms of popular belief, but spirits posited by mystical systems such as astrology and alchemy, otherworldly manifestations raised by enchanters and magi who had developed means of contacting them.

Accordingly, *Goose of Hermogenes* progresses up the alchemical ladder as, chapter by chapter, the heroine ascends toward wisdom; in *I Saw Water*, geomantic figures provide each chapter’s leitmotif (though it must be said that the rationale remains elusive to the reader).

Ithell Colquhoun was born in 1906 in India, where her father, a British colonial officer, had himself been born. (Her unusual first name, Ithell—pronounced Eye-thell—isn’t an obscure spelling of Ethel but a forebear’s surname; her last name is pronounced Kuh-hoon.) Like many servants of the Raj, Colquhoun père wished his daughter to grow up a thoroughly British girl, and she was sent to “the mother country” to be brought up by an aunt in Cheltenham. There, in that respectable Cotswolds spa town, she attended Cheltenham Ladies’ College, where the program had been shaped by the legendary reformers Miss Buss and Miss Beale, and was consequently more ambitious than the usual fare for girls at the time. Ithell was joined by her mother, then went on to the local art college. Her curiosity about magic was encouraged by a cousin, Edward Garstin, who was to publish *The Secret Fire: An Alchemical Study* (1932) and other occult works. He introduced Colquhoun to Kabbalah and the teachings of the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn. (W.B. Yeats had been active in the order for a time—Yeats, Ireland, Celtic myth, and visionary poetry would become essential elements in Colquhoun’s oeuvre.)

In 1927 Colquhoun enrolled at the Slade, part of the progressive, secular University College London and the leading art school in the country. At this stage Colquhoun was painting subjects from classical mythology and the Bible; her forms were massive, sculptural, strongly delineated—think William Blake reworked by Tamara de Lempicka—resolutely anti-decorative, and fierce in mood. She favored heroic episodes such as Judith beheading Holofernes and took her own distinct path with others: a strange painting of a scene from Exodus shows Moses rigid in goatskin and horns while Aaron, voluminously robed and coiffed, embraces him passionately. The Slade’s renowned professor, the surgeon-painter Henry Tonks, wrote to her, “You go out to gather strawberries and come back with two strange beetles and a spider instead.”

Colquhoun benefited from a small trust fund, enabling her to live as an independent artist-scholar. Free to choose, she was no idle rentier. A copious stream of poems, tracts, stories, accounts of dreams, and aesthetic theories flowed from her pen. Although she never returned to her birthplace, she hankered after the sights, sounds, and festivals of India, and she became an adept of Indian mysticism, alongside alchemy, astrology, Kabbalah, Egyptian myth, Christian hagiography, and Sufism. She also joined—and left—a bewildering number of groups and secret societies. Her appetite for arcana seems to have been unappeasable. Her art and her fiction visit sites of sacred power, conveyed in works such as *Dance of the Nine Opals* (1942) and *La Cathédrale Engloutie* (1952).

Colquhoun had affairs with men and women: a fisher lad in Corsica inspired a chapter in *Goose of Hermogenes*, and she fell passionately in love with a woman in Greece named Andromaque “Kyria” Kazou, whose strong features she drew and painted several times; the affair inspired a heady story, “Lesbian Shore,” as yet unpublished. She read widely, especially poetry, and translated Mallarmé and Breton. Breton’s style, so influential on her cult of automatism, suffuses the imagery of her own writing. Like many others she longed to achieve the dazzling incoherence of his *Fata Morgana*—that mantic cataract of a poem—and the audacity and delirious impossibility of its first word picture: “Ce matin la fille de la montagne tient sur ses genoux un accordéon de chauves-souris blanches” (This morning the mountain’s daughter holds on her knees an accordion of white bats).

During the war, Colquhoun lived and worked in London—and it was there, in the early 1940s, that she met Toni del Renzio. An improbable figure of Byronic glamor, he was a Romanov on his mother’s side and an Italian aristocrat on his father’s (with a bouquet of titles); he had deserted from the Italian army in Abyssinia, joined the Partisans in Spain, abandoned them, and fetched up in London, where he befriended E.L.T. Mesens, the Belgian-born leader of the British Surrealist group. The war had discouraged its members; del Renzio presented himself as ready and willing to regalvanize them.

Colquhoun and del Renzio married in 1943, and for a few years the marriage was turbulent but exciting. Explicit drawings and watercolors she made in this period explode with frank delight, while the series of erotic talismanic figures *Diagrams of Love* shows her again seeking to pin down the mess and flux of experience in a symbolic system. The possible primal unity of the sexes, as described by Aristophanes in Plato’s *Symposium*, was a Surrealist motif that gripped Colquhoun; she explored the potential fusion of male and female not only in her art but also in her writing. *Goose of Hermogenes* and other stories conflate the Greek myth of the original hermaphroditic state of human beings with the androgyne, a longed-for stage in the journey to the philosopher’s stone, the ultimate goal of the alchemical process.

Despite her keen advocacy of surrealism, in 1940 Colquhoun was either expelled from the British group or walked away because, it is generally agreed, she would not give up her esoteric adventures. Mesens wanted to impose his authority, and when he decreed that members of the group could not join any other society, Colquhoun wasn’t sufficiently docile. But whatever the cause of the break, I feel it damaged Colquhoun. It cut her work off from the larger collective endeavor of Surrealism and its psychological inquiries and deepened the marginalization she suffered for decades. The American anthropologist and folklorist Amy Hale, author of an enraptured biography of Colquhoun called *Genius of the Fern Loved Gully* (2020), observes that

> although most historical mentions of Ithell Colquhoun focus on her relationship with the Surrealist movement in Britain, her commitment to occultism was more consequential and, in fact, her Surrealism was in service to that obsession, which was the principal driver of most of her life’s work.

Hale welcomes Colquhoun’s occultism as pioneering feminism, whereas some of her readers and viewers—myself among them—shrink from her earnest commitment to practical magic.

Colquhoun’s marriage to del Renzio disintegrated, and they divorced in 1947. She suffered a period of grave depression and decided to retreat to Lamorna, a celebrated scenic village in the woods on the precipitous Cornish coast. She bought a shack called Vow Cave. It had no plumbing or electricity. Seeing herself as Celtic by ancestry and affinity, she found inspiration in its rugged austerity and settled there for the rest of her life.

The Surrealists idolized Woman and generally belittled actual women: they saw them as *femmes-enfants*, brides, love objects, praying mantises, sirens, medusas. (The influence of Freud shows strongly!) But in 1985 Whitney Chadwick’s *Women Artists and the Surrealist Movement*—a brilliant work of feminist recuperation—opened my eyes and the eyes of many others to the lives and work of artists such as Leonora Carrington, Dorothea Tanning, Kay Sage, and Colquhoun.[2](#fn-2) Chadwick reproduced Colquhoun’s magnificent, highly perturbing *Scylla* (1938), a painting of two fleshy pillars rising out of the sea and bracketing a narrow passage, with coral blossoming in the clear water like pubic hair. She also included Colquhoun’s *The Pine Family* (1940), a grimly comic work that shows three mutilated bodies like pollarded tree trunks lying alongside one another, one labeled, in Magritte-style script, “l’hermaphrodite circoncis” (the circumcised hermaphrodite).

Since Chadwick’s book, there has been a tectonic shift in the fortunes of women artists such as Frida Kahlo, Lee Miller, Louise Bourgeois, Paula Rego, and more. Among Chadwick’s subjects, Carrington received some recognition in her lifetime but did not live to see the global cult she has now inspired (and the prices at auction to match).

Colquhoun has had to wait longer to be fully disinterred from obscurity. At her death in 1988, she left her home and the contents of her studio to the National Trust and specified that her occult material should go to the Tate. The division seems to have paralyzed the beneficiaries, and for many years nothing was done. The photomontage artist Linder, an early fan, visited Lanhydrock, the National Trust property in Cornwall where part of Colquhoun’s legacy had been consigned, and found, to her utter dismay, hundreds of drawings, paintings, and drafts strewn about higgledy-piggledy, uncatalogued, uncherished.

In 2019, more than thirty years after Colquhoun’s death, the thousands of items in her archive were at last properly assembled; interest has been growing ever since, culminating in “Between Worlds,” a vast survey show, which opened at Tate St. Ives, near her home in Cornwall, last year and then traveled—in a slightly trimmed version—to Tate Britain in London. Curated by Katy Norris, who also edited the catalog, the retrospective displayed works from every stage of Colquhoun’s career, including *Scylla*, *The Pine Family*, *Diagrams of Love*, her later explorations of rocks and plants, and leading up to her Taro (her preferred spelling) pack, made in 1977 with a voluptuous use of mantic staining. The artworks were supported by fascinating manuscripts and other ephemera in which Colquhoun dilates on the Goddess, color symbolism, ley lines, astrological alignments, tarot, and sex magic.

Hale vividly describes her first sighting of the material: “dozens of manuscripts, poems, weird magical experiments on graph paper, conjurations of angels, love spells, petitions to healers, colourful tesseracts, magical journals and so, so many letters.” She followed *Genius of the Fern Loved Gully* with *Sex Magic* (2024), a catalog of the *Diagrams of Love*, and has just brought out *A Walking Flame*, a collection of Colquhoun’s essays on magic and the occult. Hale’s approach confirms a pervasive change in the cultural sphere about the value of witchcraft, magic, paganism, and what is caught under the rubric of “women’s spirituality.”

Attitudes to the Goddess and the Divine Feminine, as expressed by postwar feminists (my generation), have taken a different metaphysical direction. Where we deconstructed the stance of Robert Graves in his 1948 study *The White Goddess* (while enjoying its lore and learning) and opposed the arguments of Ted Hughes about “The Goddess of Complete Being” in his 1992 study of Shakespeare (while admiring his poetic knowledge), and saw such worship of Woman as saturated with patriarchal notions of whores and virgins, mothers and helpmeets, many women today are reactivating the cult of the Goddess and finding new strength in the idea of the witch and the seer. For example, the small feminist press Spiral House, an imprint of Silver Press, specializes in this new quest for female wisdom, taking it into psychology and ecology, memory and consciousness studies.

In this current turn, Ithell Colquhoun, dream interpreter, tarot reader, and crystal gazer, has emerged as a torch bearer for a definite strand in contemporary culture, which has identified a pantheon of precursors, mediumistic artists across time and place: in Victorian England, Georgiana Houghton photographed spirits and produced startlingly proto-abstract expressionist paintings inspired by her spirit visitors; during the first half of the twentieth century, Hilma af Klint in Sweden had visions and likewise broke with the figurative conventions of her contemporaries; and in Switzerland, Emma Kunz tuned in to the earth’s energy with a pendulum and made subtle geometric paintings. Such artists were once thought of as outliers but they are now inspiring vigorous interest. In 2024 in Cambridge, England, a show called “The Goddess, the Deity and the Cyborg,” selected from the Women’s Art Collection, explored psychic expressions of female power, and a recent major exhibition in Milan, curated by Massimiliano Gioni and others, showed a heterogeneous group of artists past and present—almost every one of them female, and including af Klint, Houghton, and Kunz—united under the sign of Fata Morgana, the fairy and meteorological portent invoked by Breton, and implied that together they expressed the magical unconscious.[3](#fn-3) (Colquhoun’s absence was probably caused by her works being shown elsewhere.)

During Colquhoun’s postwar years, she continued to make art but was also writing prolifically—essays, poetry, and a pair of travel books, *The Crying of the Wind: Ireland* (1954) and *The Living Stones: Cornwall* (1957), which have recently been reissued along with her novel *Goose of Hermogenes*. As a travel writer, Colquhoun shows herself to be a fine, lively collector of anecdote and ancient lore. She scrambles over rocks and stomps through briars and nettles, undaunted in her hunt for another holy well, another menhir or stone circle. In *The Living Stones*, she observes a Druidic gathering with a sharp eye, deploring the practical footwear visible beneath the elders’ ceremonial robes and the reliance on microphones. The necessity to use technological aids, as opposed to the human voice, strikes her as a grim sign of modern decline, our disconnection from nature and its divine energy.

*The Living Stones* and *The Crying of the Wind* found readers then, and they are still entertaining and richly informative. Their success persuaded her friend, the independent publisher Peter Owen, to yield to her pleas, and in 1961 he brought out her novel *Goose of Hermogenes*. The title provides a typically abstruse clue to the narrative: it sounds like a concoction—attar of roses or balm of Gilead—and indeed “goose of Hermogenes” is a rare term for the philosopher’s stone, which may help produce the elixir of life, while “Hermogenes” means “born of Hermes,” the Greek god of hermetic knowledge, codes, and tricks, who in the Roman pantheon is called Mercury, the prime ingredient in the great work of transformation.

The heroine of *Goose of Hermogenes* is unnamed. At the start, she has been summoned to the island of a mysterious uncle for reasons that aren’t clear. When she arrives, she encounters peculiar inhabitants including an “Anchorite,” who is helpful but appears to be an agent of her uncle’s; later, as she explores farther, she meets several more islanders, each more puzzling than the last. Her uncle, the controlling genius loci, remains hidden in his study until, at their first meeting, he greets her with a mysterious dumb show:

> After a few seconds a shutter, not unlike that of a box-office, opened in the middle of the panelling; and in this aperture, illumined from within by a glow faintly rose-coloured, appeared my Uncle’s hands. They held a few leaves from some rare booklet, embossed and illuminated with half-perceived designs. The pages fluttered for a few moments like a butterfly on some fleshy exotic bloom, then the shutter was drawn again and the door darkened.

What does this mean? It is left an enigma. The uncle is a Prospero-like wizard but more sinister. She suspects he is working on a spell to resist death and

> I somehow guessed that my Uncle wanted me in his house because of my jewels, which were beautiful as children’s sweets and very precious, and which he probably fancied to be possessed of alchemystic powers. But he had to obtain them from me by some not-too-openly disreputable means, and this was not easy, since I always wore them.

Colquhoun’s protagonist doesn’t want to join in her uncle’s experiments to turn base metal into gold or defy nature by living forever. Like her author, she desires wisdom, which means freedom from the burden of the self in a mystical, Eastern key. But either this uncle’s sorcery or another unseen enchantment of the island binds the heroine so that, like Ferdinand in *The Tempest*, she cannot escape.

The novel moves through a series of twelve weird tableaux, each named after a stage in the process as drawn up by the influential alchemist George Ripley, beginning with “Calcination” and ending in “Projection.” This structure feels forced, except in the chapter “Conjunction,” which tells a charming, whimsical love-story-cum-fairy-tale: the heroine comes across a manuscript about a pair of doomed young lovers. Oriole, pale and wan, teaches his soulmate Corolla to fly, and in a mystical apotheosis they follow a phoenix:

> The bird, too, must have felt a link with the fiery west, for it sailed on as though drawn without volition to plunge into that flaming core; and with this creature of air for guide, the two sailed effortlessly on, desiring no return.

On her pilgrim’s progress, the protagonist continues to roam the island. Colquhoun’s belief in all of nature being alive and sentient, alongside her considerable botanical knowledge, animates the scenery. The plot, such as it is, involves her heroine in horrific ordeals: in the chapter “Exaltation,” she is seized and stripped, bound and drugged, lashed and raped, and offered up, as in some Rite of Spring, to a statue of a faun or Silen: “I laid my mouth to its stoney lips, and a tongue, icy as an adder’s, seemed to dart from between them to meet mine.”

The next morning, she wakes to find herself undamaged. It was all a dream.

Fantasy here is intrinsically incorporeal, and the Sadean tableau another dumb show. This is not only troubling but unsatisfactory—the author appears to be quitting, abandoning the responsibility for what her unconscious has dictated.

In the final, twelfth chapter, she enters the land of the dead, visits the abandoned family home, and in an eerie Dantesque encounter meets her father, who does not know he is dead. She then enters a paradise of plenty, where “warm fountains the colour of port-wine were jetting through the earth, fertilising it to this abundant growth.” Ever the persevering pilgrim, she keeps going, toward “the mountainy country to the east…. The region was far, but even as I looked its pencilled summits were touched by the first auroral glow.” Is this a prompt from the unconscious toward the Golden Dawn, or a deliberate allusion on Colquhoun’s part? Toward the close, the protagonist opens her veins and fuses with the sea in a mystical act of self-annihilation.

The Surrealist scholar Alyce Mahon, who contributes an essay to *Between Worlds*, sees these tableaux as symbolic and sacramental. Certainly, Colquhoun’s imagination coincides with Catholic ideas of exaltation in violent suffering.

A thirteenth chapter of *Goose of Hermogenes*, “Hexentanz,” was left out of the 1961 edition and has now been restored. It features another limbless body, the Stump, and evil children who abuse him for fun. I wasn’t convinced by its appearance at the end of the novel, after the heroine has attained alchemical transformation, but it does reveal that Colquhoun could match any outré male Surrealist in provocative sadism.

Her novel *I Saw Water*—written around 1967 but not published until 2014, in an edition illuminatingly annotated and edited by Richard Shillitoe and Mark S. Morrisson—was entirely composed from her dreams, Colquhoun said, and then later organized into chapters named after a geomantic figure. For example, “Bound” (chapter 5) evokes the figure of “Carcer,” Latin for “prison,” signifying “setbacks and delays.” The stories that result are bizarre, dramatic, not always intelligible.

Although both novels risk sounding like a friend with a hangover recounting a dream, I enjoyed *I Saw Water* more than *Goose of Hermogenes*. Colquhoun is at times shrewd in her observation of her characters and can besly and funny and earthy in a voice closer to Carrington’s. The novel also describes a very real human situation, probably based on her own experience: the husband of one of her principal characters is a charmer, an extravagant wastrel, who is living off his wife’s money and flaunts his love affair with another man. All of this is startlingly frank—at a time when homosexuality was still a crime.

The heroine, Brigid, has entered a convent of the Order of the Ianua Vitae in Ménec, Brittany, where, reluctant to take final vows, she herds the sisters’ cows and strikes up an ambiguous friendship with the novice mistress, Sister Mary Paracelsus, who brews potions in her laboratory from local plants and herbs (and is named for the fifteenth-century Swiss alchemist). Out in the fields, Brigid meets a roguish local landowner and plans to elope with him but is foiled when he drowns—a turn of events she accepts without shedding a tear. A parallel story meanwhile unfolds about Brigid’s cousin Charlotte who, after the collapse of her marriage, is in pursuit of her married psychiatrist. He has a little boy who is suffering from a severe, unnamed disorder. In a healing ritual, inspired by Catholic festivals in Brittany, water from a holy well is sprinkled on the child:

> [He] uttered a cry different in tone from any we had yet heard from him and struggled out of his father’s arms. His feet touched the tessellated floor, actually supporting his weight; then he spun round to face the nave, his arms outstretched as if to embrace the crowd. For an instant his strangeness dropped away from him, his face was lit up by a look of lively intelligence and he stood before us as the child he might have been. He took one step forward, then collapsed and lay unmoving, his eyes fixed on the dome alone.

The hope of a miracle cure, and the subsequent sadness and disillusionment, are very affecting; they also provide an insight into Colquhoun’s own rapport with magic.

Eventually it becomes clear that Ménec is another Land of the Dead and that Brigid, Charlotte, and everyone there is a ghost, waiting in a limbo between bodily death and ultimate transfiguration*.* *I Saw Water* closes with a sublime sunset over the sea, in which Colquhoun’s painterly love of color, joined with her mystical Romantic tastes, carries Brigid into ecstatic communion with the elements. “There is a fluidity in Colquhoun’s work,” Mahon writes, “which echoes the flow of the sea or the wind, and which brings all the elements into harmony, usually through woman.”[4](#fn-4)

Ithell Colquhoun never gave up trying to find the combination that would unlock an emancipatory apocalypse. Like Italo Calvino, who played with a tarot pack to organize the plot of his novel *The Castle of Crossed Destinies* (1969), Colquhoun adopted magical structures to discern the meaning of the message from her exuberant profusion of her dream life. She was, however, acting in earnest. Whereas Breton wanted mysteries to remain mysteries and Calvino kept an ironical distance, Colquhoun had faith that everything, no matter how chaotic, ultimately has meaning and that with perseverance she would find it.
