# 正文

Mary K. Simkhovitch was born in Newton, Massachusetts, in 1867 to a family of well-to-do Episcopalians. Their house was full of air and light, the rooms were spacious, and the backyard was large. She never dreamed that everyone else in the world didn’t live as she lived, her biographer, Betty Boyd Caroli, tells us in *A Slumless America*: “People talked about having a house or wanting a ‘nice’ house, but ‘housing,’ especially with a capital H, never came up.” This is something of an irony as she, Mary Kingsbury, would grow up to become the most passionate advocate for public housing the United States would ever know.

The period in American history known as the Gilded Age—the years between the end of the Civil War and the 1890s—was, in the main, characterized by a rapacious industrialization that created wealth and power on an unimaginable scale as well as poverty of medieval proportions. Inequality barely suffices to describe the discrepancy between the lives of those who owned industry and the lives of those who served it. The former lived like royalty, the latter like chattel.

Between 1870 and 1900, 12 million immigrants arrived in America. Most of these people—dirt poor, undernourished, and uneducated—went to work in urban factories, where they labored sixty hours a week and earned pitifully low wages, and they lived in slum apartments where six or eight (or more) people were packed into two or three rooms the size of cubicles, enduring inadequate air and light, the toilet in the hall, barely enough food to stay alive, the tenement itself rife with disease, mothers dying like flies in childbirth, babies just dying.

By the late 1880s this state of affairs had sufficiently shocked the country’s idea of itself as a shining democracy to bring about the organized protest movement known collectively as the Progressive Era: a period lasting some thirty years (from the 1890s to the 1920s) during which hundreds of thousands of Americans became socialists or anarchists and hundreds of thousands more became reform Democrats. The anarchists and socialists wanted to bring down the capitalist system, while the reformers wanted America to deliver on its foundational promise of a decent life for one and all; very often the radicals and the reformers made common cause, working together on one of the major issues of the time: the horrifying poverty of immigrant workers, the struggle to organize labor, the ongoing fight for universal suffrage, and sometimes, with intermittent urgency, health care and decent housing.

Out of this extraordinary mix of turbulence and timeliness came a reform project known as the settlement house movement. The idea of the settlement house originated with Toynbee Hall in the UK. Located in London’s East End (the English Lower East Side), the hall was developed as a community center for the neighborhood’s working-class residents by a local vicar named Samuel Barnett and his wife, Henrietta. Within a short time, the Barnetts thought it efficacious to invite young university men who had come to the hall as volunteers to stay on as live-in residents; the intention was to break down the division between the classes and have people learn firsthand what life felt like for those in a class other than their own. This practice was known as “settling” the young men, and out of it came the term “settlement house.” Over many decades, countless thousands among those who grew up in the East End, as well as the young men who became residents, declared the experience of Toynbee Hall life-changing. The idea that the Oxbridge undergrad and the factory worker might have the same ambitions for themselves—a healthy family, a secure home, meaningful work—often came as a revelation when the undergrad and the worker shared stories at the hall.

It was a development whose moment had come. In 1886, after a visit to Toynbee Hall, the American Stanton Coit, a leader of the Society for Ethical Culture, opened the first US settlement house (the Neighborhood Guild on the Lower East Side), but it was only when Jane Addams opened Hull House in Chicago three years later that the movement caught fire. By 1897 there were seventy-four settlement houses across the United States, by the turn of the century more than a hundred. At its peak in the 1920s the movement boasted more than four hundred settlement houses nationwide.

After an initial visit to Toynbee Hall in 1887, Addams conceived the idea of starting a settlement house in America. At the time Addams was one of a rare category of persons: a twenty-six-year-old woman looking not to get married but to live on her own and do something useful with her life. A woman of independent means, she was so impressed at Toynbee Hall with the sight of the university men mingling with the poor “in the same style they would live in their own circle…so free from ‘professional doing good’” that, then and there, she imagined building such a project in America and devoting her life to it.

In *Citizen*, her biography of Addams, Louise Knight tells us that for Addams “settlement house” did not mean social reform or Christian instruction, as it often meant at other charitable institutions; rather it meant the idealism of making life better for the working class. The point of Hull House would be to promote the mutuality of democracy, not the condescension of charity. And indeed Hull House became a model of respectful social services. Here there were clubs, classes, counseling; you could study English or science, music or art, literature, sports, home economics; you could form a group or get financial advice. The place came to resemble a home away from home where lasting friendships were made and significant mentorships undertaken. For thousands of boys and girls, women and men, Hull House became the instrument of Americanization that an immigrant needed to take command of his or her own life. As for the residents, as at Toynbee Hall, those at Hull House were significantly changed by their experience of deep-dyed poverty coupled with smarts, courage, and endurance. There was however one major difference between the residents of Toynbee Hall and those at Hull House. At the hall they were all men; at Hull House they were all women.

In England the settlement house movement was dominated by men who considered themselves keepers of the culture: clergymen, professors, MPs. In America things turned out quite differently. Here it was the hour of the New Woman; a few hundred junior Jane Addamses were waiting in the wings to open or go to work in a settlement house. In the main—not only but in the main—this was a population of white, middle-class, Protestant young women whose minds and hearts were genuinely moved by the extraordinary spectacle of human suffering everywhere to hand, but fundamentally they were motivated by a serious hunger of their own to live lives of meaning: no small thing to insist on during a time when women had no rights, only obligations.

Inevitably many of these women, and often because of their settlement house experience, developed into seasoned progressives, not only becoming politically active on behalf of one or another of the social ills of the time but also rising to positions of local and national leadership. Just a handful of their names will make the point: Crystal Eastman in labor, Alice Paul in suffrage, Margaret Sanger in birth control, Alice Hamilton in public health, Frances Perkins in government itself. To this list we now add the name of Mary Kingsbury Simkhovitch, the founder in 1902 and director until 1946 of Greenwich House—one of New York City’s most prominent settlement houses.

The Episcopalian ministers of Mary Kingsbury’s youth emphasized that religion meant helping those who needed help; thus social reform movements in the late nineteenth century often began in church. While the Kingsburys were respectably observant churchgoers, Mary herself grew up intensely religious, especially aware of Christ’s injunction to take up arms against the many discrepancies between the lives of those born into privilege and those born into deprivation. From her youth Kingsbury felt drawn to the credo known as “social gospel.”

Intelligent and studious, she was sent to college and graduated from Boston University in 1890. Then what? What was she to do now? Unlike the girls she knew who were only waiting for an appropriate husband to appear, Mary longed for a purposeful life that might include but would not be defined by marriage. For a while she taught high school, but finding the students (all boys, of course) unbearable, she soon quit. At a similar time something odd happened that threatened to put her ambitions on hold: Mary developed pains in her legs so severe she began to limp, as well as a breathlessness that often kept her housebound. The pains in her back were called lumbago and the breathlessness asthma, but neither diagnosis seemed right, and in any case, no alleviation was forthcoming; however, the young Mary, devoted to living her life, taught herself to endure this debilitating condition.

Adrift at twenty-seven, she decided to enroll at the Harvard Annex (the first incarnation of Radcliffe College), where classes were being taught in the developing science that would soon be called sociology. She found herself drawn to better understand—through theory and statistics and direct observation—the causes of poverty and other social disabilities, and rather quickly, Caroli tells us, her mind and her heart were both engaged. She was hungry to think systematically about the social ills rooted in class. The year she spent at Harvard Annex was her initiation into what was to become her life’s work; what she herself called “practical sociology.”

Instead of remaining at home after that year at Harvard Annex, and despite her ongoing pains, Mary made her way to Denison House in Boston, a settlement house founded in 1890 by the College Settlements Association, which itself had been formed in 1890 to support and control settlement houses run and occupied almost entirely by women. Very early on, Caroli writes, one of the functions of the American settlement house had become providing “a place where ambitious, well-educated women…could connect and support each other both professionally and in their personal lives,” somewhat like the medieval convents that offered women an escape from unwanted marriages into a small world where a woman could live as an equal among equals.

At Denison House Mary drank in the idea that the conventional definition of true womanhood—characterized as a woman devoted to marriage and motherhood—could be traded in for a more worldly definition of one who administers to a community in need. She began to visit Black and immigrant families in their homes, where she asked questions and took notes. Very soon she was beginning to feel herself something of an activist; at Denison House she was seen as a “shining St. Joan, fair and radiant and armed with a sword.” Apparently Mary, tall, blonde, and blue-eyed, was a beauty as well as a born social worker.

In 1895, having become one of a handful of women accepted for graduate study at Friedrich Wilhelm University of Berlin, she traveled abroad with her mother. For both Kingsbury women, Europe was a revelation: music, theater, museums, all so much richer and grander than their American counterparts; and the clothes! the food! the way people treated one another in the street! “I can never tell you how I enjoy being in the midst of all this and hearing so much of interest,” her mother, Laura, wrote. “Everything here is massive and rich and you feel that the Germans never mean to be second in anything.” For Mary it was all pretty much heaven. Soon Laura was reporting home that Mary’s limp was noticeably diminished and then, within a matter of weeks, seemed to be permanently gone.

On the other hand, the sexism at the University was beyond anything Mary had ever experienced. The men, students and teachers alike, were at best deeply impolite, at worst openly rude. Some years earlier an American woman scientist, discussing male behavior at a neighboring university, had written an essay called “Before Women Were Human Beings.” There was, however, one man at the school who definitely looked *at* Mary, not through her. He was a Russian student of economics named Vladimir Simkhovitch: dark, vivid, wildly intelligent, a cocky live wire with no money of his own (he claimed his family had lost its fortune), Vladimir was also seven years younger than Mary.

Laura disliked him intensely—couldn’t believe this was her marvelous daughter’s destiny—and none of Mary’s friends thought him trustworthy. Mary herself, Caroli tells us, “had witnessed Vladimir’s exuberance, and she had equally registered his arrogance and tendency to demean others.” No matter. They were besotted with each other and could not be parted; against all odds, they kept their engagement alive for three years and were married in New York City in 1899, had two children, and lived, happily or perhaps unhappily (Caroli’s biography makes it hard to tell which), together until her death. Certainly, within their domestic commonality they lived very separate lives. He became a professor of economics at Columbia University as well as a collector of and authority on various kinds of art, with a richly full existence uptown. She, on the other hand, lived all the years at Greenwich House and became, well, Mary Kingsbury Simkhovitch.

Marrying Vladimir was the only transgressive act of Mary’s long life, but even then she seems never to have let marriage or motherhood influence her work, her morals, her deep regard for what she thought of as Christian values. It was not as though she was liberal minded on all scores; she was not; while she was deeply pained by immigrant poverty, she wasn’t at all exercised over the uniform exclusion of Black people from settlement house life. But as her greatest need was to get things done, this often meant *acting* like a liberal. Greenwich House was founded in Greenwich Village, where in the 1920s “art, love, and freedom were the watchwords” of the neighborhood’s bohemian population. Simkhovitch held with none of it. Free love, the downgrading of marriage, the easygoing acceptance of prostitution—it all horrified her. Nonetheless, when local artists offered to teach painting, sculpture, and poetry at Greenwich House she welcomed them. It was with her first job as a settlement house director—at the Friendly Aid Society—that she had learned to assess pretty accurately what she could and could not live with.

In late 1898, when she was preparing to marry Vladimir, who had no money and no visible means of support, Mary knew that their economic survival depended on her. A directorial job, which came with a salary and an apartment, materialized at the Friendly Aid settlement house in New York, and she applied for and got it, despite the fact that she was bringing along a foreign-born husband to live where no man had yet lived. Any and all objections at Friendly Aid were initially overridden by the skill and energy with which Mary immediately undertook the management of the incredible variety of activities that the house offered: classes in cooking, history, civics, and dramatics; a kindergarten; bathing facilities; an in-house bank—as well as three full meals a day. She also learned quickly to listen to what those who came to the house wanted to discuss rather than tell them what they should be discussing. For instance: she scheduled a talk on city government, and no one came; she scheduled a talk on the high rents in the tenements, and the place was packed. She rarely made that kind of mistake twice.

But the board of Friendly Aid and its dynamic young director were at odds on some major issues from the start. To begin with, the board insisted on Sunday evening Protestant church services while Mary was adamant about there being no place for religion (as such) in a settlement house, as the visitors to the house held a variety of religious beliefs. Then Mary had a baby, and the board objected vociferously to the presence of husband *and* child in the house. After three years of butting heads, Mary wanted out as much as Friendly Aid wanted her gone. No sooner had she left than she began making arrangements to open a settlement house herself. And now she came into her own. Greenwich House would be her great creation: the lasting institution she made out of an inner necessity that was perfectly attuned to its moment.

Two weeks after she left Friendly Aid, Simkhovitch, Caroli writes, “filed incorporating papers and named five of the most prominent men in New York as her backers”: Henry C. Potter, an Episcopalian bishop; Felix Adler, the founder of the Society for Ethical Culture; Eugene Philbin, a Catholic lawyer with political connections; Jacob Riis, the journalist who wrote *How the Other Half Lives*; and Carl Schurz, a US senator. Just to read this list is to see at once Simkhovitch’s gift for organizing, how wide ranging it was and how strongly it promised to ensure success. Her ability to make the necessary connections, and to keep the loyalty of those connections over the years it took to make the project come to fruition, was nothing short of remarkable.

On Thanksgiving Day in 1902 Simkhovitch’s settlement house opened its doors on Jones Street, a narrow, dirty block close to Washington Square in lower Manhattan. While many of those living in the tenements that lined the street were native Americans, many more were immigrants from Ireland, Germany, France, and England; after 1900 came the Italians whose devotion to Greenwich House increased by the generation.

Caroli writes that “a look at Simkhovitch’s professional calendar” that first winter at Greenwich House shows what she would be up against for the next fifty years: the house started a kindergarten, ran a number of boys’ clubs, and opened a station of the Penny Provident Bank; it also boasted a small circulating library, a lunch club for the factory girls next door, and classes in English, sewing and upholstery, basket making, cooking, and dancing. From the start, Greenwich House placed a heavy emphasis on arts education and practice; within a decade it had developed what to this day survives as its famous music school—originally housed in the Barrow Street Brownstone that would later serve as Greenwich House headquarters—and an equally famous pottery studio.

Greenwich House—because it was Simkhovitch’s creation—also aspired to matter as a social service organization, and for over a century, it has. During World War I it served as the headquarters for the local Red Cross and also helped the Food and Fuel Administrations distribute resources; during the Great Depression more than 10,000 people a week received material relief at the house; in the 1960s it received a grant from the National Institutes of Health to establish the first outpatient counseling center for drug treatment; and in 1987 it became the first settlement house to respond to the AIDS crisis in New York by starting the AIDS Mental Health Project. In short, for 124 years and counting the settlement house founded and run by Simkhovitch has earned a celebrated place in the nation’s long record of citizen-activated responses to a social crisis.

Greenwich House is not the only reason we must honor Simkhovitch today. Many elements of life in the slums made her heart ache—infant mortality, working conditions in the factories, the high rate of school dropouts—but above all it was the tenement apartments themselves that broke her: the dark misery of inner rooms with too little air, light, or breathing space, where young and old, male and female, sick or well crowded together like creatures rather than people. This, it seemed to her, was the very definition of a society treating its citizens as though they were less than human. It was intolerable; therefore she would not tolerate it.

Within ten years of the opening of Greenwich House, Simkhovitch was organizing independent support on behalf of slum clearance to be accompanied by newly designed tenement housing that would be made as livable for the working class as it might be for the middle class. Private money could not possibly foot the bill for all of this; the project would have to be subsidized by the government. Public housing was the name of the game. Progressive activists gathered eagerly in support of the mission while conservatives just as quickly sent up the dread cry of “Socialism!”

The suffrage movement had spawned many women’s organizations sympathetic to causes other than their own, and Simkhovitch now appealed on behalf of public housing to some of these organizations, among them the Women’s City Club (founded in New York in 1916), whose membership included the young Eleanor Roosevelt; the senior Roosevelts had been family friends since Simkhovitch’s childhood, and now Eleanor became her good friend and political ally. In this way Simkhovitch developed an ever-widening base of support for her growing insistence that the government be made responsible for public housing.

When in 1926 the New York State Housing Act was passed, bringing state government “into the housing picture in new ways,” Simkhovitch had every right to feel proud and hopeful. But it was a cause that had long had many devoted supporters as well as even more determined enemies, the stigma of socialism dragging it down into defeat at almost every turn. In 1930 Simkhovitch was still lamenting that for more than 1.5 million New Yorkers, as Caroli writes, “little had changed since 1919 census reports showed that fully one third of Americans lived ‘under subnormal housing conditions.’” And two years later she was forced to repeat, “The New York tenement still stands as a disgrace to the richest city in the world.”

Yet in November of 1933 the progressive Fiorello La Guardia was elected the ninety-ninth mayor of New York City, and the following February the New York City Housing Authority was established to carry out “the clearance, replanning and reconstruction of the areas in which unsanitary or substandard housing conditions exists.” The authority had a membership of five. One of the five was Simkhovitch.

La Guardia’s establishment of the New York Housing Authority was only the beginning of the long, tortured history of public housing in America. To this day, with all its years of struggle, and all its moments of partial advance, the cause of public housing can only be called a mean, pinched affair, serving only a fraction of the population for which it is needed. And yet, and yet.

Very often, while writing this essay, I’ve mentioned Simkhovitch’s name to one informed friend or another, only to hear my friend say, “They don’t make ’em like that anymore.” And always I’ve thought, “Not true.” I’ve never forgotten how in the days after *Roe* v. *Wade* was overturned by the Supreme Court, dozens of grassroots organizations rallied online to raise travel money for women living in states where abortion was now prohibited. I thought then of the great settlement house women of a hundred years ago, and how they would have instantly gone to work.
