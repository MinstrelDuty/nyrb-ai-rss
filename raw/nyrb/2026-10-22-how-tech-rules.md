# 正文

One of the hallmarks of the modern corporation is the separation of ownership from control. The firm is owned by shareholders but led by managers, who answer to shareholders only indirectly. Actually, it’s weirder than that. The corporation owns itself. Shareholders merely own *stock*, which is a claim on the firm’s assets and earnings.

Elements of this arrangement have been around for centuries. Scholars have detected traces of early “joint-stock” companies in tenth-century China. In 1602 the Dutch East India Company conducted the first initial public offering in history, floating its shares on the newly created Amsterdam stock exchange. But the publicly traded corporation achieved a new prominence in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, during the period of upheaval and dynamism known as the second industrial revolution.

Observing the situation in Germany—the only other country that was industrializing as rapidly as the United States at the time—a young Viennese economist named Rudolf Hilferding saw a new stage of capitalism emerging. He called it “finance capital” in an influential book of that title published in 1910. At the heart of Hilferding’s analysis was a simple observation. Building the railroads, stockyards, and steel mills of the new economy required large sums of money. These sums could not be sourced from companies’ existing revenue. They had to be obtained from external parties, by printing pieces of paper that told stories about the future: bonds that promised a stream of interest payments and stocks that promised a rising share price and a portion of the profits distributed as dividends.

Capital markets in London, New York, and Berlin arose to facilitate the buying and selling of these pieces of paper. In doing so, they effectively pooled wealth from across the world and put it at the service of industry. “The obstacles which arise from the fragmentation of capital among a host of indifferent and casual owners are removed,” wrote Hilferding. “The corporation can draw directly upon the combined capital of the capitalist class.”

Thus unfettered, an enterprise could grow larger. In Hilferding’s view, finance capital initiated a new era of monopoly, spawning giant trusts and cartels that did not compete in the traditional sense. Rather, they acted as market planners, coordinating investment and production across the global economy. By holding controlling stakes in networks of subsidiaries, men like J.P. Morgan and John D. Rockefeller could personally administer entire sectors. The separation of ownership from control had, paradoxically, created new ways to recombine the two in more concentrated form.

More than a hundred years later, we are living through another era of monopoly. Finance is still with us—and even more integral to the economy than it was in Hilferding’s day—but the seat of oligarchic power is American tech. Five companies in California and Washington have a combined market capitalization of about $20 trillion. Earlier this year Elon Musk became humanity’s first trillionaire by taking SpaceX—a company whose monstrous valuation rests in large part on the promise of putting AI data centers in space—public. A trillion is a number that, like space, operates at a scale that strains human understanding. One is reminded of the narrator in Thomas Mann’s *Doctor Faustus*, who denounces the chilly vastness of the cosmos as “Luciferian.”

Remarkably, such figures actually understate the importance of the tech sector. As the law professor Frank Pasquale has argued, the top firms and their leaders are not only very rich but “functional sovereigns,” choreographing our lives with a thoroughness that would be the envy of the tsars.

*The Rulers: Corporate Power in the Age of AI and the Cloud* is the most comprehensive picture yet of how our new sovereigns acquire and exercise power. Written by the Argentine economist Cecilia Rikap, the book doesn’t begin with technology, or even with the tech sector. Instead, Rikap’s starting point is the “intellectual monopoly.” Intellectual monopolies are “organizations that systematically capture rents derived from their sustained concentration of intangible assets,” she writes. In her view, Coca-Cola is an intellectual monopoly. So are Disney, Pfizer, and Nike.

An intangible asset, according to the definition that accountants learn in school, is an asset that lacks a physical form: patents, trademarks, copyrights, trade secrets. These are the sorts of intangible assets that are protected by intellectual property (IP) law, and they are the basis for some very profitable lines of business. Coca-Cola has its secret recipe; Disney has Donald Duck. But “IP only covers part of a much more extensive monopolization of knowledge, data and narratives,” notes Rikap. She is far more interested in how companies collect and analyze information about their suppliers, franchises, customers, and employees. This might involve tracking the flow of shipping containers around the world or the movement of workers around a warehouse. It might involve scrutinizing a consumer’s purchase history or a shop’s sales history. Data can be gleaned from any source, and hammered with software until it yields a useful insight.

Rikap traces the origins of intellectual monopolization in the broadest sense to the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries—Hilferding’s era. It was then, she argues, that several kinds of intangible assets first became essential to the world economy. One of these was scientific knowledge. Historically, the scientist was typically a gentleman ensconced in a private home laboratory. But as advances in chemistry and physics yielded valuable new methods and machinery, industrialists began putting scientists on their payroll and building R&D labs, while “polytechnic” schools on the German model churned out engineers.

The emergence of a mass consumer base, meanwhile, compelled companies to differentiate their products by investing in another kind of intangible asset: branding. Among the pioneers was Coca-Cola, which designed a distinctive glass bottle for its beverages, blanketed cities with colorful billboards, and gave away branded clocks, calendars, and wallets. Brand management became a major concern in the postwar decades, as what Allen Ginsberg called the “nitroglycerine shrieks of the fairies of advertising”—he worked for five years on Madison Avenue, unhappily—began their long assault on our senses.

In these years the United States was mostly in the business of making stuff. You needed the Don Drapers to sell what rolled off the assembly line, and the scientists to invent transistors and nylons. But these “knowledge workers,” as they would later come to be called, still served an economy centered on manufacturing, which didn’t peak as a percentage of total employment until 1953 and whose workforce kept growing in absolute terms until 1979.

It was in that last decade that the relationship between ideas and industry decisively changed. The American economy had been slowly deindustrializing since the end of World War II, but the process accelerated in the 1970s, as American companies started outsourcing the manufacture of physical goods to overseas suppliers in much larger numbers. While these suppliers were separate companies, in practice they served at the pleasure of the outsourcing firm, whose power lay in the possession of certain lucrative intangible assets. Rikap cites the example of Nike. The Swoosh, the designs, and the attendant IP are the real sources of value. The shoes themselves are made by contractors in countries such as China and Vietnam.

Nike tells its suppliers what to make and how much it’s willing to pay. It is a market planner, in other words, comparable to the financiers that fascinated Hilferding. But finance capital exerted control through ownership: Rockefeller called the shots because he owned pieces of many different companies. Intellectual monopolization, by contrast, is distinguished by what Rikap describes as “control beyond ownership.” A company like Nike still engages in more conventional forms of monopolistic behavior, like buying up competitors. But the innovation of intellectual monopoly capitalism is that capitalists don’t always need to incur the costs of ownership in order to enjoy its benefits. They can boss people around without taking responsibility for them.

Computers made it possible to implement this kind of dominance at a distance. When industry went overseas, it also changed form, evolving from the large, vertically integrated enterprises associated with Henry Ford into disaggregated ensembles of mines, mills, workshops, and sheds woven together in global supply chains. Monitoring and managing this complex distributed system would be impossible without computers. Supply chains are, as the scholar Miriam Posner argues in a forthcoming book, artifacts of software.[1](#fn-1) Among the legacies of the digital revolution are not only the PC and the Internet but a world where something as simple as a pair of socks is constructed through the combined efforts of contractors in several countries before landing in your local Gap.

The tech sector didn’t just facilitate the rise of intellectual monopoly capitalism, however. It has also produced the most successful intellectual monopolies. Software is an excellent intangible asset. The philosopher George Caffentzis observes that capitalist commodity production always involves “the reproduction of a pattern,” and the patterns of software cost nearly nothing to reproduce. This is the magic money tree that, by 1995, had made Bill Gates the richest man in the world.[2](#fn-2)

But Rikap identifies another reason for Big Tech’s success: in its most recent iteration, it has discovered a way to outsource the creation of intangible assets. The archetypal case is that of Internet platforms. As everyone (hopefully) knows, companies like Google and Facebook make money from the data that we exude by searching, liking, and posting, which is used to sell targeted advertisements. The systems that supervise this churn are created and maintained by programmers sitting in Silicon Valley, but the material is supplied by us.

For this reason, the returns on platforms are even better than those from traditional software. Gates and his employees had to make their intangible assets themselves. What the platforms have managed to do is take the logic of outsourcing one step further and turn us all into subcontracted (though uncompensated) knowledge workers. This innovation, Rikap suggests, has reached its apotheosis with large language models (LLMs), which can be considered “co-produced by everyone connected to the internet” through the training data we provide with our collective activity.

The platforms’ chief technology for harvesting the Internet’s intellectual value is the cloud. Concretely, the cloud refers to the planetary archipelago of data centers that started to appear in the 2000s, initially as the enabling infrastructure for the platforms. Then in 2006 Amazon began selling computer storage and processing capacity as an on-demand service to other companies. This inaugurated the business of cloud computing, which has grown enormously over the past two decades. (Amazon Web Services posted a profit of $45.6 billion last year.) From the Pentagon to the CIA, hospitals to Hollywood to Wall Street, everyone uses the cloud. Estimates vary, but the evidence suggests that the vast majority of the world’s organizations rely on the technology in some form.

As a result, the “hyperscalers” that own the largest clouds—Amazon, Google, and Microsoft—effectively run the information layer of the global economy. While most intellectual monopolies are “confined to specific sectors,” these firms control “digital production, exchange, and consumption networks” in such a way that enables them “to subordinate organizations across virtually all industries,” writes Rikap, including other intellectual monopolies. The machinery of the cloud is always humming in the background of our economic lives.

Let’s say you want to buy a lawnmower. You do a Google search, and Home Depot pops up. You look at the options and find one you like. The site says it’s in stock at a store a few miles away. You drive over, look at it, ask the guy working there a few questions, and decide to buy it.

Where does the cloud fit into this sequence of events? For one thing, it ran the Web applications that helped you find the lawnmower, from Google to the Home Depot site. And, as you might expect, data was created about you along the way, such that you will be seeing ads for lawnmowers everywhere you look online from now on, perhaps for the rest of your life. But the cloud also coordinated the various corporate systems that made your purchase possible, from the software that ensures lawnmowers are in stock at that particular Home Depot to the program that tells headquarters when it’s time to reorder more lawnmowers from the supplier to the service that manages the payroll and schedules the hours of the employee who helped you.

Here in the single grain of a Sunday afternoon lives a galaxy of computation. Multiply the transaction by a billion or two and you get a sense of how much information passes through the hands of the hyperscalers each day. (Home Depot, incidentally, uses Google Cloud.) Not only do they extract a steady stream of rents from the millions of enterprises that rely on them, they acquire “a vantage point over the global economy by hosting its *digital twin*,” writes Rikap. This gives them a real-time map of world commerce from which they can “extract economic insights” and “infer business trends.” No capitalist in history has enjoyed such a panoptic view of market civilization.

But maybe a map isn’t quite the right metaphor. The cloud doesn’t just model the economy; the two are, increasingly, co-constitutive. At a certain resolution, a copy becomes indistinguishable from the original. In the early 1980s the philosopher Jean Baudrillard coined the term “hyperreality” to describe this phenomenon. He was thinking mostly of television, but the most hyperreal thing ever invented is the cloud.

The most inspired moments in *The Rulers* come from Rikap’s unorthodox use of dependency theory, a social-scientific tradition cofounded by another Argentine economist, Raúl Prebisch, that focuses on the problem of global poverty. Its adherents in the decolonizing 1960s and 1970s argued that poor countries were poor because rich countries were rich: the latter composed the exploiting core of the “world system,” the former its exploited periphery.

Rikap’s move is to apply this core-and-periphery schema to tech. Dependency theorists wanted to explain how the West kept extracting value from the Third World even after colonial rule had ended. In other words, they were trying to understand a kind of “control beyond ownership.” In recent years writers have often reached for the language of imperialism and colonialism when describing the rapacious behavior and globe-spanning ambitions of Silicon Valley.[3](#fn-3) The implicit argument of *The Rulers* is that a better analogy is the world system that arose *after* decolonization, in which the old machinery of empire had to be reengineered for a new era of nominally sovereign states. The contractors linked together by the global supply chains that began to emerge in this period were, like the countries themselves, formally but not functionally independent of their masters in the metropole.

In Rikap’s account, Silicon Valley inherits and intensifies this logic. It organizes multiple peripheries, each populated by entities that are technically separate from the monopolizing core. Through platforms, the cloud, and AI, “Big Tech companies systematically transform knowledge and information produced by each and every individual and organization into their assets,” Rikap writes. “They are, in essence, the world’s largest knowledge predators.”

Tech’s peripheries don’t exist just online, however. They also take less digitized forms. One important periphery for tech is academia, Rikap explains. Industry giants “curate research fields according to their interests” by partnering with universities and collaborating with academic researchers. The knowledge is produced in common, but its value accrues to industry. “Between 2012 and 2021,” Rikap writes, “Microsoft co-authored 88 per cent of its more than 15,000 scientific publications with other organizations but shared ownership of only 1 per cent of its 35,233 patents.”

Then there is the start-up world, where companies like Microsoft, Google, and Amazon are major investors, which gives them “preferential access to capabilities, knowledge, and information” held by smaller firms operating at the cutting edge. Because they tend to move faster and take bigger risks, these firms effectively function as outsourced R&D labs. The monopolies can enjoy access to start-ups’ technology and expertise without shouldering the responsibilities of an acquisition. Tech companies still do, of course, buy some businesses directly. But they mainly interact with start-ups in their capacity as venture capitalists, which offers yet another channel for exerting “control beyond ownership.”

In fact, this “pattern of innovation,” to quote Rikap, is what brought about the current AI boom. In 2019 Microsoft made its first investment in OpenAI. The deal gave OpenAI use of Microsoft’s cloud infrastructure, which was required to train the computationally intensive LLMs that OpenAI’s scientists had begun to piece together. Microsoft, in turn, “effectively steered the company’s research priorities, projects and overall direction—from code to product development,” writes Rikap. When OpenAI struck gold with the release of ChatGPT in 2022, its patron reaped the rewards. The following year, Microsoft’s stock price rose nearly 60 percent, propelled in large part by its status as the exclusive reseller of OpenAI’s technology through the cloud.

Contemporary tech oligarchy also resembles a core-periphery arrangement in the more literal, geographic sense. Most of the planet lives under American tech hegemony; even advanced economies such as Europe and Japan have nothing comparable to Silicon Valley. The great exception is China, which has done vastly more than any other country to resist peripheralization. In recent years this has become a problem for Western capital, albeit one of its own making.

The outsourcing blitz of the 1970s that Rikap associates with the birth of intellectual monopoly capitalism coincided with China’s tentative embrace of private enterprise, initiated by Deng Xiaoping at the end of the decade. Over the years that followed, the Pearl River Delta became an industrial megacity, far larger than Manchester or Detroit at their height, as China took on the tasks that the West was busily offshoring.

But China had the means to aspire to more. It began climbing the value chain, muscling into higher-end sectors and creating intellectual monopolies of its own: national champions in advanced industries, guided by the “technodevelopmentalism” policies of the Communist Party. Sooner or later, Western firms were alarmed to find their contractors becoming their competition.

Meanwhile, US companies doing business in China “found that the benefits of access were less than imagined,” as the political economist Nick Srnicek notes in his new book, *Silicon Empires: The Fight for the Future of AI*. Foreign firms were often required to form joint ventures with domestic ones, which enabled Chinese companies to gain technical expertise, much to the irritation of the Americans.

The resulting frictions caused some American business leaders to grow wary of China, creating an opening for the hawks within the national security state to reassert themselves. Obama announced his “Pivot to Asia” in 2011, an attempt at diplomatic and military containment; Trump stoked rising tensions into a trade war in 2018; Biden added a raft of export controls after entering the White House in 2021 and started talking about a global struggle between “democracies” and “autocracies.” More recently, Trump has fashioned a kind of détente with Xi Jinping, reducing tariffs and loosening restrictions on the sale of AI chips, although most of the Biden-era export controls remain in place.

Srnicek is one of the most perceptive scholars of our networked age. His *Platform Capitalism* (2016) is a much-cited taxonomy of the economic arrangements created by Google, Uber, and others. *Silicon Empires* is a sequel of sorts, applying the same clinical precision to the commotion around generative AI. But what makes the book especially valuable is how it situates the AI boom within a wider set of political and geopolitical developments. These developments could be described as an inflection point in the evolution of intellectual monopoly capitalism—a new stage in Rikap’s story.

Understanding this latest shift requires looking at the relationship between intellectual monopolies and the state. Historically, intellectual monopolies wanted a relatively hands-off government, one that cleared the field for “market forces” by encouraging global economic liberalization. This held true for Silicon Valley until fairly recently. From the 1990s until the mid-2010s, there was a “broad agreement across both the political elite and tech elite,” Srnicek writes, that the Internet would “create an American-led world of borderless commerce and data.” The “Silicon Valley Consensus,” as he calls it, was to seek maximum globalization with minimal regulation. Economic interests were paramount; national security concerns “took a backseat.” And the Chinese market was a top priority. In 2015 Mark Zuckerberg traveled to Beijing and asked Xi to give his unborn daughter an honorary Chinese name. (Xi declined.)

Over the last decade or so, two forces have combined to pull the Silicon Valley Consensus apart. The first is what some commentators call “deglobalization”: countries around the world are drifting away from global economic integration. In the aftermath of the market shocks inflicted by the pandemic, Russia’s full-scale invasion of Ukraine, and the US–Israeli attack on Iran, among other factors, many political leaders have come to see their exposure to world markets as a source of risk, to be mitigated by bringing production back within their borders (“reshoring”) or those of allied countries (“friendshoring”), or pursuing other efforts to shorten and secure supply chains. Matters once regarded as purely economic are now downstream of war.

The second threat to the consensus is the precipitous growth of generative AI. As a technology, generative AI is capital-intensive. It requires a lot of data centers, which in turn require a lot of energy. For this Silicon Valley needs help from the government. The Trump administration has been happy to oblige, opening up federal land for data center construction, scuttling environmental review, and even offering financial support. But the resource requirements of AI are so great that the industry has to venture overseas to meet them. “In a move reminiscent of earlier forms of imperialism, AI companies are pushing abroad to get more data to train their models, more energy to run their data centres, more water to cool their servers, more land to house it all, and more capital to fund their projects,” writes Srnicek. “All of this requires significant support from the state to be achieved.”

Equally important is finding customers. The high cost of AI development means that international expansion is essential for obtaining a decent return on investment. The goal, Srnicek explains, is to lock other countries into the American AI “stack,” such that they become reliant on Nvidia chips, Amazon data centers, Anthropic models, and the rest. This too requires coordination between state and capital: given the financial and infrastructural demands of AI, foreign governments are necessarily involved.

So is the US government, which has an obvious interest in seeing nations increase their dependency on Silicon Valley while, at the same time, restricting the sale of advanced AI models and semiconductors to those it considers security threats. In early 2025 the Biden administration announced its “AI Diffusion Rule,” which sorted the world into three tiers: close allies like European countries would face the fewest hurdles, adversaries like China and Russia would be embargoed, and everyone else would have to apply for a license to import AI technology. Using export controls to hobble China serves the double duty of undermining both a geopolitical rival and an economic competitor, one with a dynamic tech sector that presents a threat to American AI dominance. All this brings us some distance from the globalizing 1990s, 2000s, and early 2010s, when the young Zuckerberg could tell people that Facebook was founded “to make the world more open and connected.”

One hundred and sixteen years ago, Hilferding noticed a similar transition taking place. The new conglomerates and cartels required more robust forms of government intervention than those offered by the minimalist “night-watchman state” of an earlier era (in the famous phrase of the German socialist Ferdinand Lassalle). They needed a state that could protect industry from overseas competition and facilitate “the conquest of foreign markets”—including through literal conquest. In Hilferding’s view, the high prices charged by monopolies reduced domestic demand for their goods, forcing them to expand abroad, and economic expansionism often went hand-in-hand with the territorial sort.[4](#fn-4) These were the years when European and Japanese armies were carving up Africa and Asia, sparking a series of inter-imperial scuffles that eventually escalated into World War I. So far, the 2020s have more than a passing resemblance to Hilferding’s 1910s: hostile blocs of state–capital hybrids, partitioning the globe.

The comparison is not comforting. It suggests that we are sleepwalking our way into the next world war. Still, Srnicek offers reasons to hope that history is unlikely to take exactly the same course. Among the things that differentiate our present moment from “previous periods of great power struggle” is “the scale of interdependence that ties together competing states.” The United States and China remain profoundly intertwined despite more than ten years of economic rivalry. It’s not possible to fully decouple them, even if, as Srnicek says, “we are moving into a far more fragmented and multipolar world.” The deep architecture of globalization will probably keep nuclear armageddon at bay.

Another pinprick of hope can be found in the new importance of the state. AI holds out the promise of intellectual monopolization in its highest and final form. The ambition of the AI sector is to absorb all human knowledge and use it to automate all human labor. The financial rewards for doing so would be incalculable. But this dream cannot be pursued without an active government partner, which presents an opening for the left. The industry’s growing reliance on Washington makes it more susceptible to political pressure. If and when Congress and the White House change hands, such leverage might be applied toward progressive ends.

Hilferding, for his part, saw the monopoly capitalism of his day as the antechamber of socialism. Finance capital had already done the hard work of unifying the economy; all the workers had to do was seize it for themselves. One can hear echoes of this line of thinking in Bernie Sanders’s recent proposal for a sovereign wealth fund that holds equity in the largest AI companies. The shares would be obtained through a onetime tax, and could earn dividends large enough to provide all citizens with an annual payment of $1,000.

Reasonable people can disagree about whether we should tie the financial fortunes of every American household even more tightly to those of Silicon Valley. But something of comparable ambition will be needed to reckon with the inegalitarian forces that AI represents. Rikap calls for “governing knowledge production as a commons,” by which she means not only distributing its benefits more widely but also “democratically deciding what knowledge—and what technologies—are produced and how.” The choice isn’t between markets and planning, she reminds us, but between different types of planners: the monopolists sitting in Menlo Park and Mountain View, chasing the next trillion, or the rest of the human race.
