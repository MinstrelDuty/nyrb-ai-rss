# 正文

By then she was mostly painting views from above. Though she had studied at the Rhode Island School of Design with a former World War II pilot who did abstract aerials, and who encouraged her to try painting from bridges, it was only on a cross-country flight to San Diego in 1969 that she discovered “a good little studio”—her seat on the plane. “You had a little shelf,” she once told an interviewer, “and if I had watercolors with me, I could just start work.” *From the Air* begins with early studies of clouds seen from a window seat, the window itself conjured with finely nested strokes of darkening pigment. As she flew more often, first commercially and then on planes chartered for her artistic purposes, Jacquette opted for pastels, with which she made vigorous map-like drawings. These high-altitude views, particularly nighttime scenes of snaking blue and orange highways, tend toward abstraction, whereas her cityscapes made in lofty hotel rooms or tall towers (including the World Trade Center), or from photographs she took in low-flying helicopters, pulse with Cubist rhythm.

Jacquette also flew above movements and schools, as did the informal head of her circle, Fairfield Porter, and her friends Sylvia Plimack Mangold, Jane Freilicher, and Nell Blaine, who exploited Abstract Expressionist and Minimalist innovations to reinvigorate figuration. The art historian Linda Nochlin once suggested that Jacquette and her peers reclaimed the domestic sphere, and realism, for inquiry into the nature of seeing. From a certain angle these pastels and paintings might be read as satires of stereotypically female art: the interior of an airliner is as far from domestic as can be, and Jacquette’s figuration sometimes hardly seems figurative at all.

Most of her oils are gathered in the show’s airy final room. Perhaps the most interesting is the one in which Jacquette shows her hand, the nocturne *Paper Co., Somerset, Maine II* (1994). The titular factory blazes with red, orange, and white light, which illuminates the vapor billowing from its twin smokestacks, but at the edges of the painting the complex is traced only in white outline on the dark teal base layer, like a blueprint. (She once had a day job making technical drawings.) It’s as if Jacquette wants to remind us that a picture isn’t ever actually a window, and that painting is always, to use her word, a “fiction” that plays out on the surface of the canvas, no matter how distant the view.

Jacquette gave “intimacy to vastness,” as she once put it, making unlikely paintings of unlikely subjects: clogged highways, industrial sites, the nuclear power plant at Three Mile Island, and strip malls, as in the charmingly odd *Walmart and Other ‘Big Box’ Stores, Augusta, ME II* (2006), whose parking lot she accents with neon pink and green straight from an arcade carpet. Most intimate of all is the sparest, and vastest, of all these paintings, *Route One, Maine* (2008), whose blurry yellow, red, white, and blue lights cluster in a black expanse. It seems we’re angled forward, flying ever deeper into the night. Just as her friend Janet Fish sought to reanimate the still life by putting Cézanne’s apples under shrink-wrap, Jacquette modernized the landscape by showing what it actually looks like now. Her aerial views are paintings of modern life. It’s fitting, then, that they’re also a little lonely.
