# 正文

As we attempt to survive a government more given to punches than nudges, it is now possible to ask what remains of the decades-long project of applying behavioral economics to public policy. In the spring of 2008, a few weeks after the collapse of Bear Stearns but two months before Barack Obama secured the Democratic nomination, Richard Thaler and Cass Sunstein published their book *Nudge: Improving Decisions About Health, Wealth, and Happiness*. Thaler was one of the founders of the field of behavioral economics and went on to win the Nobel Prize in 2017. Sunstein was, and remains, probably the most prominent and influential legal scholar in the United States, and he is the author of dozens of books, four of them in 2024 alone. *Nudge* was a stampeding best seller, republished in a thoroughly revised version in 2021. In the meantime Sunstein, who had been a colleague and informal adviser of Obama’s at the University of Chicago Law School, served as the administrator of the White House Office of Information and Regulatory Affairs (sometimes known as the “regulatory czar”) from 2009 to 2012. He returned as senior counselor to the secretary of Homeland Security during the Biden administration.

*Nudge* set out the findings from decades of research in behavioral economics and the policy agenda that resulted. As Thaler recounts in his book *Misbehaving* (2015), which is a kind of memoir and tour through the history of the subject, behavioral economics began in the 1970s with the observation that most real people do not behave like the efficient, rational, calculating agents that economic theory presumes them to be. They make mistakes, they have cognitive and behavioral biases, they work from imperfect information, and they “miswant,” meaning they want things that they know are harmful, or at least they behave in ways that are inconsistent with their stated goals. Thaler set out to develop what he called “an enriched approach to doing economic research,” one that was “not a different discipline: it is still economics, but it is economics done with strong injections of good psychology and other social sciences…to improve the accuracy of the predictions made with [economic] theories.” He drew on research in behavioral psychology, especially the work of Daniel Kahneman, the author of the best-selling *Thinking, Fast and Slow* (2011) and himself a Nobel laureate in 2002.

By the early 2000s Thaler and his colleagues (including Sunstein) had developed a concept they called “libertarian paternalism.” They argued that there are many situations in which an individual or organization must make a choice that will shape or limit the choices of others, and since there is no such thing as a neutral choice, that makes some form of paternalism inevitable. Recognizing this means selecting policies that make affected parties better off by uncoercively shaping the “choice architecture” they face. As Thaler and Sunstein put it, “It is legitimate for choice architects to try to influence people’s behavior in order to make their lives longer, healthier, and better.”

The famous example that opens *Nudge* is a cafeteria line. We might believe that it is good for people to eat healthy food, and indeed that they *want* to eat healthy food, but sometimes they make mistakes and eat an entire pizza instead. So the cafeteria can put the healthy food at the front of the line where hungry people will likely take more of it instead of the less healthy options further along. The cafeteria has to put *something* first, and people are still *choosing* what to eat instead of being coerced, so the principles of libertarian paternalism are satisfied. Or, as Thaler put it, “Our premise was simple. Because people are Humans, not Econs…they make predictable errors. If we can anticipate those errors, we can devise policies that will reduce the error rate.”

Other examples proliferated. If we want people to save for retirement, opting in to saving should be the default, not opting out. If we want more organ donors, we should make people choose one way or another when they renew their driver’s licenses. If we want men to navigate urinals more competently, we should give them a target to aim at. These ideas set fire to the minds of policymakers around the world after the 2008 crisis. David Cameron established a Nudge Unit in the UK upon taking office in 2010, and by 2014 there were 136 countries that claimed to incorporate behavioral sciences into policymaking, fifty-one of them with centrally directed policy initiatives.

Reviewing *Nudge* in these pages, the journalist John Cassidy pointed out that the Obama administration’s economic philosophy wasn’t Keynesianism or Chicago-school libertarianism but behavioralism. Indeed, in September 2015 the administration issued Executive Order 13707 to “more fully realize the benefits of behavioral insights and deliver better results at a lower cost for the American people.” In retrospect, the nudge movement was emblematic of 2010s technocratic neoliberalism, combining an abiding faith in free markets as solutions to social problems with an antiseptic dislike of the real work of politics as well as an affinity for cleverness in policy design. Reading the books of its leading proponents now feels like the excavation of a lost world.

In their new book, *It’s On You*, Nick Chater and George Loewenstein come to bury the nudgers, not to praise them. Chater is a professor of behavioral science at Warwick Business School, and Loewenstein was a student of Thaler’s and is himself one of the central pillars of behavioral economics. Thaler describes him as one of the “leaders of the field” and the only one who “has really created much new psychology.” As a result, the book has undertones of apostasy and parricide. It is a critique from within, showing a wealth of insider knowledge and personal familiarity with behavioral economics and its practitioners.

In Chater and Loewenstein’s diagnosis, behavioral economists and their psychologist colleagues have been too “focused on the shortcomings of individuals” and have been busy “running experiments and documenting the many ‘imperfections’ of human judgment and decision-making.” This unreflective tendency to use the sovereign individual as the unit of analysis has meant that the entire field has been “misled into believing that individuals created these problems, and that individuals can fix them.”

Chater and Loewenstein call this way of thinking the “i-frame,” counterposed to the “s-frame” of systemic approaches to social problems. (That awkward nomenclature is one of the many ways this book still follows the genre conventions of pop behavioralism.) “Thinking in terms of the i-frame,” they write,

> we take the rules of the game as fixed and ask how we can encourage or help the players to play the game better. Thinking in terms of the s-frame, we take human nature as fixed and ask how the rules of the game can be reshaped to produce better outcomes.

But really the book is not a methodological intervention or an argument for how to incorporate systemic approaches into behavioral models. It is a political economy critique. “Rather than i-frame policies arising organically by popular demand,” they write, “or out of policymakers’ natural inclinations, we found in case after case that there were *corporate interests* advancing i-frame solutions and perspectives.” In subject after subject and field after field, Chater and Loewenstein set out to show that the behavioral science experts have been at best the useful idiots of corporate interests or at worst their paid henchmen. As they put it:

> While publicly advocating the i-frame, corporations spend substantial resources on lobbying, funding think tanks, and sponsoring academic research to ensure that the game is, and remains, rigged in their favor. *This is the corporate sleight of hand: point to the i-frame act on the s-frame; rig the rules of the game, but blame individual citizens for any resulting social ills*.

Chater and Loewenstein pursue this line of argument through chapters focused on several examples: climate change and the idea of carbon footprints, food and obesity, retirement saving, the American health care industry, and then in a kind of lightning-round chapter on plastic waste, privacy laws, prescription drug addiction, gun violence, and automobile deaths. In each case they show the same pattern: “attempts by industry to cast problems as the responsibility of individuals, coupled with lobbying to maintain the status quo.”

Their account of industry spending extends beyond lobbyists to policy practitioners and researchers. For example, “while academics who take positions supporting the food industry are well funded, those who criticize the industry are often undermined, either by the industry’s enlisting the support of other industry-aligned academics or by more direct means.” Or take the health care system, which is a nightmarish hellscape because the fee-for-service model creates “an almost unfathomable variety of conflicts of interest, arising from a vast array of deeply flawed incentives.” Again and again, they point to experts who claimed to be producing nuanced, sober behavioral science but who in fact were snarfling in the trough of corporate money and have then told a story (including to themselves and one another) about individual responsibility. In many cases, they probably didn’t even realize it. “The self-serving bias,” Chater and Loewenstein write, “kicks in when there are multiple interpretations of what is ‘fair.’ In such situations, people tend to choose whatever interpretation best suits their own interests.”

Worse still, it appears that the nudges didn’t even work. Chater and Loewenstein cite one meta-study of 241 nudge interventions that found a 1.4 percent change in the targeted behaviors; another survey of eighty-three randomized control trials on climate change nudges found at best a tiny effect during the course of the experiment and nothing afterward.

Further, the global nudge mania in the 2010s was not merely a frustrating mistake. The focus on the nudges crowded out other possibilities, both in research and in policy. Instead of trying to turn the *Titanic*, the nudgers were rearranging the order of the cafeteria line. More distressingly, they justified inaction on urgent, potentially catastrophic problems. “The green-energy nudge,” for example, “gives people the false hope that the problem of climate change can be addressed without imposing costlier, but immeasurably more effective, policies.”

Another perverse finding is that markets can offset any nudge-friendly individual action. Perhaps you choose not to fly, because of the environmental cost of air travel. Your contribution to reducing demand might then lower the cost of plane tickets, causing other people to fly more, thereby negating your individual decision. The point about the illusion of market choices can go even further. I can choose from a bewildering parade of health care plans, but I cannot choose universal health care that is free at the point of service. I can choose among different books to read, but it is difficult to buy one without enriching a tech oligarch. We can choose within market structures but not nonmarket forms of social relations. Most of us are not consulted in the decisions that structure our lives, leaving us with “choices” that are less and less meaningful.

Chater and Loewenstein are not subtle about whom and what to blame. They point to the neoliberal ideology of Ronald Reagan and Margaret Thatcher in the 1980s, which constituted “a radical reshaping of the rules of society that systematically favored powerful businesses as well as the wealthy.” A chapter on inequality traces the rise of our current oligarchs and the ways their concentrated power has distorted incentives everywhere. In turn, people who really should know better but who are in some way complicit with big business or dependent on the spigot of oligarch money have collectively decided to blame the victims. Thus:

> When a situation seems *unfair*, we first try to take actions to restore fairness. But if and when these actions fail, or when there is simply no action we can take, we try to reduce the painful sense of unfairness. And an effective way to do this is to find a way to rationalize that the victim of the injustice *deserves* the apparent injustice.

They don’t follow the thought further, but it has a clear political implication. Most people don’t much like being blamed by clever experts who aren’t actually helping. The present widespread distrust and outright loathing of expertise in health, economics, education, and academia might well be partly attributable to this habit. Or it could be the other way around: deservedness is such a core principle of American public discourse that sincere policy wonks can’t perceive that they are reproducing the problems they are trying to solve. The i-frame is so deeply ingrained that it shapes even efforts at s-frame thinking. Either way, the dispensation of class power continues unabated.

Cass Sunstein’s latest book, *The Price of Happiness*, is a defense of “willingness to pay” (which he abbreviates WTP), a concept that is at the heart of determining which policy or regulation is “best” and thus which should be preferred from a behavioral science perspective. Willingness to pay means assigning prices to things like sunsets, the last blue whale, or the lives of your children in order to assess tradeoffs and relative preferences. How much more would you want to be paid to do a dangerous job? How much would you be willing to pay your neighbor to be quiet? How much would your neighbor be willing to pay you to keep ventilating their nocturnal music? One central price is the value of a single statistical life, which at the time of this writing is $14.1 million, although the Trump administration is trying very hard to drive it to zero.[\*](#fn-*)

This is far from the first work on the subject: Richard Thaler’s first academic paper, published in 1976, was titled “The Value of Saving a Life.” This is not even Cass Sunstein’s first book on it: at the very least, his *Risk and Reason* (2002), *Valuing Life* (2014), and *The Cost-Benefit Revolution* (2018) each build a similar case. The concept of willingness to pay was first developed by Alfred Marshall in 1890, and most of the theoretical elaborations and limitations were figured out between the “externalities” of A.C. Pigou in the 1910s and the libertarian Ronald Coase’s answer in 1960 that assigning property rights to everything would solve social problems. A 1989 law review article announced “Willingness to Pay Comes of Age.” By 2026 it must be approaching retirement. Why this book, and why now?

*The Price of Happiness* is intended to be a discussion of the objections to the willingness to pay model and a vindication of its usefulness. People might misprice things because of the range of behavioral biases familiar to behavioral economists: they are unreasonably optimistic, they have self-control problems, they have short time horizons and limited attention, or they dislike losses more than they like gains. They may not have enough information. “An uninformed choice is not exactly autonomous,” Sunstein writes. “A choice produced by present bias or limited attention is not fully autonomous.” These conditions raise problems for the applicability and accuracy of making regulatory, legal, or economic decisions based on what people say they are willing to pay for something, and that in turn leaves extensive precincts of human life where this approach is inappropriate. “If a low WTP shows a failure to give adequate attention to the future,” he writes, by way of example, “then there is reason not to use WTP.”

Sunstein discusses many other limitations. Some things, like true love or the right to be racist, cannot or should not be bought and sold in markets. Some pricing choices affect other people who are not consulted, which he calls “a real problem…. A great deal of work remains to be done on this topic.” He acknowledges that some things, like justice and deliberative democracy, are probably best approached by treating people as citizens, not consumers. He is aware that rich people have more money than poor people, but also that money has a diminishing marginal utility such that the same amount means less to a rich person than to a poor person. “Here, then, is a problem for WTP. A poor person might be willing to pay much less than [Elon] Musk for some good, even though Musk would benefit much less than the poor person from that good.” And so on.

But Sunstein does have a political point to advance. As he puts it:

> *No one, rich or poor, should be forced to pay more than she is willing to pay for goods, including the reduction of risks*. In fact this idea embodies a norm of equality (and the right one). If poor people are unwilling to pay much for the reduction of serious risks, and if government wants to help, the appropriate response is not a compelled purchase but a subsidy, perhaps in the form of cash payments.

He concludes that despite its limitations, willingness to pay is “usually a pretty good measure of the likely welfare effects of obtaining goods” and “is a way of respecting people’s autonomy.” So, in the end, “choice, freedom, and WTP are linked.”

The cumulative effect is like watching Sunstein tear down his house in order to build a fence. The objections are so numerous and varied, the acceptable scope for the willingness to pay model becomes so circumscribed, and Sunstein spends so little time solving each problem that the book is almost self-refuting. Rather than persuasive rebuttals or preponderant evidence, his case depends on his characteristic argumentative style.

In *The Price of Happiness* and elsewhere, Sunstein pursues his argument along three lines. The first is practical: nudges (or willingness to pay, or cost-benefit analysis) are effective, as shown by behavioral science, and a good tool for measurement or other technical tasks. The second is institutional: nudges (et al.) are the best way to accomplish what the legal system is trying to do. And the third is philosophical: nudges are tangible expressions of utilitarian philosophy, which Sunstein takes as the best moral system. He often quotes the work of John Stuart Mill, but the more humane parts of Mills’s oeuvre (of which there are many, to be sure) and not the parts advocating “benevolent despotism” in the colonies or explaining that liberty is not for “those backward states of society in which the race itself may be considered as in its nonage.”

Sunstein’s characteristic procedure is to slide between these three levels of argument as needed. The objection that nudges don’t work in practice is met by an appeal to utilitarian philosophy; objections to utilitarianism itself are answered by saying that nudges are the best we can do with the legal system we have. Objections to the legal system are diverted to questions about efficacy or morality.

When I read Sunstein, I am seldom persuaded by him, but it’s never because I can think of something he hasn’t. He knows the criticisms of utilitarianism perfectly well, he is aware of the inequities of the American legal system, and he admits there are many arenas where his approaches don’t actually work, but he can’t or won’t put all three of those problems together. Similarly, Sunstein is well aware of the many critiques of nudges. He responded to them at length in *Why Nudge?* (2014) and *The Ethics of Influence* (2016). The 2021 reissue of *Nudge* features a chapter of responses to critics. He doesn’t find them persuasive, but his reasons are not always clear. Sometimes he seems to believe there are no alternatives, but neither does he look for any. The certainty and volume of his output is impressive, but it’s easy to wonder about the marginal utility of one more of these books.

*It’s On You* is in some ways a powerful and coruscating book; in other ways, it is very frustrating. The evidentiary chapters are clear and lively. They also explain critiques of structural inequalities that have been made by a legion of sociologists, historians, journalists, activists, and many others for decades. Chater and Loewenstein say their eyes were opened by reading Naomi Oreskes and Erik Conway’s *The Big Myth* (2023), about the twentieth-century corporate propaganda campaign for free market fundamentalism that involved everything from Reagan’s acting career to the *Little House on the Prairie* books. *The Big Myth* is an effective text, but there is something dispiriting about two “libertarian paternalists” discovering in the year 2023 why the political left exists.

This delayed revelation itself seems symptomatic. As the historian Daniel Rodgers showed in *Age of Fracture* (2011), since about the 1970s American intellectual, civic, and political life has been pulverized by the market, transforming communities into collections of atomized, alienated individuals. That condition of market-dependent isolation was mistaken for human nature, and the attendant collapse of social and associational life made it difficult for many people to conceive of collective action as an academic subject or a political project. No wonder behavioral economists and policymakers couldn’t see systems: they inhabited a world of consuming individuals, not a world of social meaning and communal belonging; they took that condition as a given, not as the outcome of politics.

It is not necessary to agree with all of Chater and Loewenstein’s story to be convinced that the behavioralists committed a profound category error. As Thaler explains in *Misbehaving*, the point of behavioral economics was to improve the accuracy and realism of economic modeling. That is an estimable goal, but it is not a political philosophy or a principle of governance, let alone a method for delivering happy lives. Thaler goes on to claim that “designing good public policies has a lot in common with designing any consumer product,” and therein lies the problem. Looking at public policy through goggles designed to explain consumer choice has turned out to be perfectly consistent with a free-market reductionist view of the world, so instead of pushing back against Chicago-school libertarianism, the behavioralists strengthened it and inoculated it against criticism.

They found an ideological home in a Democratic Party that had developed a fastidious distaste for politics itself, mistaking what is actually a ruthless zero-sum power struggle for a well-meaning bipartisan effort to solve technical problems while leaving the shape of inequality undisturbed. They substituted an antipolitics for the creation of a positive vision of society at exactly the moment when something new was necessary, a move that constituted a unilateral disarmament in the face of an intransigent radical right wing. A pivotal decade or more was lost in which no meaningful action was taken against climate change or inequality, but a lot of calorie counts got displayed.

That category slippage is evident in the final chapters of *It’s On You*. Having presented the insidious distortions of corporate power across a range of pressing social issues, Chater and Loewenstein end the book by offering suggestions not for how to rebalance the scales but for how to improve the methods of behavioral science. They wave toward the need for “exposing and breaking up these networks of influence” and say that solutions “will mean taking money out of politics across the board,” but most of their attention is turned to things like broadening graduate student training and better publication standards in academic journals. The unescapable implication is that their tools have left them unable to address the problems they’ve identified. They can see the s-frame now, but they don’t know what to do with it.

From the vantage point of 2026, the behavioralist approach is utterly incommensurable with the times. After DOGE, after the overturning of the Chevron doctrine and the Voting Rights Act, as a masked right-wing paramilitary shoots Americans in the streets and Donald Trump bombs other countries with impunity, the prospect that the government might be doing more regulation than poor people want does not seem especially urgent. While the Democrats were busy nudging, the Republicans took over the Supreme Court, gerrymandered the legislature, and gutted the administrative state. A mass politics to democratize the United States is not going to be based on nudges.

The empty political content of behavioral science, like that of these books, presupposes that most serious problems have already been solved or have only one consensus solution. In *Nudge*, *The Price of Happiness*, and elsewhere, Sunstein and his coauthors often write in the plural, about what “we” want: if “we” want to monetize things, or if “we” care about inequality, or if “we” want to anticipate people’s errors, then behavioral economics is the right method. But that presumes a political agent, someone who knows what people want and wants them to get it.

The tools can be turned to other uses. After all, strategically rearranging the presentation of products is well known to advertisers, marketers, and shopkeepers. Deciding to arrange the cafeteria line either to help diners lose weight or to help the cafeteria sell more food is a political choice that is elided in the behavioralist accounts. That elision, in turn, means the behavioralists seem not to have applied their ideas to themselves. Perhaps wanting to monetize everything is itself a form of miswanting, and the social cost of living in a world where everything has a price is higher than they realized. If the nudgers were nudged, then what matters is not the choice architecture presented to our human capital, but the decisions of actual capital.
