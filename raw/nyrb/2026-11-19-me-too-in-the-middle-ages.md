# 正文

Jehanne de Relanges was making a criminal allegation of rape. This is how she first told her story to the municipal investigator, Jaques Borestal, who took on the case. She was sitting by the fire in her landlady’s home in Dijon around six in the evening on the Sunday following the Feast of the Innocents in January 1473. She was nearing the end of her pregnancy. Two men burst into the house; Jehanne didn’t recognize them. They told her she had to come with them. She refused. The men threatened to take Jehanne by force, and said if she came willingly she would only be raped by the two of them. Still she refused. One of the men left and came back with four more men, mercenaries, with weapons. Jehanne remembered a man with a big beard and a javelin, a man in a colored tunic with a bow and arrow, and a boy with a dagger. The one with the big beard told Jehanne that she was only pretending to be pregnant.

Jehanne pled with the men, Jaques recorded: “She did not know when she might go into labor and deliver her baby, that she was a good respectable woman.”[1](#fn-1) The men replied “that she was coming, pregnant or not pregnant, and that if she lost her baby, it did not matter since she was pregnant from a priest.” They took her outside in the snow. They held her down, hit her across the face, hit her arms with a stick, threw her on her belly, and attempted to rape her in turn. She prayed to Saint Nicolas. Jehanne told Jaques that they couldn’t rape her because “her belly and back convulsed so much.” One of the men’s servants wanted at least to make off with her clothes and abandon her in a ditch, but instead the group took her back to her landlady’s home. One left her some money and asked to be the baby’s godfather. Jehanne told Jaques that she didn’t remember anything more. She wanted justice to be done, but she did not want the men executed.

A few days after giving her testimony, Jehanne gave birth to a baby girl. She called Jaques back and told him that she “had been thinking.” She was unwell, holding her newborn in bed. There was more that she hadn’t said. Jehanne wanted Jaques to know that the men had “worked themselves so much that four of them all spilled their seed against and at the door of her womb without ever getting inside.” She had been too ashamed to reveal that detail before. One threw snow in her face and ejaculated on her twice. And when another, “the big one,” had offered to be the godfather of her unborn child and Jehanne didn’t answer him, he hit her across the jaw.

When a woman said that she was raped in late medieval Europe, some of what followed depended on what sort of woman she was generally thought to be. Who was Jehanne? A few facts were easily established: she was from Lorraine, a stranger to Dijon, and she was there without a husband. But the rest of Jehanne’s story was murkier. First Jaques wrote in his register that Jehanne was a widow. Then he scratched that out: she was married, but her husband had left her in Dijon. Jehanne had six children with her husband, she told Jaques, including the new baby. But who Jehanne said she was mattered less than who her neighbors perceived her to be. Jaques put it bluntly: he needed to find out, during his investigation, whether Jehanne was “a good married preudefemme” or “a loose woman, common and secret.”

So Jaques began to ask around: her neighbors, her landlady, the midwife, the wet nurse, the innkeeper, the shoemaker’s wife. A portrait of a woman with a complicated past emerged. “Women on the street” knew Jehanne, said one man, implying at best that she hung around with sex workers, at worst that she was one herself. But then that man was the father of one of the attackers. Another, a tanner who lived across the street, said that Jehanne’s landlady was known for taking in “femmes passans secretes”—transient women up to who knew what. Neighbors reported gossip: the baby’s father was a priest (and the wet nurse said Jehanne had indeed told her just that). One said he heard from “some women,” who had in turn heard from a shoemaker’s wife, that this was not Jehanne’s first baby with a cleric. Others said she had been cagey about the father’s identity. At first Jehanne’s husband was thought to be alive, then just old, and finally dead. Women also told Jaques that Jehanne spoke and dressed “in the Lorraine manner”—“her feet in white hose, wearing a fur-lined dress.” She had “beautiful hair.” All in all, everyone agreed that she was “a really beautiful woman, blonde and curvy, with a big beautiful body.”

While Jaques was interrogating her neighbors and acquaintances, Jehanne remained in bed with her newborn. Jehanne’s midwife told Jaques that she “saw coming from the mother’s body strange things that she had never before seen come from a woman before the baby, that is to say a great abundance of blood,” and that she had discerned evidence of injuries from the assault. Recovering from that and from the birth itself in her landlady’s home, Jehanne was threatened by a friend of the men she had accused—he would hang her from a tree and burn her, he said—and she lost her milk out of fear. Her rapists were in prison, facing a possible death penalty, but prisons weren’t especially secure, and any of the mercenaries roaming Dijon might take up his companions’ cause. So Jehanne fled, moving in with another local widow. But there were too many people “going and coming,” and she “was always afraid,” so she ran again, in disguise. Jehanne sheltered in a hospital where she planned to serve the poor, and that was where Jaques finally found her, nearly two months later, to hear her final testimony. At some point during those two months, her baby had died at the home of her wet nurse.

Jehanne’s final testimony is harrowing. She described living in constant fear of reprisal. She clarified the mysterious whereabouts of her husband: in Lorraine he had gotten “angry at her and left her, and went to fight in the war” between the duke of Burgundy and the French king. Her older children were dead. Jehanne had made her way to Burgundy and once there found “a man of the church”—she wouldn’t name him—who gave her a living, and with whom she had two children. She had both babies baptized, “as a good woman and mother should do, without doing any harm to their bodies”: the specter of infanticide was never far when a baby was born to an unmarried woman. Jaques asked again about the rape. She said she was still unwell, and described a burning all over her body that she connected to her assault. She told him that she was “so ashamed and so distressed” to “have to repeat” the story, which many had asked about, and yet, Jaques noted, “she does not want to replace or change [the accusation], because it happened.” She was unhappy that the men were in prison; she maintained that she did not want them to face the death penalty. But she had testified “because it was the truth.”

In *Justice for Jehanne*, the medieval historian Sara McDougall presents a forensic study of the case. She argues that while no prosecutor in late medieval Dijon would have seriously doubted a woman’s rape accusation, rape was difficult to prove. Without a witness who overheard a scream, or an obvious wound from fighting back, it was the man’s word against hers. Even if witnesses or physical evidence could be produced, it was unlikely that a rapist would be prosecuted, especially if the victim was one of those loose or common or secret women. Most women who were raped in medieval Dijon refused to make a criminal complaint. No one—not Jehanne, not Jaques, not any of the witnesses or lawyers or clerics brought into the orbit of the case—would have believed they were living in a society that systematically prosecuted rapists. The question becomes, then, why women made criminal complaints of rape at all.

Why did Jehanne? She was heavily pregnant, and then nursing a newborn. She was sick and suffering from her injuries. She was a migrant, with no family connections in Dijon and no husband. She was subject to threats of reprisal, threats she took seriously enough to flee several times. Dijon authorities did not offer protection against such retaliation. And her own sexual past was not unblemished: she had been the concubine of a priest and had given birth to two children out of wedlock, a fact that she must have guessed would eventually come to light if she proceeded with the investigation. Any one of those circumstances might have been enough to dissuade a rape victim from pursuing prosecution.

But she wanted justice, she told Jaques. Did she get it? Four of Jehanne’s attackers were imprisoned for nearly two months while Jaques investigated her complaint. One of them was tortured in order to extract a confession. A fifth man was banished from Dijon. The other men’s sentencing became a complicated matter of jurisdictional wrangling, but by early March all four had escaped from prison. They fled to a nearby lord’s house to claim refuge, were eventually dragged back to prison, but were then given the ultimate medieval get-out-of-jail-free card: some priests wrote letters claiming that the rapists were in fact clerics, and therefore could not be prosecuted by Dijon authorities. The priests “promise[d] to do good justice as pertains to the case,” and we do not hear of Jehanne’s rapists again.

In her study of Jehanne’s case, McDougall argues that rape was differently conceived in the medieval past because sex was differently conceived. There was legalized sex work, but except within marriage and for the purposes of procreation, sex was a sin and often a crime. Sex between two unmarried people was fornication; sex between people who were married but not to each other was adultery. Clergy were meant to be celibate. When so much sex was immoral and even illegal, what was rape?

Consent mattered. In the Middle Ages rape was sometimes conflated with the separate crime of abduction, the taking of a woman by force, which might precede rape; the punishment for abduction was the death penalty. For the rapist (almost always understood to be male), a woman’s lack of consent could worsen the severity of the crime, but he had most likely broken the law whether she was consenting or not. For the victim, proving nonconsent meant that she would not be prosecuted for fornication or adultery. McDougall argues that medieval women had a degree of legal recourse we might find surprising, and that judicial procedure was more accepting of imperfect victims and contradictory narratives than it is today.

And yet “rape was a fact of medieval life,” McDougall writes. Late medieval society held that humankind was fundamentally weak and inclined toward sin; men were passionate and aggressive by nature. Cities like Dijon were unsafe. Prisons could be escaped. Mercenaries roamed. In Jehanne’s case, the absence of her consent was indisputable. She was violently assaulted. Her late-term pregnancy made such violence all the more abhorrent. Aware of this, her rapists, having heard a rumor that Jehanne had been a priest’s mistress, claimed that her sexual past vitiated the moral fact of her pregnancy, telling her that the life of her fetus was worthless.

Rape would seem to be a fact of life now too. In the US about one in five women will experience attempted or completed rape in their lifetimes. Rape culture permeates everywhere. That toxic cluster of beliefs about gender, power, sex, and violence turns out to be persistent across the centuries. When Jehanne told the prosecutor that she had done nothing “to justify their doing such abuses and dishonor to her,” I hear that she knew a woman’s behavior and sexual past might make her seem more or less rapeable. Rape victims today understand this as well. There is Jaques’s division of women into the good (only the pure can be violated) and the bad (not violable or less deserving of justice). And there is this idea, almost incredible in its durability, that masculine desire is fundamentally uncontrollable.

Low reporting rates, invasive standards of proof, low prosecution rates: all of it sounds familiar. In the UK, where I live, 85,000 women are raped a year. Eight in ten rape victims do not report their assaults to the police. When asked why, a substantial minority said that they did not think the police would believe them or could help even if they did. Fewer than three in one hundred rapes recorded by the police result in an alleged rapist being criminally charged. Even fewer of these cases end in a conviction. Given such figures, feminist organizers in the UK have argued that rape has effectively become decriminalized.

As the medieval historian Judith Bennett wrote, the patriarchy has a long history. It has not always looked the same, but its continuities are as important to consider as its transformations. Bennett has argued that gender historians sometimes emphasize change over time—women’s power and progress and agency—instead of the more depressing endurance of patriarchal control.[2](#fn-2) McDougall wants us to notice some of the less expected discontinuities. In medieval Europe, men were considered culpable for rape, for example. Women were believed, even if real justice was often impossible. These differences indeed merit our attention. That Jehanne—single, vulnerable, compromised—could make a criminal complaint of rape, be taken seriously by a prosecutor, and receive help from her neighbors in the form of food, shelter, and disguises: all suggest that attitudes toward rape victims in the Middle Ages were not so medieval after all.

And yet. Of the seven men accused of raping a female student at a Cornell fraternity in 2024, two were expelled, two suspended, and one (a graduate) barred from campus. Four were made to write repentant essays; none were criminally charged. (Under a great deal of pressure, prosecutors have since reopened the case.) This is only the most recent sign of a striking continuity in the long history of rape. The majority of women have not made, do not make, criminal allegations of rape. Those who do rarely achieve an outcome that could be considered just. That’s true of Jehanne, and so far it’s true of Jane Doe. The centuries-long persistence of so many beliefs that are central to rape culture is worth trying to explain. Feminist thinkers have long argued that depriving women of bodily sovereignty is one way in which the patriarchy defines itself and continues to function. That much has not changed.

McDougall proceeds point by point through Jehanne’s case, hewing closely to Jaques’s investigation. We learn what happened in the same fragmentary way that Jaques himself did, as witnesses contradict one another and as Jehanne’s mysterious past is gradually revealed. There are false starts, red herrings, plot twists. McDougall includes long extracts from Jaques’s writings, in “painstakingly literal” translations in order “to communicate as directly as possible what could actually be Jehanne’s own words.” She writes that Jaques would have read back to Jehanne her own testimony, to make sure he’d gotten it right, and that therefore “it is hard to imagine many inventions on Jaques’s part.” There are long digressions into the taxation histories and minor legal troubles of various witnesses and bit players. We are asked to assume a “hypervigilant mode of reading and interpretation,” to “remain vigilantly suspicious” toward the sources. She compares her methods to those of “a good police procedural.”

Presenting the story in this way is an interpretive choice. It lends a certain reality effect: that if only the historian can amass enough sources (and McDougall has), if only these sources can be presented as transparently as possible (and they are), then we might see…what? I’m still not sure. McDougall knows we can’t find out what “really” happened. We come away with little sense of how these sources were shaped by the imbalances of power between prosecutor and victim. McDougall writes in the first few pages that what follows is “Jehanne’s story, the story of her quest for survival.” But all we can ever have is her story as pieced together, copied down, investigated, and assembled by a prosecutor.

McDougall knows all this. At odds with her procedural social history is a cultural historian’s insistence on the fictiveness of the archives. She writes that Jehanne was “a storyteller about herself” and that she probably did “some serious self-fashioning.” She tells us that Jehanne’s accounts were “crafted,” that they were a “performance” for Jaques. At least since Natalie Zemon Davis’s *Fiction in the Archives* (1987), this idea has been axiomatic to cultural historians—that, as Davis writes, “the artifice of fiction did not necessarily lend falsity to an account; it might well bring verisimilitude or a moral truth.” Historical protagonists were not necessarily trying to represent factual truth but a social truth when they spoke to prosecutors and inquisitors and judges. When witness after witness described Jehanne as a “beautiful blonde” or commented on her accent, for example, they were not just talking about her hair color or her foreignness. They were saying that she was different, and that she drew their attention. When Jaques wrote “widow” to describe Jehanne, crossed it out, and wrote above it “married,” that ambiguity pointed toward the moral truth that Jehanne was alone. She was alone, and pregnant, and so an explanation had to be found.

But the idea of Jehanne as a storyteller means more to McDougall, I think. Jehanne “reserved the right to tell people what suited her about herself.” McDougall wants us to understand this as a reclamation and a demonstration of resilience, evidence of her “ability to endure.” Storytelling becomes redemptive, a substitute form of justice in a system unlikely to mete out anything like actual justice. McDougall argues that “survivors now should know, too, that telling stories then and now had the power to heal, and to rebuild.”

It is remarkable that Jehanne made a criminal complaint of rape and that she saw it through—an unusual choice. Her circumstances ought to have been enough to dissuade her from seeking justice. But we can heed the singularity of Jehanne’s decision and what it might say about her bravery or her rage without searching for a silver lining. Storytelling is not always healing, and healing is not the same as justice. Some hard things deserve the kind of attention that is undiluted by consolation. For Jehanne, what happened was true. That had to be enough.
