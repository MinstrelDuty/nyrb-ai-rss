# 正文

Arturo (Arthur) Schomburg was born in San Juan, Puerto Rico, in 1874. His mother was a black laundress from the Virgin Islands and his father a mestizo merchant of German descent long resident on the island. He came to New York in 1891 and joined the Cuban and Puerto Rican revolutionary movement, but factional disputes at the close of the Spanish-American War led him to turn his attention to the cause of black American freedom instead.

Schomburg was not just a “race man,” as activists used to be called, he was also an organization man. He belonged to a number of groups of pioneering black intellectuals. In 1911 he cofounded the Negro Society for Historical Research. In 1920 he was elected president of the American Negro Academy, of which W.E.B. Du Bois had been one of the original members when it was founded in Washington, D.C., in 1897. Alfred A. Moss Jr. in *The American Negro Academy: Voice of the Talented Tenth* (1981) observes that although Schomburg had written articles and had a reputation as a book collector, he did not have a college degree, and his election over more distinguished members was a sign to some of the Academy’s decline. Washington as the center of black intellectual life had been superseded by New York, and according to Moss, Schomburg was unable to attend to the Academy’s business in person. He was its last president when it folded in 1928.

The Harlem branch of the New York Public Library opened in 1905 on the corner of 135th Street and Lenox Avenue. By 1920, when the area was becoming overwhelmingly black, Ernestine Rose, the head librarian, and Catherine Latimer, the first black librarian there, believed that the branch needed a special reference collection to preserve the historical record of black people. Schomburg, a longtime patron of the library, was chairman of the committee that developed the plan for the Department of Negro Literature and History, which was established in 1925. The following year the Board of Trustees of the New York Public Library received a grant from the Carnegie Corporation to purchase his collection of 3,338 bound volumes. He was not paid what it was worth, Elinor Des Verney Sinnette tells us in *Arthur Alfonso Schomburg: Black Bibliophile and Collector* (1989), but he didn’t care too much. He knew what he had done. He served as curator of the collection from 1932 until his death in 1938, and the library was renamed the Schomburg Center for Research in Black Culture in 1972.

Because of the eventual fame of the collection, many people assumed that Schomburg was a philanthropist, but he’d amassed his library on a modest income. He searched in the warehouses and secondhand bookstores of New York at a time when what he was looking for was not of much value to most people. As a Caribbean-born Spanish speaker, he urged black Americans to look into black history before slavery and black culture in other countries, not just in the United States. He also took an interest in black artists, and in the early twentieth century he began making a collection of prints and etchings.

The Schomburg Center has been celebrating what its director, Joy L. Bivins, announced was a double centennial: that of the founding of the Department of Negro Literature and History as well as that of the Schomburg purchase. It has been marked by a year of events, starting last June with the launch of *Joy Goddess: A’Lelia Walker and the Harlem Renaissance*,a biography of the Jazz Age hostess and patron of the Dark Tower cultural salon by her great-granddaughter A’Lelia Bundles. Deborah Willis, an artist and former curator of photographs and prints at the Schomburg, interviewed Bundles on the Schomburg stage, and the line afterward for book signings and advice from Bundles on researching family stories extended out the lobby doors.

There have been evenings of music and film as well. The Schomburg Collection holds some five thousand motion pictures and videotapes. In May the series “Black on Screen: Inner Lives, Outer Frames” included the short film *Cleopatra at the Mall* (2024) by Madeleine Hunt-Ehrlich. It commemorates the rediscovery of *The Death of Cleopatra*,a large marble sculpture of the enthroned queen in death by the African American/Ojibwe artist Edmonia Lewis, which was exhibited in Philadelphia in 1876 and Chicago in 1878 and then forgotten until it was tracked down in storage at a Chicago mall by the art historian Marilyn Richardson in 1989.

The series also screened *Losing Ground* by Kathleen Collins, followed by a panel headed by Melissa Lyde, the founder of Alfreda’s Cinema in Brooklyn. The film follows a philosophy professor and her artist husband through a summer during which her ambitions and his wildness threaten the stability of their marriage. Made in 1982, *Losing Ground* had no commercial release. It is remembered not only as one of the first features directed by a black American woman but also as depicting a character seldom seen in American cinema: the intellectual black woman. Collins died at forty-six in 1988. A collection of her short stories, *Whatever Happened to Interracial Love*, was published posthumously in 2016 with a foreword by the poet and scholar Elizabeth Alexander.

The centennial celebrations continue with the exhibition “To Uncover and Reveal to the World: Arturo Schomburg’s Library.” It beautifully displays fifty-nine books and pamphlets from what the center’s former director Kevin Young called Schomburg’s “seed library”—the books he had collected and then donated to the Department of Negro Literature and History—as well as artworks and postcards related to his collecting activities. The exhibition expands our understanding of his contribution to the study of people of African descent. Handwritten minutes from Las Dos Antillas, the political club that the journalist Rafael Serra cofounded in 1892 to work for Cuban and Puerto Rican independence, remind us of Schomburg’s early activism in New York, when he served as the club’s secretary. Vanessa K. Valdés in *Diasporic Blackness: The Life and Times of Arturo Alfonso Schomburg* (2017) tells us that his outlook was always international, which sometimes put him at odds with those who preferred to concentrate on the black struggle in the US.

Some 160 languages are represented in the center’s collection. *Poetas de Color* (1887), for instance, a volume about Afro-Cuban poets edited by Francisco Calcagno, reflects Schomburg’s understanding of writing by black people as the literature of a diaspora. In addition to texts critical of Spanish colonialism, such as *The Color Question in the Two Americas* (1922) by Bernardo Ruiz Suarez,the exhibition features collections of Haitian records from 1741 to 1804 and works of Haitian history. Schomburg regarded Baron de Vastey, King Henri Christophe’s secretary, as an important thinker on questions of colonialism and race. Schomburg’s seed library includes Vastey’s *Le Cri de la patrie* (1815), a pamphlet whose title he translated as *Cry of the Fatherland in the Interest of All Haytians*. Among the other volumes on display concerned with the diaspora are *From West Africa to Palestine* (1873) by Edward Wilmot Blyden, often called the father of Pan-Africanism, and *The African Abroad: or, His Evolution in Western Civilization* (1913) by William H. Ferris, a Congregational pastor whose work introduced many black students to the ideas of Marcus Garvey.

David Bryant Fulton, a member of the Negro Society for Historical Research, published in 1900, under the pseudonym Jack Thorne, *Hanover: or, The Persecution of the Lowly*,his fictional account of the Wilmington, North Carolina, massacre in 1898, when white supremacists overthrew the Reconstruction municipal government, burned the black press, killed more than two dozen black people, and drove hundreds more from the city. Schomburg’s generously inscribed copy of *Hanover* is on display in the exhibition. The massacre is also the subject of Charles Chesnutt’s novel *The Marrow of Tradition* (1901), which is diplomatic about how complicit white business leaders and politicians were in the massacre compared to Fulton’s assertion that they financially underwrote the mobs. Chesnutt’s son-in-law, Edward Christopher Williams, the librarian of Howard University and the author of a witty epistolary novel about the black upper-middle class, *When Washington Was in Vogue*,which remained unpublished until 2004, had been elected a member of the American Negro Academy in 1911 during a period of optimism about its appeal to a new generation and the number of occasional papers it would publish. (Chesnutt, on the other hand, declined to become a member when the Academy was founded.)

The activists, educators, churchmen, editors, librarians, and writers involved in such institutions also formed the circle of bibliophiles with whom Schomburg could share his passion: Reverend Charles D. Martin, the founder of the Fourth Moravian Church in Harlem; William C. Bolivar, a collector in Philadelphia with a noteworthy library of books, pamphlets, tracts, manuscripts, and letters; John W. Cromwell, a lawyer, teacher, and author of *The Negro in American History* (1914); John Edward Bruce, a newspaper editor and columnist; George Washington Forbes, the first black librarian at the Boston Public Library; and George Young, whose Book Exchange in Harlem and catalog were important resources. Bruce was a cofounder with Schomburg of the Negro Society for Historical Research in 1911 and the Theban Literary Circle in 1921. They and their corresponding members on three continents collected works of black history in different languages. In Schomburg’s best-known essay, “The Negro Digs Up His Past,” published in Alain Locke’s *The New Negro* (1925), he was adamant that there was a “definite desire and determination to have a history, well documented, widely known at least within race circles, and administered as a stimulating and inspiring tradition for coming generations.”

John Edward Bruce, born into slavery, helped Schomburg rescue and have bound the handwritten manuscript of *The Greatness of Christ and Other Sermons* by Alexander Crummell (one of 240 manuscripts of historical importance in the Schomburg’s Manuscripts, Archives, and Rare Books Division). Crummell, the son of free black abolitionists in New York, was the first black graduate of Cambridge University, the founder of St. Luke’s Episcopal Church in Washington, D.C., and a cofounder of the American Negro Academy, and he was remembered by Du Bois in *The Souls of Black Folk* as one of the inspirations of his youth. Bruce, who died in 1924, was a freemason like Schomburg, a stalwart supporter of Garvey, and the great-uncle of the Harlem Renaissance gay bohemian artist and writer Richard Bruce Nugent.

The exhibition’s informative companion volume of essays, *Black Studies on 135th Street: The Founding and Future of the Schomburg Collection*, edited by Barrye Brown, Laura E. Helton, and Vanessa K. Valdés,says that the number of books in Schomburg’s library was known, but no detailed inventory survived. Curators painstakingly searched books for clues such as his bookplates, inscriptions, and address stamps. Nearly a third of the book is a fascinating bibliography, edited by Helton and compiled with the help of Schomburg Center staff, of Schomburg’s seed library.

Some of the collection’s holdings were known to have been his because they meant so much to him. For instance, the first comprehensive survey of writings by people of African descent was published in France in 1808: the Abbé Henri Grégoire’s *De la littérature des Nègres: ou, Recherches sur leurs facultés intellectuelles, leurs qualités morales et leur littérature; suivies de notices sur la vie et les ouvrages des nègres qui se sont distingués dans les sciences, les lettres et les arts*. Grégoire belonged to the school of Enlightenment writers who campaigned to refute the notion that black people were inferior to white people. He rushed to have the book translated into English because the Napoleonic state was in turmoil and he expected the French edition to be suppressed at any moment. It was published in Brooklyn in 1810 under the title *An Enquiry Concerning the Intellectual and Moral Faculties, and Literature of Negroes; Followed* *with an account of the Life and Works of Fifteen Negroes and Mulattoes, Distinguished in Science, Literature and the Arts*. Schomburg later had other sources of writings by black people, such as Henri Ternaux-Compans’s *Catalogue des livres et manuscrits de la bibliothèque de feu M. Raetzal* (1836), but Grégoire’s book was his first guide and inspiration.

Among the other books on display in the exhibition is a 1550 translation of *De partibus divinae legis libri duo doctissimi*, *omnibus theologiae studiosis apprime vtile*s…,the exploration of divine law by thesixth-century North African bishop Junillus Africanus; a 1632 edition of Leo Africanus’s *Description of Africa*; and *The* *Travels of the Jesuits in Ethiopia*, a translation of the writings of Manuel da Almeida, a seventeenth-century Portuguese Jesuit ambassador to Ethiopia, published anonymously in 1710, half a century after Almeida’s death. Jacobus Elisa Joannes Capitein*,* born in 1717 in what is now Ghana, was enslaved at an early age, but in keeping with certain social experiments of the Enlightenment, he studied as a free man at the University of Leiden. *Political Research Paper on Slavery* is a translation of his 1742 dissertation, *Staatkundig-godgeleerd onderzoekschrift over de slaverny*…, for his degree in theology. Capitein argued that support of slavery favored the propagation of the Gospel and he returned to Ghana in 1742 as a missionary, where he died.

Schomburg was so taken with Phillis Wheatley and her *Poems on Various Occasions, Religious and Moral* (1773),the first book by a black person to be published in English, that he also began collecting books she had owned, such as *The Works in Verse and Prose of William Shenstone* (1773). In her essay in *Black Studies on 135th Street*,Tara A. Bynum tells us that Schomburg’s reverence for Wheatley led him to purchase every edition of her poems and letters that he could find. *The Interesting Narrative of the Life of Olaudah Equiano, or: Gustavus Vassa, the African, Written by Himself* was published in London in 1789. Equiano’s autobiography was celebrated by Grégoire as the first slave narrative written without editorial direction or intervention from a white person. He compares Equiano’s style to that of Defoe and marvels at his power to convey what it was like to see white people, a ship, the ocean, flying fish, snow, and a watch for the first time. Schomburg owned copies of the English and American first editions of Equiano’s book as well as five early-nineteenth-century reprints.

The exhibition includes *Religious Experience and Journal of Mrs. Jarena Lee, Giving an Account of Her Call to Preach the Gospel* (1849), a defiant work, considering that women were not wanted as preachers. The copies of Eric Walrond’s *Tropic Death* (1926) and Claude McKay’s *Home to Harlem* (1922) have very personal inscriptions to Schomburg. They say as much as the accompanying newspaper articles by Walrond and Zora Neale Hurston about how important Schomburg was to the Harlem Renaissance writers. *Black Studies on 135th Street* reminds us that when McKay was abroad, he depended on Schomburg for help with research and money. Schomburg’s seed library includes Langston Hughes’s first book, *The Weary Blues*,and Steven G. Fullwood, a former assistant curator in the Manuscript, Archives, and Rare Books Division, tells us in his contributionthat Schomburg didn’t just collect Hughes’s work; Hughes sent him books he found on his travels and continued to send books to the collection after Schomburg’s death.

In 1972 the director William Greaves gathered several witnesses to and participants in the Harlem Renaissance at the apartment of Duke Ellington—among them Eubie Blake, Leigh Whipper, Aaron Douglas, Richard Bruce Nugent, Arna Bontemps, George Schuyler, Romare Bearden, and Richard B. Moore—to remember those days and to discuss the impact of the period. Greaves’s son, David Greaves, sifted through 60,000 feet of the film his father shot that afternoon and assembled the selections into the recently released *Once Upon a Time in Harlem*, a warm and moving documentary. At one point in it, Regina Anderson Andrews, hired as a librarian at the 135th Street branch library in 1923, remembers how the young writers and poets of the Harlem Renaissance would come to sit and write, to feel at home and protected in the quiet.

Schomburg used the money from the sale of his library in 1926 to go on a hunt for books in Europe. He had given an address to the Negro Academy in 1923 on the subject of Juan de Pareja, the painter enslaved to Velázquez. In 1926 Schomburg had the director of the Prado in Madrid unlock several doors to reveal Pareja’s *Calling of Saint Matthew* (1661), which portrays the well-dressed painter as the sole figure in the composition looking directly at the viewer, though he is on the edge of the painting. Antonio Palomino in his history of eminent Spanish painters tells the story of Velázquez painting Pareja in Rome in 1650 and then having him deliver himself the portrait intended for exhibition at the Pantheon. Those who saw the painted canvas, so dignified and alive, stared in amazement at it and then at Pareja, not knowing which to address or which would answer them.

The Metropolitan Museum invited the Schomburg Center to be a part of its exhibition “Juan de Pareja, Afro-Hispanic Painter” in 2023, because Schomburg had been the first to attempt a catalogue raisonné of his work and because he had been the only one to write about Pareja in the early twentieth century. The exhibition displayed some of the works from the sixteenth century that had been in Schomburg’s seed library as well as a work he purchased in 1932 and cherished: *Ad catholicum pariter* *et invictissimum Phillippum Dei gratia Hispaniarum regem* by Juan Latino, the Ethiopian-born, emancipated Latinist at the University of Granada, of which only two copies survive. A collection of poems and epigrams, it was printed in Granada in 1573 in tribute to Don Juan of Austria, the illegitimate son of the Holy Roman Emperor Charles V, who led the fleet that defeated the Ottomans at the Battle of Lepanto in 1571, and in praise of Philip II of Spain.

The gallery for the Schomburg exhibition is designed to evoke the wallpaper and hearth of his parlor. Schomburg’s family—he had eight children—put up with the ever-increasing number of books. They understood. Also on display are photographs of his modest homes, first in Harlem, then Brooklyn. The postcards of the nineteenth-century black actor Ira Aldridge and images of Wheatley and Dumas remind us that the collection first had a domestic setting. There are several works by Albert Alexander Smith, a black artist who lived in Paris in the 1920s and 1930s: watercolors of Wheatley and Rene Maran, the author of *Batouala* (1921), the Goncourt Prize–winning novel that offered the first positive portrait of primitivism; and portraits of Dumas and Pushkin.

William Ward’s mezzotint of the Chevalier de Saint-Georges, the eighteenth-century composer from Guadeloupe, hangs high on a gallery wall, and it is his opera, *L’Amant anonyme*, that we are hearing as we look at Schomburg’s books. One of Saint-Georges’s concertantes is among the audio selections available on a “Listening Wall” in the Schomburg lobby, which range from Yoruba *dundun* drums and Samuel Coleridge-Taylor’s “Hiawatha’s Wedding Feast” to a 1925 recording of Paul Robeson intoning “Water Boy,” and a 1960 recording of Harry Belafonte singing “Steal Away.”

The Schomburg’s collection of books and rare manuscripts, music and oral history recordings, prints and photographs amount to more than 11,000,000 items. And yet this is a very intimate exhibition, a moving portrait of a cultural hero. For book lovers, it is a dream. It is also affirming. The Schomburg Center is not a locked-up campus or a school library under threat of books that have been deemed dangerous to the young being removed by a local authority. It is full of what had been commercial failures and what had been unwanted, offensive, opposed. Its collection challenges the well-meaning tendency in US publishing to internalize the will to censorship. That the Jazz Age is one hundred years ago feels weird. But the liberations set in motion since World War I go on, right along with the urge to defeat them. A power-stupid administration may be seeking to impose revisions of history on a nation’s educational institutions and museums, but as A’Lelia Bundles has said, nothing can stop us from telling our stories.

I remember being in a penitential mood on my first visit to the Schomburg. It was 1981 and the Schomburg’s new building, the work of J. Max Bond Jr., had opened a few months before. I’d never been in the old building, by McKim, Mead, and White, and when introduced elsewhere to its curator of many years, Jean Blackwell Hutson, I was ignorant of her importance.

In the lobby there was a portrait of Schomburg and a bronze and white marble bust of Ira Aldridge, dressed for his most famous role, Othello, by the Milanese sculptor Pietro Calvi, who supplied polychrome marble busts of black people for the smoking rooms of Europe in the days when white gentlemen wanted such sentries as evidence they’d traveled. Stairs took me down to the reading room and to a room of machines where I could squint at the microfilm of the beautiful handwriting of Paul Laurence Dunbar’s desperate letters to his absconded wife, Alice. Dunbar disliked Whitman.

A bust of Miss Hutson presided over the card catalog, the rows of little drawers like shelves in an ossuary. In the Jean Blackwell Hutson Research and Reference Division, among lithographs by Jacob Lawrence and Paul Colin, the Romare Bearden collage, busts by Richmond Barthé, Bo Walker’s wood sculpture of Frederick Douglass, the Norman Lewis, Archibald Motley, and Malvin Gray Johnson paintings, the library staff was coping with the afternoon’s crop of whispering students, scholars, sons constructing family trees, and a loud crazy who had mysterious reasons for not wanting to check her wrinkled plastic bags in the cloakroom upstairs. I was another. In the downstairs reading room, with the sunken courtyard of birch and ivy, under the murals by Aaron Douglas, under something that resembled an electronic bingo board that told you when the books you had asked for had come from the stacks, I sat down.
