# 正文

JD Vance, the fiftieth vice-president of the United States and a likely Republican nominee for the 2028 presidential election, is the product of an extraordinary degree of self-creation and mythmaking, even by the standards of American political careers. He was born James Donald Bowman, the son of a single mother and a father who left when he was a toddler; at age six, having been adopted by his mother’s new husband, he became James David Hamel; graduating from law school in his late twenties, he took the last name of his beloved grandmother and became J.D. Vance. Even after he found fame with that name—as the author of the multimillion-selling memoir *Hillbilly Elegy* (2016) and as one of the liberal media’s more prolific anti-Trump pundits—the tinkering continued, though at a more modest scale: when he ran for the Senate in 2021 he did so with a revised first name to go with his new hard-line MAGA political convictions, having dropped the periods between his initials to become JD Vance. This remains, at the time of this writing, the vice-president’s name.

It is not unusual, of course, for an American political career to be built on the foundation of a life story. In 1995, when he was still a young lecturer at the University of Chicago, Barack Obama published his memoir *Dreams from My Father*, and he became an Illinois state senator the following year. And from the 1980s until the 2010s, first with his book *The Art of the Deal* and then via the fifteen-season run of NBC’s *The Apprentice*,Donald Trump cultivated the straight-shooting business savant persona that propelled him to the White House. Perhaps because he lacks the formidable personal presence of either of these predecessors, Vance has built his career to a remarkable degree on narrative foundations.

*Hillbilly Elegy* was published in June 2016, and its fortunes benefited greatly from the media’s scramble to account for the MAGA movement’s popularity among white working-class voters. Vance, an opportunist of rare instinct and exceptional ruthlessness, retailed hard truths about American society translated into a language the liberal elite wanted to hear. His book took a complicated history of Appalachian poverty and deindustrialization and made of it an American Dream–friendly story of personal redemption and self-determination. By his account, the reasons Appalachian communities were thwarted by social deprivation and addiction had less to do with structural forces—generations of class inequality, dispossession of labor by capital—and more to do with the failure of individuals to extricate themselves from bad situations, as he himself had done with such dramatic success. Vance made his name, in a sense, by speaking for his people with one side of his mouth while condemning them out of the other. “There is a lack of agency here,” he wrote in *Hillbilly Elegy*, “a feeling that you have little control over your life and a willingness to blame everyone but yourself.”

Writing some kind of memoir is, of course, a nonnegotiable component in the machinery of a high-level American political career. When it comes to presidential races, the number of candidates will invariably be equaled by the number of memoirs, more often than not ghostwritten.[\*](#fn-*) And the reasons for this are not hard to discern: the cultural soil of the United States is famously rich in entrepreneurialism and individualism. If a person is to make something of himself in American life, he must have a sales pitch; he must be able to sing, for the general public, the song of himself and his origins.

And yet Vance is, in certain crucial respects, an outlier: he was a hugely successful writer well before he embarked on any kind of political career. *Hillbilly Elegy* has a great many literary flaws, but it is by no means a standard campaign meet and greet. We might be tempted to compare it to, say, Obama’s *Dreams from My Father*, but in fact that book didn’t exactly fly off the shelves when it was first published; *Hillbilly Elegy* sold hundreds of thousands of copies in its first year and in 2020 was made into a film by Ron Howard starring Amy Adams and Glenn Close. Vance’s publishing-to-politics trajectory seems to me, in this sense, an entirely unique proposition—as though Frank McCourt had parlayed the success of *Angela’s Ashes* into a Senate run, or as though *Eat, Pray, Love* had propelled Elizabeth Gilbert all the way to the vice-presidency.

A first-time writer of more ordinary ambition would have followed the success of *Hillbilly Elegy* with another memoir picking up the thread where that breakout success left off, as McCourt did with *’Tis* and Gilbert did with *Committed*. But there is a limit to the power and prestige that accrue to even the most successful of memoirists. Annie Ernaux won the Nobel Prize for her artful excavations of family and class, but she has never been in a position—nor has she, it should be noted, shown any sign of wanting to be in a position—to negotiate peace deals with Iran or to berate EU leaders for their lax military spending.

And yet as we have said, ambitious politicians do tend to become memoirists; so it is that Vance has returned, via this elaborately indirect route, to his old job, or some version of it. According to a report in *Variety* in 2017 Tina Bennett, Vance’s then literary agent, negotiated an $8 million publishing deal for a follow-up to *Hillbilly Elegy*. *Communion* both is and, in many notable ways, is not that follow-up. Vance has said that he began writing it in 2017, well before he entered politics. The reason the book took so long to complete is self-evident, and certainly more impressive than most writers’ excuses for not turning in a manuscript on time. The reason that it’s finally being published now, in 2026, is just as clear: its author very much wishes to become the next president of the United States.

*Communion* presents itself as an account of Vance’s personal journey to the Catholic faith. In this sense, it really is a follow-up to *Hillbilly Elegy*, which was the story of his personal journey from the Appalachian working class to the elite realms of Yale Law School and venture capital. Like that book, *Communion* is characterized by its author’s almost obsessive self-positioning as an outsider, as a man who gained entry to the cloisters of those coastal elites and who has nothing but contempt for the careerism and hypocrisy he encountered there.

Vance makes frequent displays of self-criticism, often depicting himself in his younger days as unthinkingly prostrate before the false liberal-elite god of professional advancement. “At some point in my university career, my desire for stability morphed into a craving for conquest,” he writes. “I didn’t just want a nice car, a house, and a decent vacation. I wanted to win for winning’s sake.” This early part of the book is filled with the sort of stranger-in-a-strange-land shtick that will be familiar to readers of his earlier memoir. (One of the chapters is, in fact, entitled “Stranger in a Strange Land.”) He parses the complicated prestige economy he observed at Yale, where, because everyone is already assumed to be wealthy, social status is derived not from owning a large vacation home or from securing a position at a prestigious private equity firm but instead from getting a White House job or a federal appeals court clerkship. “But prestige, interestingly enough, never flowed from the nonprofessional parts of life,” as he puts it in one of the book’s many grotesquely faux-naive passages.

> No one discussed family life with the same level of pride with which they announced their fancy jobs. In other words, prestige could be accumulated through professional choices and accomplishments. But being a good mother or a good husband wasn’t valued nearly as highly as achieving a new credential, being admitted to a top school, or getting a great job.

This sort of thing is risible enough, of course, but it is downright absurd when you reflect, as any sentient reader must, on the writer, who is one of the most nakedly careerist public figures in living memory. And this is where *Communion*—which I will say now, in order to get it out of the way, is a very bad and boring book, in ways that greatly exceed even the badness and boringness of *Hillbilly Elegy—*gets interesting, if not to read then certainly to think about. Precisely because it is so bad and boring, I found myself reading it through its author’s palpable desire to become president, and therefore as a book whose true subject can never be acknowledged or even alluded to.

That critique of shallow credentialism and careerism at Yale, for instance, is rendered at once explicable and absurd by the reader’s knowledge of a fact rigorously withheld from the text itself: that Vance is criticizing careerism precisely in service of his own monstrous professional ambition. Once you begin to see this technique as something like an organizing principle, the whole book becomes an exercise in avoidance, like a sort of Oulipo-style experiment in formal constraint.

Vance does allude to what he calls his “evolution from Trump critic to Trump supporter,” but has nothing at all to say on the substance of his own criticisms. It’s left up to a moderately well-informed reader to recall that Vance, whose mother struggled with opioid addiction, referred to Trump in a 2016 *Atlantic* article as “cultural heroin” and that, in a leaked message to a former Yale roommate, he reportedly said, “I go back and forth between thinking Trump is a cynical asshole like Nixon who wouldn’t be that bad (and might even prove useful) or that he’s America’s Hitler.”

He also says next to nothing about why he pivoted so hard from his original stance. He merely states that the many great successes of Trump’s first term made his former position unsustainable, though he never suggests what these successes might have been. “To my critics,” he writes, “it was a politically cynical maneuver to gain political power. I doubt I’ll ever change their minds.” In fact, he doesn’t try. He contents himself with a kind of autobiographical tautology: his “political evolution,” he claims, “was downstream of the much more fundamental change in how I saw the world. I had looked inside our elite institutions and found them intellectually and spiritually broken. Was it any surprise that I would eventually reject their politics as well?”

To dwell on Vance’s hypocrisy and cynicism here is to risk seeming naive, but the depth of his contempt for the reader is genuinely impressive. Can this man really be claiming that his experience of an intellectually and spiritually broken elite led him to embrace the presidency of *Donald Trump*? Does he really expect his readers to overlook the lurid venality and corruption of an administration of which he himself is a central figure—to simply forget about the crypto pump-and-dump schemes, the lavish gifts from foreign interests, the vast fortunes amassed by the president and his family in his second term—and unquestioningly accept this abject excuse for a moral rationale? Apparently he does. And he may have good reason for doing so, given that reviewers of his first book, and the liberal media pundit class more generally, swallowed *Hillbilly Elegy* whole. As Gabriel Winant wrote in a 2023 essay for *n+1*, the book’s success was symptomatic. It

> bespeaks the intellectual and political barrenness of elite liberalism that so many people and institutions—the agent and publisher who got him in print, the Yale faculty who advanced his career, the journalists at outlets like Vox and the *New York Times* who reviewed him favorably, the politicians like Hillary Clinton who praised his work—failed to see what he was doing straightaway.

Not only does Vance fail to provide any kind of serious account of his political conversion, but he devotes strikingly little energy to Trump at all. In one sense, it could be argued that this omission makes sense and is even necessary, given that *Communion* is framed as a reflection on religion and specifically Vance’s conversion to Catholicism. But the book is at most partly that, and even in the passages in which he attempts to engage in some depth with the questions of religious faith, the writing is almost entirely defined by what’s not said. Vance makes basically no attempt, for instance, to reconcile the Trump administration’s extraordinary and deliberate cruelty toward immigrants (not to mention its starting a war with Iran, or its support of a genocide in Gaza) with the explicit teachings of Christ.

It’s worth recalling that, mere days after becoming vice-president, Vance got embroiled in a public conflict with the Vatican on exactly this subject. In January 2025, in an interview on Fox News, he invoked a garbled version of the Augustinian notion of *ordo amoris*, the “order of love,” to claim that Christians ought first to love family, then neighbors, then fellow citizens, and only then the rest of the world. Pope Francis responded with a letter to American bishops in which he pointedly clarified that love, for Christians, is not a “concentric expansion of interests that little by little extend to other persons and groups”; rather, he wrote, “the true *ordo amoris* that must be promoted is that which we discover by meditating constantly on the parable of the ‘Good Samaritan’… that is, by meditating on the love that builds a fraternity open to all, without exception.” Francis’s successor, Pope Leo XIV, has been no less critical of the cruelty and violence of the Trump administration’s foreign policy, pointing out that Jesus “is never on the side of those who once wielded the sword and today drop bombs.” Infamously, Vance responded to that statement by saying that the pope should “be careful” when speaking on matters of theology.

There is something irreducibly comic about Vance making a great public display of his conversion to Catholicism only to immediately, on assuming the office of vice-president, start a high-profile dispute with the actual pope. There is a word for people who dispute the pope’s authority on doctrinal matters, and that word is “Protestant.” And despite his book’s ostensible focus on how and why he became a Catholic, there is really no sense, in these pages, of Vance having any serious spiritual commitment; there is no real engagement with the question of why he chose to join the one, holy, Catholic, and apostolic Church, as opposed to, say, renewing a connection with the evangelical faith of his family (or, for that matter, converting to the Hindu faith in which his Indian American wife, Usha, was raised). He says nothing about the veneration of Mary—aside from noting that it was admired by Charlie Kirk—and his discussion of the sacrament of confession is laughably perfunctory. “The idea of speaking of my sins to a stranger mortified me,” he writes. “Yet our Catholic friend Sam described confession in a way that resonated with me: It’s like therapy, but with less whining and more guilt.”

Similarly, despite the book’s grand title, the sacrament of Communion is afforded just a few brisk sentences. “The word itself comes from the Latin root *communio*, which means sharing something in common or union or fellowship,” he writes, in the immemorial style of an undergraduate padding out a term paper the night before it’s due. “For Catholics, Communion means not only to personally encounter Christ but to unite us as a single worshiping body.” But even this aspect of the faith—the Mass, coming together, the literal communion—seems to have gained no real purchase on Vance, who writes in the book’s very first lines that “about half the time these days, we attend Mass at home, in the three-story historic mansion where we are living during my term as vice president.” The book has no sooner begun, in other words, than the reader is forced to doubt the seriousness of its author’s commitment to his supposed new faith. In fact, you don’t even have to open the book for these doubts to take hold: as multiple observers have noted, the pretty, white-steepled church featured on its cover is in fact a Methodist house of worship in rural Virginia.

The point here is not that the church happens to belong to a different denomination but that its not being a Catholic church should have been blatantly obvious to anyone who knew the first thing about Catholicism, separated as it is by a famously vast aesthetic chasm from the Low Church Protestant faiths. (Is Vance, as vice-president of the United States, busier than most writers with a book about to come out? Almost certainly. Is he too busy to glance at a cover sent by his publisher for his approval? Certainly not.) If *Communion* were, in fact, an Oulipo-style exercise in formal constraint—an account of a conversion to Catholicism by a man who isn’t actually a Catholic; a book written in service of a presidential run by a man who cannot give voice to his desire to be president—HarperCollins could have done a lot worse than put such a legibly Methodist church on its cover.

One of the memoir’s more notable negative spaces is that around Peter Thiel, an account of whose influence is vital to any real understanding of Vance’s remarkable career. Thiel is mentioned in the book, but merely in passing; Vance writes of attending a talk he gave at Yale on the connection between shallow and hypercompetitive elites and technological stagnation. Thiel, he writes, “gave voice to something I had been feeling, but had not fully understood: that I was obsessed with achievement per se—not to accomplish something meaningful, but to win a social competition. I had prioritized striving over character, and for what was I even striving?”

The overbearing irony here is that it was Thiel who more than anyone else fueled and facilitated Vance’s striving. Under his influence, Vance decided to forgo a career in law to work in venture capital. In 2016 he became a principal in Thiel’s firm Mithril Capital. In 2019, with Thiel’s backing—along with that of Marc Andreessen, Eric Schmidt, and others—he started his own Ohio-based tech VC firm, Narya. In 2021 Thiel, an early Silicon Valley adopter of the MAGA movement—and a decisive figure in Vance’s “evolution” from Never Trumper to MAGA true believer—brokered a rapprochement between Vance and Trump at Mar-a-Lago. This prepared the ground for Vance’s Senate run the following year, with Trump’s endorsement and with Thiel’s financial backing (at $15 million, the largest such donation in Senate history). According to a report in *The* *Washington Post*, when Trump was deliberating on a running mate for the 2024 election, he received multiple calls from Thiel, among other powerful Silicon Valley figures, imploring him to add Vance to the ticket. The Biden presidency was broadly unpopular with Silicon Valley’s investor class, because of its strict regulation of cryptocurrencies and its attempts to regulate the development of artificial intelligence. The calculation—that a Trump presidency with a tech VC on the ticket would rectify the situation—proved correct.

For decades Thiel has been working to reshape the American political system, and as a consequence the international order, according to his postdemocratic ideological convictions. Thiel has infamously claimed that freedom (by which he seems to mean capitalism) is no longer compatible with democracy, and he has advocated for remaking the state in the image of the corporation. His antidemocratic tendencies have, in recent years, been informed by an increasingly extreme conservative Christianity. Last year he gave a series of lectures in which he claimed that environmental regulations and guardrails on developing technology—AI in particular—could prepare the ground for the coming of the Antichrist. (Among his suggestions as to the identity of this Antichrist was Greta Thunberg.)

Thiel’s influence is clearly legible in Vance’s increasingly extreme and illiberal political positions, his opposition to the regulation of AI, his contempt for international institutions, and his venomous antipathy toward universities and academics. “The professors are the enemy,” he said in a 2021 speech, quoting Richard Nixon. “We have to honestly and aggressively attack the universities in this country.” In a podcast interview that same year, he called for a “de-Ba’athification program” for government employees: “We should just seize the administrative state for our own purposes. We should fire…every single mid-level bureaucrat, every civil servant in the administrative state, [and] replace them with our people.” Anyone who is familiar with Thiel’s political positions, or with those of his friend Curtis Yarvin—a far-right blogger who for many years has been among the most influential figures in Silicon Valley’s rightward turn—will recognize the provenance of these ideas and the purpose they serve for Vance as he becomes their vector.

If *Communion* has a central premise, it’s the fiction of Vance’s apostasy from a hollow secular-liberal faith of prestige and careerism. This vein of pure Thielism is one of the few aspects of the book through which Vance’s real motivation—his own annihilating personal ambition and Thiel’s ideological interests—is laid bare. And that strange emptiness, that lack of character in multiple senses of the word, accounts for much of what is uniquely unsettling about Vance. This is a man who will become a mouthpiece for any political position, who will attach himself to any powerful person he feels will further his own ambitions; this is a man who will betray whomever and whatever it is necessary to betray in order to obtain power.

In one of the countless passages in which Vance claims to see the error of his former ambitious and careerist ways, he borrows a phrase from the opinion columnist David Brooks: *résumé virtues*. This, he writes, describes “the things that make us more marketable or successful in the professional world (in contrast to the *eulogy virtues* that people discuss after our deaths).” He was, he tells us, “obsessed with these résumé virtues and running a race I didn’t fully understand. I didn’t care; I just wanted to win.” The passage is a sort of purloined letter, a confession hiding in plain sight, placed there by an author whose work, whose entire political career, is characterized by a blithe contempt for the intelligence of his audience. Vance’s professed Catholicism, a faith he wants us to believe has deposed that former ambition and careerism, is itself nothing more than a résumé virtue, a calculated move in a larger bid for the job he desperately wants and cannot yet admit he wants.

Christ himself had much to say about those who used the outward performance of faith to further their worldly ambition; he wished woe on the “scribes and Pharisees” who strove for the outward appearance of righteousness while being inwardly “full of hypocrisy and iniquity.” To read *Communion* is to feel strongly that Vance understands himself to be just such a scribe and a Pharisee, just such a whited sepulchre containing nothing but bones and rotted flesh. The Bible passage he repeats more than any other in *Communion*, blurting it out like a repressed truth that cannot be assimilated by even the most efficient machinery of lies, is Jesus’ teaching recorded in Matthew 7:16–20:

> Ye shall know them by their fruits. Do men gather grapes of thorns, or figs of thistles? Even so every good tree bringeth forth good fruit; but a corrupt tree bringeth forth evil fruit. A good tree cannot bring forth evil fruit, neither can a corrupt tree bring forth good fruit. Every tree that bringeth not forth good fruit is hewn down, and cast into the fire. Wherefore by their fruits ye shall know them.

Vance’s book, rotten fruit that it is of his own corruption and of the wider corruption of the system that produced and sustains him, cannot withstand the poetry of these words. *Wherefore by their fruits ye shall know them*. The words on the page burn with a furious dramatic irony, consuming the cheap vanity of this hollow and iniquitous book, reducing it to ash in the reader’s hands.
