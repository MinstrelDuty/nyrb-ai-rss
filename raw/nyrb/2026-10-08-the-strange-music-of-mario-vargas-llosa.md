# 正文

When Mario Vargas Llosa announced in October 2023 that his new novel, *Le dedico mi silencio—*now published in English as *I Give You My Silence*—would be his last, I was intrigued by what that farewell to fiction might involve. How would this prodigious author of eighteen other novels—among them the most adventuresome in the Latin American literary canon—conclude his career? (He died in April 2025 at the age of eighty-nine.)

I had been enthused by *The Feast of the Goat* (2000), his antidictatorial tour de force, but disappointed by the six subsequent novels, which lacked, I thought, his inventiveness and stylistic brilliance.[1](#fn-1) Refreshingly, his swan song, albeit a minor achievement compared to the exceptional works of his younger years, is charming and thought-provoking, again addressing the dilemma of Peru’s identity that he had pursued since *The Time of the Hero* (1963), his groundbreaking first novel. If Balzac is right (and Vargas Llosa has insisted that he is) that “the novel is the private history of nations,” then how to write about a heterogenous Latin American country that has the extraordinary privilege of not having an identity, “because it has all of them”? This is an idea he formulated in his Nobel Lecture in 2010:

> A compatriot of mine, José María Arguedas, called Peru the country of “every blood” (*todas las sangres*). I do not believe any formula defines it better: that is what we are and that is what all Peruvians carry inside us, whether we like it or not: an aggregate of traditions, races, beliefs, and cultures proceeding from the four cardinal points.

A positive evaluation, but in his most significant, anguished narrative approaches to his homeland, Peru appears as “fucked up,” the outcome of centuries of rivalries and violence, its inhabitants separated by intractable hatreds of class, race, and history and by a fractured geography ranging from highland sierras and Amazonian jungles to lush valleys and teeming coastlines. It could be ventured that Vargas Llosa’s life has been dedicated in his foremost works to mapping out “a radiography of Peruvian unhappiness” and trying to resolve the puzzle of a land forged from the hostility of *todas las sangres*.

In *I Give You My Silence*, the unlikely candidate for this exploration is Toño Azpilcueta, a thwarted middle-aged dreamer with modest scholarly pretensions. Rabidly nationalistic, this failed intellectual is critical of literati who have abandoned Peru for Paris (such as the Francophile Vargas Llosa, the only foreigner inducted into the Académie Française). Azpilcueta’s patriotism finds an outlet in his idealistic love of the *vals criollo* (also known as the *vals peruano*), the dance that the underprivileged people of African descent in Lima appropriated from the waltzes imported from Europe by the upper middle class in the nineteenth century—proof, Vargas Llosa once said, of “the creativity of the ignorant part of our society to transform what comes from abroad, and to use those…products as very positive grounds to create and to build something different.”[2](#fn-2) This hybrid, which only Peru could have produced, is Toño’s passion, though it quickly turns into a fixation bordering on insanity.[3](#fn-3)

Readers may recognize in Toño another of the monomaniacs inhabiting other humorous Vargas Llosa experiments, most memorably Pedro Camacho, the *escribidor* (scribbler) of *Aunt Julia and the Scriptwriter* (1977), who goes crazy as he spews out his radionovelas, or Pantaleón, an army captain ordered to organize brothels for sex-starved recruits in the Peruvian jungles in *Captain Pantoja and the Special Service* (1973). Vargas Llosa treats these farcical avatars benevolently, as he does the hero of *I Give You My Silence*.

Toño is a foolish man but endowed with some admirable qualities. His fanatical devotion to his hack writing is akin to the author’s own belief that literature is “the first loyalty, the first responsibility, the first obligation,” a ruthless egocentrism willing to sacrifice everything (in Toño’s case, his beleaguered, altruistic wife and two neglected daughters) in pursuit of his vision. Like Don Quixote, he is brave in the defense of his ideas, which is the opposite of the cowardice that, according to Vargas Llosa, is the worst cardinal sin. Nor does Toño’s courage veer into the boastful machismo (or the accompanying racism and misogyny) that drives many Vargas Llosa male characters to self-defeat. For his last novel, he has traveled far from his initial fictional efforts (*The Time of the Hero*; *The Green House*, 1966; *Conversation in the Cathedral*, 1969): no widespread violence, no rape, no hero ensnared in the prison house of his creator’s existential bleakness. Even Toño’s platonic love for Cecilia Barraza lacks the transgressive sexuality of the author’s major novels.[4](#fn-4)

What makes Toño worthy of literary attention is an accident. One night he listens to Lalo Molfino, an undiscovered mulatto guitarist performing in a marginal neighborhood of the Peruvian capital, whose “miraculous fingering” hushes the audience as he strums,

> wringing profound, disconcerting, unprecedented tones from the strings that had a ring of madness that rendered the silence tangible…. It was as if the music were penetrating [Toño], entering his body, marrying with his blood and flowing through his veins…. Tears bathed Toño’s face…and he longed to embrace his countrymen, his brothers and sisters who had witnessed this marvel…. So deeply had the music permeated those present that any social, racial, intellectual, or political differences between them faded into the background.

This quasi-mystical experience “of fraternal love and love of country” that inflames those present compels Toño to chase a totalizing thesis: the vals peruano that Lalo has borne to its most sublime heights is the dormant expression of a nation that, despite its past ruptures, is quintessentially unified. At a fratricidal time (the novel is set during the Sendero Luminoso terrorist insurrection of the later 1980s) when blood is being spilled everywhere, it is urgent to proclaim that *todas las sangres* are, after all, one blood.

When Toño hears, a few months later, that Lalo has died a beggar’s death, unknown, unmourned, buried in a mass grave, he determines to go beyond publishing ephemeral articles in Lima newspapers and to write a book on the vals peruano and the man who was “the greatest guitarist in the world.” His research leads him to Lalo’s birthplace in remote Chiclayo and Puerto Etén, where he learns that Lalo was forsaken as a baby in a gargantuan, reeking garbage dump and was about to be devoured by rats when he was rescued by an Italian priest called Molfino. This allows Vargas Llosa to indulge his lifelong fascination with excrement and detritus, echoing the proclivities of Zavalita, the hapless rebel of *Conversation in the Cathedral*: “Each time I write about something that fills me with repugnance, I make [it]…as disgusting (*asqueroso*) as possible.” Vargas Llosa seems to view abjection as an aesthetic weapon wielded against conventional tastes.

The horrors of the rotting tons of waste are compounded for Toño by a phobia that has hovered over most of his waking life as well as his nightmares: at the most stressful moments he feels rodents invading him, imaginary assaults that make him strip in public and scratch away at the hallucinatory beasts. These rats are not new to Vargas Llosa’s fiction: in Lima, in the Amazon, the arid plains of Piura, and the high sierras, his Peru is infested with these pests, but not until this final novel do they become central to his storytelling, as they carry, according to Toño’s paranoia, the “corruption, the sicknesses and the weakness that undermine the collective spirit that music has forged.”[5](#fn-5) It is not far-fetched to suggest that Vargas Llosa, in his final fanfare, chose to immerse his character—as a parallel to the author’s own voyage throughout his work—not only in detritus but also, more significantly, in the lower depths of communities, without which it would be impossible to present the plenitude of Peru.

That vast cesspool where the greatest musical performer in history survived death as an infant must be experienced for Lalo’s life to be written, for Toño to identify with the subject of his investigation, both of them beset by rats, both (all three, if we include Varga Llosa) trying to articulate a fratricidal, orphaned nation that can only rise above that threatening, primeval squalor through the salvation of art. An endless search, as that endeavor to imagine “what Lalo Molfino had felt there, abandoned, enveloped in the stench, surrounded by various viscous insects crawling over his body” is as close as Toño’s posthumous homage will ever get. Lalo never ceases to be an enigma that, like Peru, cannot be wholly plumbed, leaving behind as his sole legacy a mysterious message: “Le dedico mi silencio.”[6](#fn-6)

If Lalo is reserved, enigmatic, and ultimately inscrutable, his pursuer Toño is anything but silent. His travails, affably narrated in the third person, are interspersed, in alternating chapters, with a series of long-winded pseudo-academic divagations excerpted from the disorganized book he will publish. This bifurcation between the perspective of the all-knowing narrator and Toño’s pompous mini treatises on the vals peruano allow Vargas Llosa, enamored of duplication and duplicity, to indulge in his favorite narrative strategy. His previous fiction is riddled with a multitude of contradictory points of view, landscapes, conversations, characters, chronologies. Here this drastically simplified forked tactic makes sense: as Azpilcueta neurotically corrects and rewrites his digressions in a frenzied search for perfection and closure, he keeps adding ever more extraneous issues (bullfighting, the Catholic Church, the Incas, witch doctors, alternative medicine, colonial history, soccer, cuisine, the Spanish language), permitting us to witness, up close, his snowballing delirium and gyrations as he pursues totality, a search that is an homage to Flaubert, whose own last novel, the unfinished *Bouvard et Pécuchet*, mocks the flailing exertions of the two amiable imbeciles of the title to exhaust all forms of knowledge.

Flaubert’s shadow presence in the novel is accompanied, paradoxically, by his conspicuous absence in its form. The French master helped Vargas Llosa discover that the author must be like God, present everywhere and visible nowhere, never intervening with his opinions. This undeviating principle is flouted in *I Give You My Silence*. The voice of the protagonist, full of allusions that he could not have come up with, is dizzily interwoven with the voice of Vargas Llosa himself, flummoxing readers who are frequently unable to distinguish one from the other. How could Toño know of the existence, for instance, of Salvador Garmendia, the Venezuelan experimental writer of alienation, or the problems in Paris of the Peruvian poet César Moro, both favorites of Vargas Llosa?

As I advanced further into the thicket of these progressively dull, repetitive interpolations, I felt that it was not just Toño who was losing his way but the narrator as well. How often do we need to hear that the love of two minor characters, the white upper-class Toni Lagarde and the poor mulatta Lala Solórzano, proves that the vals that brought them together can break down the barriers of provenance and prejudice? And I began to wonder if these fluctuating narrators in their *folie à deux* did not indicate Vargas Llosa’s hidden discomfort with the vals peruano itself. Or was I projecting my own unease—and steadily emerging ennui—onto the author?

Growing up in Santiago de Chile, I, like many of my elite contemporaries in other Latin American lands, had judged those songs as vulgar, mawkish, and schmaltzy. Why would the ultrasophisticated, Europeanizing Vargas Llosa spend meticulous hours in dusty Lima archives tracing the origins and byways of the cheesy vals peruano, which occupied a minor place in his preceding works, as I confirmed when I scoured them for signs of meaningful appearances? (Was I becoming as obsessed as Toño?) I reexamined novels seething with popular songs and dances, in cafés and streets, at private parties, and in brothels where fumbling men and women courted each other, but the rumba and other Latin American rhythms predominated. Even when his characters sporadically dabbled in valses (the harpist Don Anselmo in *The Green House*, the cabaret singer Hortensia in *Conversation in the Cathedral*), no specific lyrics or titles were mentioned; their music was a secondary aspect of their lovelorn, doomed existences. And when the upperclassmen (from “every corner of Peru”) at the military academy in *The Time of the Hero* force a young cadet to sing tunes one hundred times, they first demand a Mexican corrido, followed by a Colombian bolero and then a Cuban mambo, before finally ordering a vals, obviously not the paramount predilection of the cadets—or the author.

At the same time, I recognized how, all through Vargas Llosa’s saga, those romantic vals songs weepily intoxicate countless characters from all parts of society. Just as Madame Bovary’s mind and heart are shaped by the daydreams of the amorous *romans* she has been reading, they (and probably the youthful author himself) have learned their melodramatic way of looking at the world, at desire and betrayal, through the stanzas of those overemotional compositions.

*I Give You My Silence*, therefore, uneasily jumbles two contrasting views of the vals peruano. Though Vargas Llosa keeps an ironic distance from his protagonist’s eccentric and unverifiable insistence (also ridiculed by a number of skeptical onlookers) that the vals criollo is the savior of a shattered, contested nation, in the end he found no other cultural manifestation that could represent, however ambivalently, his own search to resolve the unresolvable conundrum of Peru’s identity.

It was a choice that had been percolating for a long time: trawling his past writings I unearthed, in a collection of his journalism centered on Peru, “El país de las mil caras” (“The Country of a Thousand Faces”), a 1983 opinion piece in a Lima newspaper that is an irrefutable antecedent of what was to become his last novel, a tentative rehearsal for it, highlighting the vals criollo as the maximum expression of a peculiar national phenomenon called *huachafería*. This is an elusive group of traits that even the refined and perceptive Vargas Llosa is unable to fully pinpoint, except through a series of adjectives: urban, antirational, full of excessive rhetoric, intuitive, verbose, melodic, and, above all, *sensiblera* (maudlin), a *huachafo* sentimentalism that he insists is the defining characteristic of Peruvian idiosyncrasy and psychology. And he goes on to expand being huachafo into a universal category, Peru’s “contribution to world culture,” signaling, for example, very much tongue in cheek, its presence in the Sacré Coeur, the Byzantine Empire, and even the Athenians (as opposed to the Spartans, who were never huachafos). Forty years later both attitudes find their unsettled way into *I Give You My Silence*: many of the opinions in the article, and even its title, “Un champancito, hermanito?,” reappear in Toño’s mouth and provide, initially, the title of his book that keeps trying to bring together *todas las sangres* of Peru.[7](#fn-7)

*Todas las sangres*? Not quite. Vargas Llosa, in that 1983 article, affirms that huachafería is prevalent in all social classes (rich whites, middle-income *mestizos*, Black plebeians) with the exception of peasants (*campesinos*), who are never huachafos and thus unable to compose a vals criollo. This, then, is the unnamed specter that haunts Toño’s attempt to unify Peru through the vals and haunts the novel in which that mission is narrated and, ultimately, the Peruvian nation that gave birth to Mario Vargas Llosa: the campesinos, or to put it more bluntly, the indigenous people of the country’s immense and traumatized highlands.

The liberal and tolerant Vargas Llosa has reliably denounced both the exploitation of and the widespread contempt toward the millions of his compatriots whose origins in Peru date back to pre-Columbian times. His particular fascination with the tribes living in the jungles can be attested since *The Green House* and *The Storyteller* and sporadically in other fictions. But those Amazonian aboriginals, destined to be exterminated or absorbed, do not threaten Peru’s construction of its nationhood. When the Aguaruna leader Jum in *The Green House* keeps asserting in pidgin Spanish his rights as a “piruano” not to be tortured, not to be oppressed, not to have young girls kidnapped from his native village, only to be taunted by his tormentors, he is laying bare that there is no space in Peru for someone like him, bound for annihilation and anthropological studies.

This is not the case of the masses of Quechua-speaking Indians of the sierras (whom Vargas Llosa considers essentially unqualified to appreciate the allure of the vals peruano), immiserated heirs of the destroyed Inca Empire. The stubborn persistence of those indigenous societies whose members preserve their customs, language, and culture, spurning assimilation into the mainstream, has been a perennial challenge to how Peru identifies itself—and to how Peru’s most famous writer wishes to express that nation in all its variegated complexity.

The only novelist who set himself the task at which Toño fails was José María Arguedas, whose phrase *todas las sangres* was quoted in Vargas Llosa’s Nobel speech, after he had many decades earlier praised him as a “great creator, one of the purest and most original born in (Latin) America” and described him years later as the only writer born in Peru with whom “I have managed to establish an *entrañable* (visceral and endearing) relationship, like the one I have with Faulkner or Flaubert or like I had with Sartre as a youth.” What seduces Vargas Llosa is that Arguedas, a white child brought up in Quechua by the Indians of the sierras, is the only Peruvian (and Latin American) author who magnificently and heartbreakingly straddles both those warring worlds and is therefore as split in his life and culture as the country itself.

In order to overcome that breach, Arguedas does something that Vargas Llosa concedes he cannot do—speak from inside the Other—and he pays a terrible price for that predicament and privilege by committing suicide in 1969. That intention of killing himself is stated by Arguedas at the beginning of his final novel, *El zorro de arriba y el zorro de abajo* (1971), the only one to take place on the coast of Peru instead of in the high hinterlands, in which he tests his thesis, articulated in his 1964 novel *Todas las sangres*, that the indigenous culture and values will be able to save Peru (and every other nation as well, if they only dare to listen). His death, in the book and in reality, is a way of admitting that he has been wrong and would rather die than face the inexorable permanence of his country’s rifts and cleavages.

Despite Vargas Llosa’s appreciation for the work of Arguedas, he took this psychological and literary breakdown as the starting point for a weighty book, *La utopía arcaica: José María Arguedas y las ficciones del indigenismo* (1996), eviscerating Arguedas’s deep-seated desire for what Vargas Llosa terms a false and archaic utopia responsible for Peru’s incapacity to fully modernize and leave behind the liabilities of the past. It can be argued that such a reversal of the initial sympathy he felt for the plight of Arguedas stems from two major events. One is Vargas Llosa’s defeat in the 1990 presidential election, in which he was rejected by darker-skinned Peruvians who saw in him a white elitist incapable of understanding them[8](#fn-8); the other is the fanatical determination of Sendero Luminoso to violently reestablish the Inca Empire that the author mistakenly conflates with the beliefs of Arguedas, labeled as conservative, utopian, and anachronistic. It matters less that this is a questionable reading of Arguedas than to note that Vargas Llosa does not address in his novel *Death in the Andes* (1993) his own preconceptions regarding an indigenous culture adamantly entrenched in the magical thinking diametrically at odds with the rigorous realism that he espoused—the supernatural does not appear in any of his stories.[9](#fn-9)

Vargas Llosa’s blindness to the spiritual culture of those sierra dwellers had already found a derelict manifestation in *Death in the Andes*, in which he forces Lituma (a character as recurring in his work as Rastignac in Balzac’s) to endure a long sojourn in Naccos, a fictitious mining town in the Andes. The experience is infernal and suffocating, and the indigenous populace is portrayed as ignorant, irrational, dirty, delusional, fatalistic, and furtive, willing to sacrifice humans to bloodthirsty Andean deities. Behind the extremity of Lituma’s views, one can discern the revulsion of the secular, militantly atheistic Vargas Llosa, that champion of reason, capitalism, and Western civilization. These mysterious indigenous compatriots are not seen, as Arguedas knew them to be, as dignified heirs to a long relationship with Nature that is collaborative and communal rather than extractive and individualistic, but as backward and superstitious and, confoundingly, impenetrable and unknowable, refusing to be smoothly incorporated into the nation or Vargas Llosa’s representation of it.

Symptomatically, *Death in the Andes* is the most architecturally disjointed of all Vargas Llosa’s creations, unusually incoherent and rambling. I once witnessed the care with which he planned and converged the multiple threads of his intricate timelines. During our fellowship at the Smithsonian, I visited his office and caught a glimpse of an immense bulletin board on which he had charted every incident, every change in time and perspective of *The War at the End of the World*, the magnum opus he was in the process of writing. That tight control of his structure, the *dessin prémédité* (Flaubert again), is a constant in his work, so I feel justified in interpreting the botched elaboration of *Death in the Andes* as the result of his being jinxed and discombobulated by setting his story in the Andes, with its incomprehensible singing rivers and animated stones. Consequently, when he composed his last novel, he fashioned someone whose frantic search for the unity of Peru excludes the sounds and rhythms resonating from those bewildering highlands, a bumbling “expert” who ignores that his nation’s lasting musical legacy to humanity derives, in fact, from the flutes and drums of Andean songs and dances, the *huaynos* and *danzas* that Arguedas asked to be played at his funeral.

Though conceited Toño is not permitted awareness of how this exclusion undermines his quest, the same cannot be said of the superlatively conscious Vargas Llosa himself. At the very start of his career, he envisaged writing books that were total and perfect—though not in the laughable manner of Toño’s impossible and epic desire six decades later. In a letter from Paris to his friend Abelardo Oquendo on November 9, 1963, Vargas Llosa rejoices that he has finished *The Green House* and now will proceed to write the novel that will become *Conversation in the Cathedral*, and he intends to write after that new one, “another, and another, and one more. Not to die before the fifteenth novel, and fight all-out (*sin cuartel*) to write a work that was quantitively Balzacian and qualitatively Flaubertian.” The encompassing totality of Balzac and the stylistic perfection of Flaubert: Could one ask for a more ambitious goal? I suspect that, as he composed his final odyssey, he came to recognize vicariously and reluctantly through the eyes of his hero and Molièresque brother in letters that this was a task that he would not, could not, fulfill.

At the end of *I Give You My Silence*, Toño is at peace with himself: he has abandoned his fantasies and idealistic projects, is no longer harassed by concocted rats, and has settled into an ordinary, resigned life with his family. It is a mellowing serenity that mirrors, I surmise, Vargas Llosa’s as he accepted the cancer that would kill him. Maybe he also accepted that, just as the vals criollo will never entirely represent Peru because there is too much left out of it, he would never do so either, because Arguedas and the *serrano* highlands have no fathomable standing in his work and that to represent *todas las sangres* was beyond his capacity. It is tempting to believe that he comprehended as his life closed that both he and Arguedas, as I argued in the late 1960s, were not rivals disputing reality, but complementary visions of one conflicted Latin America, that the continent was “sufficiently grand to contain them both.”[10](#fn-10) It is significant that the final word of the title of his final novel is *silence*, Vargas Llosa’s way of acknowledging, like Hamlet and Lalo Molfino, that he is also impenetrable, that he has nothing more to say.

And yet, *silencio* is not the last word he wrote. He concludes *I Give You My Silence* less desolately, by narrating how Toño heads back to “the place he now calls home.” Home. *Hogar*. A benign ending, though laced with ambiguity. Because *hogar* shares the same Spanish root as *fuego*, fire, which he once defined as the essence of Latin American literature. Vargas Llosa was an exceedingly thoughtful author, taught by Flaubert to select every *mot juste* with excruciating deliberation. I would like to imagine that there is a secret message in that resonant parting word, *hogar*, and that after a restless life full of so many contradictory universes, Vargas Llosa awaits us over and over again in the supreme home and fiery refuge of the resplendently incomplete literature he left behind.
