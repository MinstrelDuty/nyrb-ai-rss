# 正文

“What was she doing in Mexico?” asks Hermione Lee in her biography of Penelope Fitzgerald.[1](#fn-1) In October 1952, decades before Fitzgerald became one of the most celebrated British novelists of the twentieth century, she and her five-year-old son, Valpy, sailed from England to New York City and took a bus south of the border, where they remained until January 1953. Fitzgerald’s husband, Desmond, and their two-year-old daughter, Tina, stayed behind. Fitzgerald was pregnant with their third child, Maria, who was born a couple of months after she and Valpy returned.

In a 1980 essay called “Following the Plot,” Fitzgerald briefly describes her time in Mexico, which she says she wanted to use as the basis for a novel. The voyage, she writes, was proposed by “two old ladies called Delaney” from a Mexican city named Fonseca. The women

> lived comfortably, in spite of recent economic reforms, on the proceeds of the family silver mine…. Their relations in Ireland had died, they were alone in the world, and it was hoped that because of some distant friendship they might take kindly to my son and leave him all their money.

The Fitzgeralds’ stay quickly turned into a farce. The old women “were drinking themselves steadily to death.” Residents of Fonseca who were also angling for their wealth “wanted to get rid of me and my son as soon as possible.” Penelope and Valpy were blamed for an accidental death, and they departed “without a legacy, but knowing what it was to be hated.”

When Fitzgerald began publishing novels in the late 1970s, she drew on her odder experiences: working at the BBC during World War II (*Human Voices*), living on a houseboat in London (*Offshore*), teaching at a school for child actors (*At Freddie’s*). The Mexico trip certainly sounds like a promising kernel for one of her dark comedies of ineptitude, self-delusion, and the strange dynamics of small communities. But the story was already too much like fiction. “I could never make it respectable (by which I mean probable) enough to be believed as a novel,” she writes. “Reality has proved treacherous.”

In the biography, Lee is skeptical of Fitzgerald’s account, especially since there’s no place in Mexico called Fonseca: “It sounds, as she says herself, like ‘a yarn,’ a play by Tennessee Williams or a story by Conrad.” Lee seems not to have turned up evidence of the old women or their legacy, but she notes that “the details of the story match the facts about the Saltillo area,” where Valpy remembers going. Saltillo, a city in northeastern Mexico near Monterrey, is “where there were silver mines, and where many Irish and English émigrés settled.”

Then why did Fitzgerald disguise the city? Fonseca is the name of a phantom island that for hundreds of years appeared on European maps of the Caribbean. Could the tale Fitzgerald tells in “Following the Plot” have been a put-on, a phantom story created for an essay about how made-up stories work? Or was the name, which means “dry well” in Latin, a joke about her fruitless efforts both to win the inheritance and to turn the trip into fiction?

Jessica Francis Kane’s daring novel *Fonseca* is not an attempt to write the book Fitzgerald couldn’t but an effort to figure out why she failed to write it. Drawing on Lee’s biography, the scant details Fitzgerald gave about the journey, and Kane’s own recent correspondence with Valpy and Tina, she imagines the Fitzgeralds’ stay in Mexico with the Delaneys. *Fonseca* throws Penelope and Valpy into a mysterious, unsettling place where the rules of etiquette are inscrutable, the social masks grotesque, and reality more treacherous than Penelope at first understands.

Some critics have pronounced Fitzgerald’s economical and precise novels—with their keen yet icy emotional insight, careful attention to the physical world, and lightly worn certainty that our lives might at any time tip over into travesty—as close to perfection as the form can get. Any attempt to emulate her style seems certain to fall short. Kane, an American writer who has published two story collections and two previous novels, includes plenty of Fitzgeraldian touches but wisely avoids imitation. *Fonseca* never really feels like a Fitzgerald book; at times it’s more like a mid-nineteenth-century novel—say, an exoticized cross between *Villette* and *Great Expectations*: secrets and simmering hostilities, an inheritance plot, moldering old women in a “shuttered mansion,” and an observant protagonist in a foreign land who’s at once cautious and headstrong.

As a novel about a well-known writer, *Fonseca* has a clear affinity with *The Blue Flower*, Fitzgerald’s novel about the German Romantic poet Novalis. An intensely private person, she may have been horrified to see the epigraph from that book, Novalis’s statement that “novels arise out of the shortcomings of history,” applied to herself. But the Mexico trip is an excellent subject for speculation. Kane sets out to answer Lee’s question—What was she doing there?—and to solve the great puzzle of Fitzgerald’s life: With a brilliant undergraduate degree from Oxford, accomplished early work as a critic, and ambitions to write literary fiction, why did she not publish anything between 1953 and 1975 besides the text of an illustrated children’s serial called *Jassy of Juniper Farm* and an introduction to a posthumous collection of verse by her father, Edmund “Evoe” Knox, a comic poet and the editor of *Punch*?

The obvious answer is that she had to care and provide for her three children and her husband, who struggled with alcoholism, and that she wasn’t able to return to writing until after her kids had grown up. Fitzgerald herself claimed, not very believably, that she started writing her first novel, the satirical mystery *The Golden Child*, merely to entertain Desmond during the illness that led to his death in 1976. Kane looks for a deeper explanation. “I think every period makes sacrifices of a certain kind of person,” Fitzgerald said. “I am drawn to people who seem to have been born defeated, or even profoundly lost.” *Fonseca* proposes that the Mexico journey plunged Fitzgerald into doubt about her ability to achieve lifelong goals and that the winter of 1952 was when she came to see herself as one of the lost and defeated—an “exterminatee,” as she later called her characters.

In the early 1950s she was worrying about money, possibly more than she ever had. Her upbringing in the formidably accomplished but middle-class Knox family likely led her to believe that even if an intellectual career wouldn’t make her rich, it would provide for her needs. Her uncle Dillwyn was a Cambridge papyrologist who during both world wars worked as a military codebreaker. Ronald, another uncle, was a Roman Catholic monsignor, prominent writer, and Oxford chaplain. A third uncle, Wilfred, was an Anglo-Catholic priest who took a vow of poverty and became a chaplain and fellow at Cambridge. “All of them,” Kane writes, “right back to her bishop grandfathers”—Edmund Knox, the bishop of Manchester, and Edward Hicks, the bishop of Lincoln—“distrusted wealth. But bishops and professors were given housing.”

It must have been a shock to Fitzgerald that she encountered financial difficulties despite having a job her family would have approved of. In August 1950she and Desmond took over *World Review*, a small British monthly. (Desmond had trained as a lawyer, was a decorated officer in World War II, and in 1949 published a history of his regiment.) During their brief editorship, they turned *World Review* into an ideal little magazine: wide ranging, cosmopolitan, and alive to the major issues of their time. In addition to covering global affairs, they published essays and dispatches by Albert Camus, Bertrand Russell, and Patrick Leigh Fermor; critical considerations of Bertolt Brecht, Alfred Jarry, and Francis Bacon; and stories by Muriel Spark, J.D. Salinger, Alberto Moravia, and William Saroyan. Stevie Smith, obstreperous and unpredictable, reviewed new novels in the back of the book. But *World Review* didn’t sell, and by late 1952 it was struggling to stay afloat. So were Fitzgerald and Desmond as they tried to raise their young children on a dwindling income.

It remains unclear whether Fitzgerald and her son were actually summoned to Mexico with the prospect of an inheritance. Throughout *Fonseca* Kane inserts emails from Valpy and Tina in answer to her questions, and Valpy confirms for her—as he seems not to have for Lee—that he and his mother stayed at a Saltillo mansion owned by three (not two) old women named Purcell (not Delaney). Valpy tells Kane he researched the Purcell estate and found that “there were direct heirs,” taking this to mean that “the inheritance does not appear to have been an issue at all.” The trip, then, may have simply been a way for Fitzgerald to get a break from pressures at work and home. (Kane also suggests that she went to Mexico with the hope of gathering material for fiction, possibly out of professional jealousy of Spark, who had just won a prize for a story based on her experiences in Africa.) Nevertheless, *Fonseca* sticks closely to the account Fitzgerald gives in “Following the Plot.”

Kane’s Penelope expects the matter of the inheritance to be straightforward, the visit a formality. But things go wrong as soon as she and Valpy arrive in Fonseca (Saltillo in all but name), knock on the Delaneys’ door, and learn that Chela, the sisters’ housekeeper, expected just one guest. Chela bluntly tells Penelope to take a bus back to America, but Penelope convinces her that the young boy can’t be left alone.

There’s something otherworldly about the house, which Kane calls Mirando. The two Delaney women—the widow Doña Anita and her sister-in-law, Doña Elena—only ever leave it for Sunday Mass, and until the church doors close behind them the neighborhood birds fall silent. The northeast corner, where Doña Anita’s husband died, has a constant chill. The gothic aspects of *Fonseca* strike the right note. Fitzgerald loved ghost stories and believed in the existence of the supernatural. (She always maintained that the “rapper” in *The Bookshop*—who makes banging noises at unpredictable times—was based on a real poltergeist, and she portrays it as if it were just another nuisance, like a neighbor’s radio played too loudly.)

Most evenings the Delaneys host a cocktail hour, attended by Fonseca’s respectable Mexican residents and members of the city’s English-speaking colony. The doñas know these visitors only want their money, but they enjoy having an audience as they drunkenly squabble over the Victrola and play the same maudlin songs again and again. The guests compete for the legacy by proposing grand philanthropic projects. Señor Molina suggests establishing a fleet of bookmobiles. Señor Garza hopes to turn his small collection of taxidermy into a national bird museum. Mrs. Clancy wants to expand her community outreach in surrounding mountain villages. Mrs. Slater plans to turn Mirando itself into an arts center.

With the exception of the sincere Señor Garza, the schemers are clearly motivated not by goodwill but by greed and a selfish desire “to shape Fonseca into something they liked better.” Penelope thinks of these hangers-on as “Pretenders,” a group in which she includes herself. Even though Valpy is the one being considered for the inheritance, she views it as a potential cash infusion for *World Review*. She’s so concerned about her finances, Kane writes, that “she dreamed of money every night. In fact…it was not uncommon for her thoughts to take the form of an interview with a bank manager.” Yet with so many buzzards circling the old women, she fears she doesn’t stand a chance. When the other Pretenders challenge her to say what she would put the money into, her answer—a literary magazine—is met with laughter.

Penelope has other things on her mind besides the legacy. Before she left England, Desmond had been drinking heavily, and while in Mexico she learns from a friend that he had to be carried home from a pub two nights in a row. In letters to Penelope, Desmond asks for advice on matters related to *World Review* and proposes schemes for cutting back: having his first drink ten minutes later every evening, limiting himself to one a night, sticking to Guinness. Predictably, none of these resolutions works.

The people at Mirando have heard about, or guessed at, her marital troubles, and to prevent more rumors from spreading, she tries to hide her pregnancy. She begins a clandestine flirtation with another Pretender—a man calling himself Ernest Delaney and claiming to be related to the doñas—in part to even out the distribution of culpability in her marriage and to turn her anger at Desmond on herself. She keeps busy by writing an article about Mexican muralists, which she assigned herself for *World Review*, and working on a fictionalized story about Mirando, hoping it will help her better understand the strange situation she finds herself in.

Remarkably, traveling with a young boy may be the easiest part of Penelope’s journey. Valpy is a “delightful child”: precocious, friendly, “kind and observant, quiet.” The transatlantic crossing and the long bus ride from New York to Mexico seem to have passed without any major incidents, and he thrives in Fonseca. The servants at Mirando teach him Spanish and let him help out in the kitchen and garden. He joins a local scout group that’s free of the class and racial divisions found elsewhere in the city. But a couple of months into the visit, Penelope starts to betray his trust—losing a rare silver coin that Valpy found in Mirando’s garden and that he naively believes can solve his family’s money troubles—possibly because the pregnancy makes her feel “foggy, out of it, a little irresponsible.” When he gets angry at her for offending the doñas, his composure begins to crack, and he finally seems like a realistic child.[2](#fn-2)

Kane’s most ingenious move may be her fictionalization of Edward and Jo Hopper, who happened to be in Saltillo at the same time as the Fitzgeralds. There’s no record that they ever met, but in *Fonseca* Penelope befriends Jo and marvels at the way Edward, in work and life, “edited out…humanity.” Kane’s depiction of the irascible painter measures up to the uncanny celebrity cameos in Fitzgerald’s work, including Noël Coward in *At Freddie’s* and Antonio Gramsci in *Innocence*.

Although Fitzgerald never managed a Mexico novel, she did get a few pieces of writing out of her stay there, and Kane borrows freely from them. Two were essays that appeared in the final issue of *World Review* in the spring of 1953: the article on muralists and a review of a Tate exhibition of Mexican art and culture that includes her observations of the country. She also wrote a short story, “Our Lives Are Only Lent to Us,”about a devastating fire in a Mexican mining town and the English-speaking expatriates who are oblivious to the suffering of its poor residents. Unpublished during her lifetime, “Our Lives Are Only Lent to Us” is distinct from the fiction Penelope is trying to write in *Fonseca*, but Kane lifts many names from it for her own characters, as if theywere the originals of the ones in Fitzgerald’s story. This is a clever nod to Fitzgerald’s habit in her novel manuscripts of using the real names of people on whom her characters were based.

Throughout *Fonseca* Kane drops in details from Fitzgerald’s novels, as if they had been planted in her mind in the winter of 1952 and lain dormant for decades. Mrs. Slater, with her quest to turn Mirando into an arts center, is a version of the villainous Mrs. Gamart from *The Bookshop*. Penelope’s inner conversations with a bank manager are inspired by the daydreamed magistrate who grills Nenna in *Offshore*. Desmond’s plan to push his drinking back ten minutes every night comes from an alcoholic stage actor in *At Freddie’s*.

These references are generally “securely bedded down,” as Lee describes Fitzgerald’s own knack for fully assimilating research into her characters’ worlds, but there are a few too many of them. In fictionalizing Fitzgerald, Kane set herself a difficult task, one that can’t be accomplished through an accretion of details from Fitzgerald’s work. After six or seven of these Easter eggs, they start to feel like a shortcut, a way for Kane to show familiarity with Fitzgerald without having to dig deeper into her consciousness.

Amid Kane’s attempt to flesh out Fitzgerald’s tale from “Following the Plot,” she often fails to animate Fonseca’s residents, including Penelope. There are flashes of Fitzgerald’s steely wit, as when Penelope tells Ernest Delaney, “Decision is torment for anyone with imagination.” But for the most part Penelope’s mind is oddly free of ideas, of literature, of history, of an active force sifting and sorting and analyzing information—the things that suffuse Fitzgerald’s novels and give them their vitality. It’s true that Fitzgerald says in “Following the Plot” that she was defeated by the Mexico trip, but in *Fonseca* the struggle to make sense of it is over before it begins. “Stories and facts in Fonseca were hard to separate,” Penelope thinks. We just don’t get a clear picture of her efforts to uncouple them.

Kane’s previous novel, *Rules for Visiting*, is a sprightly, occasionally essayistic book about middle-aged friendship, narrated in the first person and rarely with more than two people in a given scene. The leap from that book to *Fonseca*’s slowly paced, close third-person historical fiction with a large, noisy cast would have been difficult even without a beloved writer at its center. Kane shares with Fitzgerald a sensitivity to unusual objects and customs, the quiet vibrancy of plants and pets, the syncopations in the rhythm of daily life. Yet there’s a lack of control over detail and character in *Fonseca* that tends to overshadow Kane’s similarities to her subject, especially since control is one of Fitzgerald’s most striking qualities.

A generous reading of *Fonseca* is that its tentativeness reflects that of its main character. Perhaps rightly, Kane gives us a Fitzgerald before the rough edges have been sanded down. Because of the late start to her career, it’s believable that her younger self would still be some ways off from the hard-bitten outlook that shapes her books: all plans fail, all acts of charity do more harm than good, the only chance at happiness is having the dumb luck to be protected from reality by ingenuousness or *amour fou*. There’s plenty of hypocrisy and preening in *Fonseca*, but Fitzgerald’s matter-of-fact despair, which so distinguishes her novels from the British comedy of manners, is just starting to come into view.

What *Fonseca* gets exactly right is that, as in Fitzgerald’s books, its keynote is failure. Penelope proves inept at winning the sympathies of the Delaneys and at resolving the questions Kane raises—about the eerie mansion and its inhabitants, about the identity of Ernest Delaney, about a potentially disastrous development at the doñas’ silver mine. Penelope never does manage to unravel the yarn.

In reality, after Fitzgerald’s return to England, the failures mounted. *World Review* collapsed, Desmond was disbarred after stealing £373 (about £7,000 today) from his law firm in 1962, and in 1963 her family’s houseboat sank in the Thames. “Do you regret going?” asks Penelope’s inner bank manager at the end of *Fonseca*, implying that the Mexico trip was what set these later troubles in motion. Penelope replies, “To Fonseca? No. Anyway, Fonseca wasn’t a place…. If anything it was an idea, or a time. It was a dry well. It was certainly the end of a dream and the beginning of a long wait.”
