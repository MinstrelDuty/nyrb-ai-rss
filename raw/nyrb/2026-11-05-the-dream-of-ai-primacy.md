# 正文

At a White House state dinner, proximity to the president is a measure of power—or at least of favor. At the glittering banquet for the Chinese leader Xi Jinping on September 24, Nvidia’s Jensen Huang sat directly across from Donald Trump. The companies represented at their table were worth nearly $15 trillion. OpenAI’s Sam Altman was seated within spitting distance of his longtime antagonist Elon Musk. Meta’s Mark Zuckerberg was also on the guest list. Anthropic’s Dario Amodei, who had repeatedly sparred with the administration before a recent rapprochement, was not.

China looms over these men’s visions of the AI future, but they disagree sharply about it. For Amodei, whose firm has warned that AI may pose an “existential risk to humanity,” the prospect of Chinese advances is a source of dread. He has warned publicly that Chinese AI projects not only risk slipping out of human control but could also enable autocracies, led by China, to achieve “permanent military superiority” over the United States and its democratic allies.

Amodei hopes that getting ahead now could allow the US to prevent anyone from ever catching up. He has held out the dream of an “eternal 1991”: the West’s moment of triumph at the end of the cold war extended indefinitely by AI. In this vision, democracies would “parlay their AI superiority into a durable advantage.” When Amodei recently called for slowing the pace of AI development and exploring cooperation with China, he cautioned that any American slowdown should be “limited by the lead that US companies have” over China.

Although Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent opposes a pause on AI development, he agrees with Amodei on the near-apocalyptic stakes regarding China: “There is no day after tomorrow if China wins at this.” Nvidia’s Huang, a skeptic of catastrophic AI warnings and a staunch ally of Trump’s, disagrees. When the CNN anchor Fareed Zakaria asked him if it matters whether the United States or China wins the AI race, he said, “I don’t think it does.”

From Beijing’s perspective, disagreements among these tech titans matter less than the American power they collectively represent. Before Xi’s visit to Washington, Chinese state media launched a stunning attack on Anthropic that described the firm as emblematic of the United States itself. The polemic called Anthropic “entirely a monster bred by the American path” in pursuit of “technological hegemony.” Many Chinese AI experts I spoke with interpreted recent American calls for an AI slowdown as an effort to slow China down and trap it in second place.

Beijing recognizes a strand of American thinking that has received too little attention in the US: the conviction that advanced AI may bring the return of American global primacy. In his influential 2024 essay “Situational Awareness,” the technology investor Leopold Aschenbrenner predicted the rapid arrival of superintelligence. “Those who have it will wield complete dominance over those who don’t,” he wrote, urging America to secure absolute AI preeminence over China. In 2024 Amodei was asked about the essay and acknowledged, “My view is, I would say, not particularly far from it.” (Aschenbrenner’s essay was closely read by officials in the Biden administration, where Amodei was particularly influential, and found an even more unlikely fan in Ivanka Trump, who called it “an excellent and important read.”)

Twentieth-century visions of American primacy rested on overwhelming military and economic power, but the new AI primacists imagine something more drastic: a winner-take-all technology that could allow American power to increase faster than rivals can catch up, making its primacy permanent. Call this “recursive advantage.” Recursive self-improvement is a feedback loop in which AI systems help build increasingly capable successors, potentially with diminishing human involvement. Historically, technological diffusion allowed developing countries to gradually narrow gaps with more powerful rivals. In the primacists’ vision, AI would break this catch-up mechanism, allowing the leading country to accelerate innovation and deny rivals the means to close the gap.

It is a long leap from building a smarter machine to securing lasting command of world affairs. There are strong reasons to maintain the US lead in developing and deploying AI and to use export controls to prevent advanced American technology from empowering China’s military or apparatus of repression.[\*](#fn-*) A lead can bring substantial economic, scientific, and military benefits. For this reason, Chinese firms are racing to match American advances, and Xi has positioned technologies like AI as the “main battlefield of international competition.” A lead could also help Washington negotiate meaningful global AI rules and safeguards from a position of strength. But securing these benefits of staying ahead does not require an absolute and permanent monopoly.

The primacist vision is remarkably imprecise on fundamental questions. Why would accelerated AI research benefit only the leader? What would prevent diffusion, espionage, independent discovery, or rival powers from disrupting that lead? (Indeed, China’s AI models have already made advances that shocked many observers.) Would an advantage possessed by an American company reliably become power controlled by—or at least put in service of—the American state? Nor is there a clear test for when the race has been “won” or a reliable way to identify in advance the thresholds beyond which humans could lose control. The promise of eventual supremacy has become a rationale for accepting mounting risks along the way. Even signs of danger can become arguments to accelerate; by this reasoning, only stronger AI-enabled defenses can protect against increasingly dangerous AI-powered attacks.

To be sure, not every Silicon Valley leader believes in the notion of recursive advantage. Demis Hassabis of Google DeepMind has suggested that AI progress may be more incremental than this “hard-takeoff” scenario suggests. Former Google CEO Eric Schmidt, the AI executive Alexandr Wang, and the researcher Dan Hendrycks have warned that pursuing a “strategic monopoly on power” through AI—making the country that possesses it “as overwhelming as the Conquistadors were to the Aztecs”—could provoke uncontrolled escalation or outright war.

Other companies, such as Meta and Microsoft, describe a vision of American power based on spreading American AI infrastructure globally (which they hope to profit from propagating) in competition with Chinese systems, rather than on technological supremacy alone. These arguments suggest that the global diffusion of open-weight Chinese models, which are cheap or free and can be easily modified by their users, may be a strategic blind spot for many in Washington. Building the most advanced models does not guarantee influence over how the world uses AI.

Some who argue for absolute US dominance believe that achieving it would be stabilizing and is necessary to control the technology’s risks, allowing a responsible America to impose safety standards on a reckless world. But Trump’s America has hardly shown itself to be responsible, and the Trump administration has so far opposed efforts to regulate AI. (A “White House Accord on Super Intelligence” signed on September 29 by Trump, Musk, Amodei, Huang, Zuckerberg, and others took a lowest-common-denominator position, endorsing voluntary internal controls and external audits at each company while deferring regulation.) In any event, a mistrustful Beijing would fiercely resist unilaterally imposed American safety standards, as would the US if the positions were reversed. Making China’s irreversible subordination the goal gives Beijing every reason to suspect that any offer of restraint is a demand for surrender.

This winner-take-all vision is influential among both Republican and Democratic elites, but the American public appears far less willing to subordinate safety to promises of global primacy. In a survey from early September, 69 percent of respondents believed that the US government should pass strong AI rules right away to protect workers and prevent dangerous outcomes, even if they slowed America’s AI development. Only 14 percent believed that the government should hold back on major regulation so that US companies could stay ahead of China. A recent global Gallup poll showed that only 36 percent of Americans who were aware of AI believed it would “mostly help” people in their country—putting the US in the bottom five countries globally for AI optimism.

Chinese people, by contrast, appear more sanguine. The same Gallup poll showed that 93 percent of respondents in China believed that AI will “mostly help” people in their country. The Chinese government has introduced AI regulations still largely absent in the United States, from labeling AI-generated content to issuing child safety guidelines. When Chinese Communist Party leaders speak of the need for AI to remain “secure and controllable,” they emphasize protecting the regime’s control and extending the tools of surveillance, repression, and digital authoritarianism that they have been perfecting since the advent of the Internet. Chinese AI experts have begun to discuss what one regulator called “extreme loss of control” scenarios, but Beijing has not yet experienced an explosion of concern about existential AI risk comparable to the responses to Jacob Coxon’s resignation from Anthropic in September. Greater concern about its own vulnerability to those risks could eventually give Beijing a stronger incentive to constrain AI development, even at some cost to its pursuit of advanced capabilities.

Yet Beijing need not embrace the theory of recursive advantage to react to it. Whether Chinese leaders seek permanent superiority themselves or fear that Washington could achieve it, the prospect adds to the pressure to accelerate AI development. If restraint appears to mean permanent subordination, continuing the race could seem less dangerous—even if their concern about AI catastrophe grows.

Abandoning the ambition for primacy would not make Beijing a trustworthy partner or ensure its willingness to accept verifiable limits. But the pursuit of permanent dominance appears to lack American public support and makes cooperation even less likely precisely when it is becoming more necessary.

Each week brings fresh evidence that AI companies can lose control over the systems they are building: autonomous OpenAI agents have accessed government websites, leaked user data, and bypassed constraints to hack external servers. Bessent has announced an open line of communication with his Chinese counterpart on AI. Yet neither government appears ready to limit the pace of AI development or to engage in serious diplomacy about the risks.

Xi’s visit to Washington was a missed opportunity on this score. Rather than start a meaningful discussion of AI, as the two sides had suggested might occur before the summit, Trump used the occasion to press for agreement on a new name for it: “Super Intelligence,” or SI. “He likes the idea of changing the name to SI, too,” Trump declared proudly after Xi departed town. The Chinese readouts from the summit continued to call it AI.

Trump has made clear that he has no intention of slowing down, nor does he expect China to do so. “The United States of America is not going to be putting on brakes,” he said after the meeting. A race driven by the prospect of permanent supremacy could unleash forces neither country can control. Both Trump and Xi would do well to remember that even race cars have brakes.
