# 正文

“For any event, there is an infinite number of possible outcomes. Our choices determine which outcomes will follow. But there is a theory in quantum physics that all possibilities that can happen or could happen do happen in alternate quantum realities.” This summary of the many-worlds interpretation of quantum physics, a theory first proposed by the American physicist Hugh Everett in the 1950s, is given by a sentient android named Data serving as an officer on the Federation starship USS  *Enterprise.* Technically it’s delivered by the actor Brent Spiner, performing as Data, at a pivotal moment in “Parallels,” an episode from the seventh and final season of *Star Trek: The Next Generation*. In that episode Data’s fellow officer Worf, a Klingon, returns to the *Enterprise* after some time spent at an intergalactic sports competition, only to discover that he has passed through a hole in space-time—there’s *always* a hole in space-time—and that the reality he now inhabits is different from the one he left, in ways that are subtle and profound, comic and tragic.

Most strictly scientific formulations of the many-worlds interpretation hold that there is no practical way to move between branching quantum realities, or even to prove that these alternate realities exist, except as far-out mathematical possibilities. For *Star Trek*, getting Worf back to his home reality is as simple as flying back through that pesky space-time fissure, then sealing it with a blast from the ship’s engines.

While the theory has been the subject of intense debate within the scientific community—Stephen Hawking and Richard Feynman believed it to be likely true; the Nobel Prize–winning Dutch physicist Gerard ’t Hooft has rejected it as implausible—popular culture has run away with the idea of a multiverse in which every possible iteration of reality can coexist. In recent years, superhero movie franchises have become jittery, reality-hopping narratives of rebooting, recycling, and reincarnation, grafting the idea of infinitely branching realities onto old-fashioned tales of good guys punching bad guys. Setting your stories within a multiverse creates the opportunity for endless marketable derivatives: if a beloved character is killed in one film, you simply open up an inter-universal portal and let them stroll back in during the next.

On a less mercenary, more personal level, exploring alternate realities is a deeply human urge. Most of us, especially in moments of stress or depression, picture other careers, other partners, other selves, often looking for the decisions that allowed our current circumstances to come into being. We don’t always do this in a posture of regret: sometimes we want to confirm the thesis that the life we are currently living is, in fact, the best of all possible lives. Or, at least, not one of the worst.

*How It Works Out*, a raunchy, hilarious, and occasionally unsettling debut novel in stories by the Canadian writer Myriam Lacroix, is filled with exactly these kinds of speculation. The book is a literary cabinet of curiosities, from which spill alternate realities in ways that are as thunderingly literal and self-consciously silly as any episode of *Star Trek* but that are also clever, surprising, and deeply felt.

Lacroix grew up in Montreal and now lives in Vancouver with her partner, Allison. That kind of personal detail may seem irrelevant in a book review, but it’s important for understanding Lacroix’s debut, in which the stories are linked by the names of their main characters: Myriam and Allison, who are presented as different people in each tale but who often share biographical facts with the writer and her real-life partner. (How much any of these fictional Myriams and Allisons resemble the actual ones we can only guess and, in the case of the book’s darker stories, hope that the answer is “not much.”)

To describe what happens in *How It Works Out* is to risk getting lost in an appellative tangle. For example, there is the real-life Myriam Lacroix, the one who is the author of the book, and then there is the Myriam Lacroix of “The Sequel,” the book’s fifth story. *That* Myriam is a mentally troubled former barista who achieved enormous cultural, critical, and financial success with a book called *How It Works Out: Building a Healthy Lesbian Relationship in the Patriarchy*, which she cowrote with her partner, Allison. This fictional version of *How It Works Out* is uncompromisingly political. “Even lesbians who succeed at marriage ultimately fail,” the book asserts, “for they have succeeded only in infiltrating an institution that hates them.”

The book was a best seller, we learn, with copies “on every queer woman’s shelf.” But now, a decade after its publication, sales have evaporated. The culture has shifted; those queer women’s bookshelves have been cleared to make room for even more uncompromising manifestos. “The new generation doesn’t find us radical enough,” Allison, the story’s narrator, tells us. “Nobody cares if a couple of Canadian lesbians have a happy ending.” Their agent, Steve, however, has a plan that involves the kind of rebranding attempted by many C-list celebrities in the era of Trump. Allison sums it up:

> If we can’t win over the queer feminists, we might have a chance with the right-wing lesbians, the gated-community dykes. They’re the ones with disposable income, anyway. First step was to get married, get the media’s attention, show publishers the names Myriam and Allison still mean something.

At the moment when Allison says “I do” in a lavish ceremony at a luxe hotel in the mountains, she has trouble staying focused, given all that is happening to make the event go viral: “I’m distracted by the *Elle Canada* photographer, whose finger is going at the shutter button like she’s trying to get it off. Steve is typing like crazy on his phone, live-tweeting.” Allison is the more fame-skeptical and emotionally grounded of the pair, but she’s not above a bit of clickbaiting of her own: when she kisses her new bride, she slips in “some tongue for the press,” then takes care to smile in a way that shows off her “crooked canine, which some internet forums agree is my best attribute.”

Steve’s rebranding strategy appears, at first, to go well: the actor Kristen Stewart retweets one of the wedding photos; #MAwedding and #howitworksout start trending. But then Myriam, who suffers from extreme fits of paranoia and fear, becomes ill after eating a salad, and Allison begins an intense affair with the doctor who treats her. The story then jumps ahead in time, positing a future in which Myriam is out of the picture and Allison has achieved fame with her second wife, the doctor, with whom she has had a baby. The story ends with the happy couple dancing on set with Ellen DeGeneres before a live studio audience, as confetti falls from the ceiling.

The satire in “The Sequel” is as dark as it is meta. (And it is *very* meta: the real-life Canadian lesbian sister pop duo Tegan and Sara, who blurbed the actual Lacroix’s actual book, make a cameo in the story as the kind of bored, cynical celebrities who get paid to hang out at weddings.) The gags are so sharp, it’s possible to miss the more thoughtful story happening behind them. Lacroix (the real-life one) wants to do more than merely skewer the most obvious excesses of 2024-era clout chasing. The Allison character, though she is a willing partner in the rebranding, mostly goes along with it because she is fighting to stay close to Myriam, whose illnesses and obsessions threaten to dissolve all the joy left in their relationship. Remove the cultural fame, the agent, and the celebrity wedding, and she is any partner attempting Hail Mary passes to find their way back to what they had before, to fly the spaceship back through the hole in space-time.

Given that Lacroix is a relative unknown in the literary world, the sheer audacity of building her first book from funhouse-mirror distortions of her own life is impressive. Imagine Nicolas Cage playing up his own outsize persona in a film like *The Unbearable Weight of Massive Talent* (2022) before having starred in anything else. *How It Works Out* could have easily turned out a pointless, self-indulgent mess. But Lacroix presents her outrageous scenarios as settled facts, without any special pleading or giving the reader an opportunity for doubt. The opening of the book’s first story, “The Meaning of Life,” contains all these strengths in compressed form:

> They’d planned on getting beer from Toby’s, but instead they got a baby, and they were not unhappy about it. Myriam and Allison hadn’t talked about kids yet, but finding one seemed to be a sign that it was time. Besides, being lesbians, this spared them the trouble of adopting or getting one of their friends to have sex with them.

Here, as she does throughout the book, Lacroix writes with a mixture of emotional coolness and disarming jokiness. The tossed-off gags seem to signal, “Hey, everything’s OK here, reader,” when it quickly becomes apparent that everything is not: the comic shrug in that last line allows us to accept the freakish behavior that follows without the sense of judgment that might be prompted by a more dour literary reality.

In this version of the Myriam–Allison relationship, the pair are the kind of hip, young urbanites who spot an old couch and immediately wonder “if they could turn it into some sort of art installation.” They are also the kind of people who, having found a baby in an alleyway, decide to take it home and name it Jonah, because “they had no idea what Jonahs were like, and they liked the element of surprise.” Taking care of a baby challenges and deepens their understanding of the world. “Nuclear families were a concept made up by the same people who wanted you to get full-time jobs or sell your art for money,” Myriam thinks. “Still, when she lifted Jonah from Allison’s heaving chest and held him in her arms, she could feel the exact dimensions of her happiness, its weight and humid breath. It wasn’t a dream.”

It turns out that Baby Jonah belongs to Dina, an unhappy woman who lost track of her kid while her husband, Ken, was getting beaten up for obnoxiously heckling a comedian. The loss of her child causes the already miserable Dina to crash emotionally, but also brings her newfound clarity: “Realizing that she felt like shit was a bit better than feeling like shit but being too dissociated to realize it. Now, if she started to punch her steering wheel, she knew why she was punching it.”

The Myriam–Allison and Dina–Ken pairings in the story are stereotypes, if not quite caricatures. (Ken, true to clichéd form, is a foulmouthed and lazy brute who sits on his basement-apartment couch spouting homophobic curses while watching hockey; the presence of a stained undershirt is not explicitly mentioned, but it can be inferred.) This flattening of character is intentional: the story, despite its moments of violence, plays out like a fable, with Dina deciding to leave Ken and join the girls in raising Jonah. These people inhabit a gauzy fantasy world, which belies the fact that the story is about a young couple kidnapping an abandoned baby.

The soft focus of that first story works as a setup for the nightmare of the next, “Love Bun,” in which Myriam is a depressed postgraduate being supported by Allison as she struggles to complete a “thesis on trauma, and the cycle of abuse, in the works of certain realist writers.” After a cooking accident, Myriam discovers that eating a small slice of Allison’s flesh makes her feel “giddy and light, like I’d been pumped full of helium.” It also sharpens other appetites: “Three orgasms later, I ate an entire bowl of penne primavera.”

Shortly after that incident, Allison loses a finger in an ice-skating mishap, and Myriam does the unthinkable: she keeps the finger and eats it raw. (It has a “tangy umami flavor.”) The effect is immediate:

> It felt like emerging from some sort of primordial glue. I could smell the flowers under the snow, waiting to be born. An elderly woman smiled and I could see behind her teeth, into her throat, down her esophagus, right inside the pouch of her stomach, where love was brewing like a dark green potion. Love was everywhere. Rows of shops sprung out of the sidewalk like pages from a pop-up book, bricks so bright with their own light I could hardly look.

It gets worse. Myriam and Allison decide to have children, whom, with a grim sense of inevitability, they eat—only taking tiny nibbles of some of them, consuming others whole. The cannibalistic descriptions in the story are vivid, difficult to read, and visceral (literally so). It is here that Lacroix’s approach most resembles the horror-infused literary parables often associated with writers like Carmen Maria Machado (*Her Body and Other Parties*), Kristen Roupenian (*Cat Person and Other Stories*), and Rachel Yoder (*Nightbitch*). Lacroix does not spare the violence or the blood, but even as the horrors of “Love Bun” are compounding, she keeps the jokes coming: the depraved couple name their children things like Framboise and Kale. When their firstborn, Jonah—Lacroix’s book is lousy with callbacks—gets a life sentence for eating a male friend, Allison blames the verdict on homophobia. (These stories, incidentally, serve as an excellent corrective to the claims that “wokeism” has taken all the edge out of comedy: *How It Works Out* is uncompromising and exact in its targeting of people on all sides of various culture wars, and yet manages never to punch down.)

“Love Bun” represents the most extreme and horrifying variation on Myriam–Allison. (The oddest is “Mantis,” in which Allison is a pet dog, Myriam a pet mantis in a terrarium.) The levels of surrealism change from story to story, though in almost all of them reality feels both heightened and warped. The interpersonal dynamics shift, too: sometimes Myriam is the villain, sometimes it’s Allison, sometimes both. The one constant is that at least one of the partners yearns for the other. Even in its strangest scenarios, the Myriams and Allisons of *How It Works Out* exhibit the most basic of needs, the need for love.

The answers to whether any of these variants actually *fulfill* this need range from “of course” to “of course not,” but even in the book’s darkest moments, there is no doubt that love is worth struggling for. This romantic core, though often buried under much cultural and personal rubble, is what distinguishes Lacroix’s book from the more pessimistic visions of Roupenian et al. These are dark stories, but they have an undeniably sentimental heart, which keeps them from feeling like too clever experiments. Even when the Myriams and Allisons are presented as monsters, we somehow want them to be together, and feel genuine sadness when they can’t manage it. As for the fate of the actual Myriam and Allison, that is somewhat spoiled by the book’s dedication page: “To Allison, I figured it out. I know how it works out.”
