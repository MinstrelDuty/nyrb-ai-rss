# 正文

The Italian novelist Fausta Cialente once wrote that she found it impossible to move her characters around a room unless she had furnished it first, down to the smallest details. This was in the late 1930s, when she was about forty and had been living in Egypt, with her Italian husband and daughter, for nearly half her life. Her fiction is filled with a precise and intimate sense of place, complete with colors, smells, and sounds, the textures of buildings and rooms, the weather’s cycles and extremes, all weighing on the characters, shaping their decisions and behavior. Nowhere is the feeling of immediacy that this creates stronger than in her 1966 novel *A Very Cold Winter*, which was published in English for the first time this year.

Cialente is one of a small group of twentieth-century Italian women writers—along with Alba de Céspedes, Sibilla Aleramo, and a few others—recently rediscovered by the Italian literary world, where they are regarded as feminists ahead of their time. During her long life she published six novels, two novellas, a collection of short stories, and a lot of journalism, yet until now only one of her novels, *The Levantines*, had been translated into English. She enjoyed bursts of celebrity and praise, followed by periods of profound neglect, with long silences between books, and had a tendency to postpone manuscript delivery dates and change publishers (which may explain why she is not better known).

Most of Cialente’s characters are women and young girls who are rootless, uncertain about their identities, and searching for ways to understand the complexities of the world around them. They are highly critical of themselves and others, but not unaware of frailty. Few find happiness. Many lose the people they love, including children. They are addicted to flight.

In her restless tales of self-discovery, Cialente borrows repeatedly from her own life, using her real experiences, barely transposed. Born in 1898 in Cagliari, on the island of Sardinia, she grew up fascinated by the conversations of adults. Her early childhood was spent in Trieste during the declining years of the Austro-Hungarian Empire with her parents and her older brother, Renato, to whom she was devoted.

Her mother, Elsa—one of four daughters from a wealthy Trieste family—was, unusually for her class and time, a semiprofessional singer with a fine soprano voice. All her life Cialente would remember her mother’s exotic sisters and the charms of prosperous, cosmopolitan, sparkling Trieste, when the city was full of refugees and travelers speaking a dozen different languages. The past was a vast, intricate jigsaw puzzle, and she was always trying to reconstruct, piece by piece, a world she could understand, always conscious that there was much she was missing.

Cialente’s father, Alfredo, was a cavalry officer—volatile, choleric, and constantly being posted to new places. The family’s frequent moves instilled in her a lifelong fear of departures and of never belonging. Before Italy entered World War I, Alfredo had resigned from the army due to ill health and embarked on a series of business disasters and adulterous affairs. Elsa took on music students, and Cialente was sent to study in Genoa. In the closing months of the war, while Cialente obsessively followed the ruinous defeats of the Italian forces, her cousin Fabio was killed.

In 1920, as Mussolini was jostling for power in the postwar chaos, Cialente met Enrico Terni, a small, bony Jewish man with a shiny bald pate, described affectionately by his friends as looking like Gabriele D’Annunzio and walking like Groucho Marx. Terni was twice her age and the father of two almost grown-up sons. She married him in 1921 and moved to Alexandria, where his family had lived for three generations. Numerous Italian laborers had immigrated to Egypt in the late nineteenth century to escape poverty, some by working on the Suez Canal. Many of them then stayed, forming a lively and artistic community. They set up enclaves, opened schools, and built palatial Moorish and Art Deco houses.

Marrying Terni fed Cialente’s “impulse to flee, to leave, to go away,” she later explained. It was, she said, an “autentica fuga,” a genuine flight from a despotic and unreliable father and a deeply troubled family. But having watched her parents fight, she had few illusions about marriage and especially about men, too often “unimpressive, quarrelsome and annoying ghosts.” Her relationship with Terni seems to have nonetheless been companionable, if not intimate, though she later remarked on his “aggressive ways and presumption.”

Terni worked for a branch of the Banco di Roma in Alexandria, but he was also a gifted musician, and the couple held regular musical soirees. He was a great reader (and a friend of E.M. Forster’s), and in his large and rambling house Cialente discovered an unexpected world of books. She began to read her way through the French and English classics, delighting too in Gide, Mann, Conrad, and Kafka and devouring each new volume of *À la recherche du temps perdu* as it appeared. Later she would say that she had read it six times.

Cialente was intrigued by the cosmopolitan world of Egypt in which she found herself. She had brought with her a pronounced dislike of the nationalism she had seen spreading in Italy. She also felt a growing distaste for the many expatriates who treated Egyptians with disdain and a sense of racist superiority. The colonial life of the Middle East—*levantinismo*—appalled her. Additionally the family she had married into was Jewish, as were many of the Italians who had settled in Cairo and Alexandria, which increased her ever-present feeling of being different, “foreign and distanced.”

As a girl Cialente had written stories. By entering what she called “the prickly garden of invention,” she was able to fill the void left both by the lack of a proper education and by the fact that, having moved so often, she had made few real friends. Though none of these stories survives, she admitted that at least one explored a theme that remained dear to her: how human beings are “eventually irredeemably alone.”

In 1923 Cialente gave birth to her only child, Lionella. When her daughter was two, Cialente began work on her first novel. *Natalia* is set in a provincial Italian city in the aftermath of World War I. The eponymous heroine—the first of her inquiring, liberated women—is bold, more interested in exploring her own feelings than in following convention, and at the heart of the book lies the very physical attraction she feels for another woman.

Published in 1930, *Natalia* sold three thousand copies and was translated into French before it fell afoul of Mussolini’s censors. They demanded that she change her description of the Battle of Caporetto to a “retreat” rather than a “defeat” and also remove passages about the book’s central lesbian friendship. Cialente refused, and *Natalia* was banned. When it was reprinted after the war, some of the more explicitly homoerotic scenes were omitted.[1](#fn-1)

Meanwhile she started work on *Cortile a Cleopatra* (Courtyard in Cleopatra), named after a working-class suburb of Alexandria that was home to many Arabs. The hero, unusually for Cialente’s novels, is a boy, the beautiful and rebellious twenty-year-old Marco. Like her, he is an outsider, having come to Egypt in search of his Greek mother, and he shares Cialente’s own complicated feelings about being privileged while many Egyptians live in poverty in their home country.

*Cortile a Cleopatra* made little stir when it was published in Italy in 1936. Its sympathetic account of ordinary Egyptian shopkeepers and servants and their feelings was distinctly at odds with the prevailing taste for Eastern exoticism and its depictions of cruel Bedouins, primitive Africans, and heroic young fascists. However, the book, along with a number of short stories she wrote for *Il Giornale d’Oriente*, the newspaper that served Egypt’s Italian population at the time, established her as a *femme de lettres* in Egyptian society, and when it was republished after World War II it brought her many new readers in an Italy whose tastes had changed.

In 1940, as Italy drifted toward war, the Terni house became a center for militant antifascists. The family had been in the habit of returning to Europe for the summer, but they gave it up, and Terni and Cialente began compiling reports from foreign papers that described the violence and persecution in Italy. They circulated the reports among the Italian communities in Alexandria, Port Said, and Cairo to counter fascist propaganda.

Egypt was a British protectorate from 1914 until 1922, and in 1936 the Anglo-Egyptian Treaty ended British military occupation in the country, though it made an exception for up to 10,000 British troops and 400 Royal Air Force pilots to be stationed in the Suez Canal Zone. Egypt remained strategically important for Allied military operations, and thousands of British troops were deployed there during the war. In July 1940 the Special Operations Executive, Britain’s wartime spy organization, began to look for ways to recruit agents and spread antifascist and anti-Nazi propaganda. In October Cialente was summoned to Cairo to work on a radio program that was broadcast to Italian prisoners of war who had been captured in the Libyan desert, with a view to reeducating a generation of young Italians who had known nothing but fascism. She agreed—on the condition that the program consist of Italians talking to Italians rather than parroting British opinions—even though it meant leaving her seventeen-year-old daughter and her husband in Alexandria for the foreseeable future.

Cialente lived in Cairo with a friend whose lushly decorated house later provided details for her many vivid interiors: in her novels flowers and plants are often minutely described, every room has a view, pianos are gleaming and lustrous, and cupboards smell of lavender and iris. Cialente loved the turbulence of the city, with its adventurers, spies, and refugees from all over German-occupied Europe, seeking deals, providing information, trying to reinvent themselves. She had a special fondness for the Greek exiles, finding them humorous and dignified. Many of her colleagues were Communists whose views she largely shared, but she maintained the nonpartisan tenor of the radio program, editing the news bulletins and often broadcasting them herself twice a day.

Nevertheless, in 1943 she parted ways with the British. They had begun to find her somewhat too far to the left, while she mistrusted their political certainties and felt the need to start planning for a different postwar world. Together with Italian colleagues she started a magazine, *Fronte Unito*, intended for the ever-growing number of Italian prisoners in Allied camps across the Middle East and North Africa. Though it ran until the end of the war, she found herself buffeted by the quarrelsome antifascists jostling for position.

Cialente stopped writing fiction and instead kept a war diary, a “journal of working and fighting.” She filled it with commentary on the progress of the fighting in the desert and with portraits, some of them sharp, of the people who passed through the Middle East; she noted with contempt the “decidedly pusillanimous” men sent out by Mussolini. She seems to have regarded her diary primarily as a historical and political record, and she wrote it soberly, without embellishments, occasionally adding her own views on fascism but otherwise steering clear of politics.

With the end of the war, she gave it up. Her wartime experiences had made her into a person she had never imagined herself to be, with a “cunning, arrogance, capacity for intrigue and aggression” that had not been part of her nature before. The war diary ran to more than 1,700 pages, and though she talked about publishing it she never got around to it.[2](#fn-2) The diary had been for her a “necessary activity,” but one that she felt had wasted her talents. These, she said, lay in telling stories, inventing and creating characters out of nothing—“that is something worthwhile.”

In 1946, having been away from Italy for seven years, Cialente returned to see her mother, who was now living in Milan. She found the city derelict, full of bombed-out houses, rubble, and potholes. That winter was exceptionally bitter. Snow fell early, especially in northern Italy, bringing arctic winds from the north, and in the unheated houses people froze. Electricity was erratic and wood hard to come by. The end of the war, which might have brought relief and hope to the devastated Italians, had in fact come with an outbreak of violence, hatred, and much score settling with the fascists.

It was to this setting that she returned, some years later, in *A Very Cold Winter*.The novel opens in mid-November, a “season as bitter and disagreeable as a sour apple.” Camilla, a woman in early middle age who has been deserted by her husband, Dario, is living in a frigid attic in a Milanese apartment block with her three children. The younger two, Lalla and Guido, are still in school, but the just-grown-up Alba already has a job. With them are Regina, who has had a baby out of wedlock with Camilla’s nephew Nicola, who was killed in the last weeks of the war; another nephew, Arrigo, and his wife, Milena, who are somewhat lazy and insist on speaking in French; and Enzo, a homeless young anarchist raised in Egypt whom Camilla has taken in.

The attic’s inhabitants have partitioned it with curtains, but the lack of privacy and the intense cold make them fractious:

> The night before, they’d all argued around the table that was now barely visible in the makeshift dining room, right by the front door (in fact, if a visitor came in while they were eating, he’d practically fall face-first into the dinner plates)…. It was an unpleasant conversation about how they’d survive that lousy, wretched winter—as if one of them…were solely to blame for all their troubles, as if they were the only family in all of Milan having a hard time!

Camilla, who spends her days battling to keep the household fed, calls her establishment an “albergo dei poveri,” a poorhouse. Apart from occasional visits to her bad-tempered mother, who lives outside the city, the family is nearly always inside these bleak, overcrowded rooms. Everything, writes Cialente, “was buried beneath the frozen snow, a dark fog enveloped the hills, crows and blackbirds hovered somberly over the skeletal trees, resting now and then on the dirty white ground.”

The narrative moves from character to character, following their thoughts and memories and expectations. Camilla cannot imagine that the future could be very happy. Regina succumbs to feelings of inadequacy and self-doubt, a trait common to many of Cialente’s women. “I’m like one of those wrecked buildings,” she reflects, “with the façade still standing, but empty inside.” Fourteen-year-old Lalla dreams of becoming a writer. She will “forever be a girl,” Camilla thinks, “in search of adventure, never afraid of getting lost in the woods. She has a light inside her.”

All suffer from claustrophobia, but none more so than Alba, who yearns for luxury and imagines that she might get it by taking up a colleague’s suggestion to find a wealthy man to keep her. Meanwhile Enzo, not quite Levantine and not quite Italian, speaks and thinks in a jumble of languages, worrying that his Middle Eastern upbringing has made him a foreigner, a stranger, wherever he goes. Enzo’s memories include what Cialente loved about Egypt: the warm smell of salt from the shimmering waters of the nearby ocean, the scent of jasmine in the early mornings, the violets and reds of the setting sun—“a dream, to remember it now in that dreadful Milanese cold.”

When tragedy strikes, it comes as an event that had earlier struck Cialente’s own family. Alba, having disappeared with a rich lover, is killed in the fog by a passing car just as she is returning home. (Renato, Cialente’s older brother, had been killed in Rome in November 1943 by a Nazi truck while coming home late one night from the theater where he was acting in a play by Gorky.) Devastated, Camilla doubts whether she can survive in the drafty attic, with the wind howling around the windows, while Guido and Lalla look like “two caged owls with uncombed hair and wide, bewildered eyes.” But with spring come the first fragile shoots of a future for herself and for the others. “Come what may,” she thinks to herself, “she’d never turn into an icy stone at the bottom of a stagnant pond.”

*A Very Cold* *Winter* is full of rich details, and the physical world is described with loving, yet sometimes dark, precision. Among the pleasures of the original Italian are Cialente’s immensely long sentences, with each thought and detail built on another, as if she herself were standing there noting them down one by one. Her work has proved hard to translate, but apart from a small number of jarring colloquialisms that ring with too modern an air—“go figure” for *figurati*, a “piece of work” for *fatta a* *modo suo*—and the shortening of Cialente’s sentences, which sometimes alters the long flow of her thoughts, Julia Nelsen’s translation serves her well.

At the end of the war Cialente was undecided about whether to remain in Egypt or move to Italy to join her mother and daughter, whose husband worked for the British Council. She spent a few months in Europe, and when she returned to Alexandria, she found herself at odds with the new Egypt. The expatriate world of the 1930s that she had thrived in and written about had disappeared, the foreigners returned to their own countries; most of her friends were dead or had gone home; and the country’s postwar politics felt alien to her. After two and a half decades of marriage, she and Terni had drifted apart. Her mother, now a widow, joined her in a flat in Rome, an arrangement that they both enjoyed. With her family, wrote Cialente, she could escape for a little while the “disorder” of life, its “shamefulness and inhumanity.”

Cialente considered starting a women’s magazine, much like the French *Marie Claire*, thinking that she could act as editor and occasional contributor, but funding could not be found, and it came to nothing. She wrote articles for *l’Unita*, mentored younger women writers and helped them find publishers, and turned her hand to short stories, several of them about children because, as she said, she was always interested in children who were refractory to the hypocrisy of adults. Cialente and her mother spent seven years living together, and when apart they continued to exchange letters about their lives.

Elsa asked that her daughter’s letters from Egypt be buried with her, and when she died in 1955, this was done. After her mother’s funeral, Cialente left Rome and divided her time between a new family house at Cocquio-Trevisago in the north of Italy and the various overseas postings where her daughter lived with her husband and their two daughters. In a life marked by movement and change, what remained constant was her closeness, first to her mother and then to her only child.

Cialente was not quite done with Egypt in her writing, though what interested her now was the past, the mix of people she had so enjoyed, the Armenians and Greeks, the Jews and Christians, the courtesans and spies, with their gossipy conversation and hidden meanings. Some years before *A Very Cold Winter*, Cialente wrote *Ballata levantina*, which follows a young orphan, Daniela, who is sent to live with her grandmother in Egypt in the years between the late nineteenth century and World War II. Daniela is her perfect heroine, a girl who loses her innocence as she confronts her family’s past, specifically that of her grandmother, who lived with a married man and whose secrets fill her with shame, “a squalid, ugly feeling.”

Cialente told an interviewer that of all her books, this was the one most built out of her own memories, pared down to the essential. Daniela is the child she herself was, listening and trying to understand the world in a “cavalcata furiosa,” a frenzied rush of thoughts. One of Cialente’s particular talents as a writer is her ability to convey character with a few small images. Daniela keeps asking the women who gather around her grandmother to tell her whom she most resembles. Not her beautiful grandmother, they say, or her mother,

> who wasn’t beautiful, but the moment they mentioned her they all began to say how much they pitied her because she had died young…and they praised her sweet nature with emphatic tones, kissing their fingertips and making gestures in the air.

*Ballata levantina* was translated into French, German, and English—as *The Levantines*—and lost Italy’s prestigious Strega Prize by just one vote. With its cast of lively characters against a background of war and emigration, it established Cialente as the foremost chronicler of the end of empire in the Middle East. In the 1960s, with the publication of *A Very Cold Winter*, her reputation grew. And then in 1972 she published *Il vento sulla sabbia* (The Wind Over the Sand), set in Egypt and elsewhere in North Africa, which formed a trilogy with *Cortile a Cleopatra* and *Ballata levantina*.

Cialente never lost the habit of furnishing her fictitious rooms and bringing her characters to life within them. She did this to great effect in *Le quattro ragazze Wieselberger* (The Four Wieselberger Girls, 1976)—the richest and most ambitious of her novels, and perhaps the most admired. She called it a novel, but of everything she wrote it comes the closest to a family memoir*.* Starting with the history of her mother’s irredentist family in Trieste, where her rich and musical grandfather brought up his four daughters, it traces the lives of the beautiful Adele, so dazzling in “her maroon coloured hat…under a frothy cloud of tulle fluttering over its crown” that she drives suitors away; the difficult and moody Alba; the biddable Alice, who marries a man with a severe tic; and Elsa, a stand-in for Cialente’s own long-suffering mother.

History in this portrait of Trieste, Cialente wrote, might appear at first glance to vacillate between ordinary exaggerations and mistakes. But once you looked at it closely you saw that it was really made up of “cynical, dirty interests, of cruelty and hidden lies,” of the “baseness of racism, the intolerance of fanaticism.” Much of the story is told by Elsa’s daughter, who, like Cialente herself, is a passionate reader, often at odds with those around her, mourning the lost world of her Trieste childhood and escaping into storytelling. “I no longer think about the remote disorder of life to which I must return, however inhuman, shameful and impossible it is,” she muses to herself. “I have silently accepted what seems to me to be the promise of a meager happiness, but that too is remote.” Cialente’s weaving together of past and present, thoughts and memories, made this her most Proustian novel.

*Le* *quattro ragazze Wieselberger* sold 200,000 copies, and at seventy-eight Cialente became, at the time, the oldest recipient of the Strega Prize. It was her last book. After that she translated Henry James, Louisa May Alcott, and *Clea*, the fourth volume of Lawrence Durrell’s *Alexandria Quartet*, a decidedly more florid and turbulent account of Egypt in the closing years of empire than her own. She died in Pangbourne, England, where her daughter’s family had settled. Elegant and frail, seeming to belong to another age, she had long stopped writing. As she said in an interview with *Le Monde*, “When you have told the story of your own family, your own life, perhaps there is nothing left to say.”
