# 正文

PLAYBOY: You’ve often said that you believe very strongly in fascism. Yet you also claim you’ll one day run for Prime Minister of England. More media manipulation? BOWIE: Christ, everything is a media manipulation. I’d love to enter politics. I will one day. I’d adore to be Prime Minister. And, yes, I believe very strongly in fascism. The only way we can speed up the sort of liberalism that’s hanging foul in the air at the moment is to speed up the progress of a right-wing, totally dictatorial tyranny and get it over as fast as possible. People have always responded with greater efficiency under a regimental leadership. A liberal wastes time saying, “Well, now, what ideas have you got?” Show them what to do, for God’s sake. If you don’t, nothing will get done. I can’t stand people just hanging about. Television is the most successful fascist, needless to say. Rock stars are fascists, too. Adolf Hitler was one of the first rock stars. PLAYBOY: How so? BOWIE: Think about it. Look at some of his films and see how he moved. I think he was quite as good as Jagger. It’s astounding. And, boy, when he hit that stage, he worked an audience. Good God! He was no politician. He was a media artist himself. He used politics and theatrics and created this thing that governed and controlled the show for those twelve years. The world will never see his like. He staged a country. Playboy had no more follow-up questions until the very end of the conversation, when Crowe, who was nineteen at the time, asked Bowie whether he stood by what he had said. “Everything but the inflammatory remarks,” Bowie replied, without specifying what they were. In later years, Bowie struggled to explain this interview and various other instances in which he dabbled in fascist rhetoric. He said that he had been “out of my mind totally, completely crazed”; that he had been thinking in mythological terms, not in political ones; that he had indulged in “glib, theatrical observations”; that the horror of the Holocaust had “completely evaded my extraordinary fucked-up nature at that particular time.” Critics and biographers took a similar line, proposing that Bowie had been suffering from “cocaine psychosis,” or that he had been speaking “at least partially tongue-in-cheek,” or that he had been dealing in “illogical irony.” The issue has periodically resurfaced in the years since Bowie’s death, in 2016, but it has hardly dented his reputation, which long ago attained the status of greatness and genius—a seamless extension of the old Romantic cult of the artist-hero. What’s striking about Bowie’s flirtation with fascism is how commonplace it was. Such is the import of “This Ain’t Rock ’n’ Roll: Pop Music, the Swastika, and the Third Reich” (Akashic), a recent book by the British musician and writer Daniel Rachel. (The title is taken from Bowie’s song “Diamond Dogs,” which begins with the announcement “This ain’t rock ’n’ roll—this is genocide!”) Although Rachel devotes a chapter to Bowie, he touches on so many other semi-fascist or full-on-Nazi outbursts from rock and pop musicians of the nineteen-sixties, seventies, and eighties that the Thin White Duke, as Bowie called himself circa 1976, gets lost in the shuffle. In this light, Kanye West’s intermittent admiration for Hitler seems less an anomaly than an homage to a venerable tradition. Reading Rachel’s compilation, I stopped worrying about the moral particulars of any one case—can the art be separated from the artist?—and instead wondered about the art itself. Something in the pop ritual keeps inducing the same symptoms. Rachel focusses on the British context, going back as far as the late fifties. When John Lennon was a Liverpool art student, he drew a picture of himself delivering a Hitler salute, with the legend “HEIL JOHN.” Lennon occasionally snapped such salutes in the following decade. When the members of the Beatles were choosing faces to feature on the cover of “Sgt. Pepper’s Lonely Hearts Club Band,” Lennon suggested Jesus and Hitler, neither of whom is visible. The innocent explanation is that Lennon and others of his generation were mocking the patriotic solemnity that their parents had conferred on the Second World War. George Harrison later said, “We were the generation who didn’t suffer from the war and we didn’t want to have to keep being told about Hitler.” At the time, the war was viewed more as a stomping of the Jerries than as a contest with genocidal evil. This was also the era of Mel Brooks’s “The Producers,” with its riotous “Springtime for Hitler” sequence—although Brooks’s obvious satirical intent, not to mention his status as a Jewish war veteran, places him in an entirely different category. A notable scene took place in Melbourne in 1964, when the Beatles greeted thousands of fans from a hotel balcony. Lennon raised his right arm and mimicked a Hitler mustache with his left index finger. Paul McCartney and George Harrison followed suit. One newspaper caption read, “Whenever the Beatles appeared on a balcony, they couldn’t resist giving Hitler-type salutes.” The Beatles clearly had in mind old newsreel footage of the Führer and the Duce greeting their hordes. You could ascribe a critical intent to the gesture—a subversive response to unchecked hype. For the most part, the band seemed wary of the unprecedented fame that had been conferred upon it. Creepier tics cropped up among the Beatles’ successors. Brian Jones, of the Rolling Stones, was photographed wearing an S.S. uniform and crushing a plastic doll with his boot. Mick Jagger praised Leni Riefenstahl’s “Triumph of the Will” and modelled for the director. Keith Moon thought it hilarious to march through a Jewish neighborhood in London wearing Third Reich regalia. Sid Vicious did much the same in Paris. Malcolm McLaren’s infatuation with Nazi style meant that swastikas became a staple of punk culture. Metal exhibited similar Teutonic tendencies—umlauts, Fraktur type, Iron Crosses. For Nikki Sixx, of Mötley Crüe, umlauts were “German and strong,” evocative of conquering youth. Motörhead’s Lemmy amassed a storehouse of Nazi memorabilia. Sinister associations were inscribed in band names: supposed sexual-slavery operations in German concentration camps, in the case of Joy Division; the prison where Rudolf Hess was held in postwar Berlin, in the case of Spandau Ballet. A Nazi-curious playlist would include the Sex Pistols’ “Belsen Was a Gas,” Devo’s “Triumph of the Will,” and the Ramones’ “Blitzkrieg Bop.” Many of rock’s ersatz Nazis were, like Bowie, subsequently embarrassed by their behavior. Rachel catalogues their various excuses, titling one section of his book “Never Mind the Swastikas.” Some opted for an aesthetic defense: everyone looks good in shiny black. According to McLaren, the Nazis had “the best clothes sense.” Their uniforms were deemed “great” (Eric Clapton), “just so pretty” (Dee Dee Ramone), and “the coolest” (Ace Frehley, of Kiss). Others insisted that they had just been larking about. When Lou Reed was asked why he had an Iron Cross shaved into his hair, he replied, “I was young. I was having fun.” Siouxsie Sioux, of Siouxsie and the Banshees, called Nazi antics an “anti-mums-and-dads thing.” Peter Hook, of Joy Division, declared that his band was “nothing more than a bunch of lads obsessed with the war.” Nick Cave said that his use of the swastika on two record covers in the early eighties was “deliberately moronic.” All the same, the trend troubled many members of the rock community—those who had managed to be young and dumb without donning an S.S. outfit or collecting Wehrmacht daggers. In the late seventies, the Rock Against Racism movement in the U.K. mobilized in response to anti-immigrant diatribes delivered by Clapton, which echoed the far-right National Front. Billy Bragg, who contributes an introduction to Rachel’s book, was a youthful member of that antifascist wave. The Dead Kennedys sang, “You still think swastikas look cool? / The real Nazis run your schools.” The rock critic Lester Bangs diagnosed white-supremacist strains in the punk world: “Anytime you conclude that life stinks and the human race mostly amounts to a pile of shit, you’ve got the perfect breeding ground for fascism.” In the dishevelled grunge years, Nazi regimentation lost prestige, although it didn’t disappear completely. Carlos Dengler, of the early-two-thousands band Interpol, adopted Hitler Youth drag, which he later rationalized as a critique of his German heritage. The final chapters of Rachel’s book turn to modern pop, showing in the process that fascist chic is not exclusively a white male problem. Michael Jackson’s teaser for the album “HIStory,” in which the star marched between rigid ranks of soldiers, was echt Riefenstahl. Madonna and Lady Gaga each dipped into S.S. kink by way of the 1974 film “The Night Porter.” Nicki Minaj alluded to “Triumph of the Will” in her “Only” music video. Last year, Kanye West released a song titled “Heil Hitler”; this year, he blamed a car accident and a bipolar condition for the misstep. For the most part, however, Nazi strutting belongs to the glory days of white rock and roll, alongside the decorative use of the Confederate flag and other gestures of reactionary rebellion. The British-born media theorist Dick Hebdige offered a gentler interpretation in his 1979 book, “Subculture: The Meaning of Style.” Focussing on punk, Hebdige argues that the likes of Siouxsie and Sid Vicious aimed to jolt the establishment by aping history’s supreme bogeymen. One scenester justified the swastika look by saying, “Punks just like to be hated.” Hebdige then turns to post-structuralist theory: “The signifier (swastika) had been willfully detached from the concept (Nazism) it conventionally signified.” In other words, in a postmodern, end-of-history age, fascist symbols lose their specific menace. Likewise, Pamela Rooke, a Sex Pistols adjutant who sported a red armband during the band’s television début in 1976, later shrugged off the episode by saying, “There was no Nazi Party now.” Johnny Ramone claimed that “there’s no threat right now of any Nazi thing,” though he did perceive a “communist threat.” Alas, signifiers have a way of reuniting with their signifieds, and the Nazi thing is alive and well. The source of the syndrome may ultimately lie in the theatrics of the pop spectacle: isolated figures receiving the adoration of a mesmerized public. Such ceremonies of idolatry go back into the deepest past, but mass communication empowered performers to an unparalleled degree, as it had empowered Hitler. In this sense, Bowie’s discussion of media manipulation in that Playboy interview offered a valuable bit of analysis. The Beatles recognized the new reality when they walked out on a Melbourne hotel balcony. Rapt throngs were materializing out of nowhere, summoned by signals that had circled the planet. Centuries of Anglo-American cultural imperialism laid the groundwork for the Beatles’ ascendancy: English was the language of hegemony. Sound reproduction put the entire globe within reach. Electric amplification enabled the band to perform for audiences of awesome size—fifty-five thousand people in Shea Stadium, in 1965. Without microphones, the Beatles and all their successors would have been inaudible. Such a superhuman magnification of the individual is unsettling to behold at close range. I witnessed the internal mechanics when I followed Radiohead on tour in 2000. Members of the band were variously thrilled, unnerved, and scared by what they were unleashing—a healthily complex reaction. Narcissists, not uncommon in pop circles, have no such hangups. They have always been preparing to greet crowds from a balcony; they are not surprised to hear their thoughts echoed by tens of thousands of voices in unison. But fame at the stadium level can have a corrosive effect on formerly amiable personalities, as we know from watching countless rise-and-fall documentaries on VH1. The music alone cannot generate a Nuremberg-rally atmosphere: the crucial factor is the audience’s desire for collective submission. That longing to become one with the throng is elemental. We humans love to discard our anxious selves and join a unanimous pack. If no sinister ideology is at work—and, usually, none is—what harm is being done? In a hyper-mediated context, though, the synchronization of thousands of bodies in a rite of glorification cannot avoid being political, and the politics in question is hardly egalitarian. This was a theme of Susan Sontag’s 1975 essay “Fascinating Fascism,” which was alert to darker trends in the pop realm. Sontag wrote, “The relations of domination and enslavement take the form of a characteristic pageantry: the massing of groups of people; the turning of people into things; the multiplication or replication of things; and the grouping of people/things around an all-powerful, hypnotic leader-figure or force.” Theodor W. Adorno went even further and denounced mass culture as inherently totalitarian. Having had a few ecstatic experiences of my own at stadium shows, I resist such drastic judgments. When Prince performed at Madison Square Garden in 2004, he imposed nothing more, and nothing less, than his own staggering virtuosity. At the same time, I mistrust pop culture’s boundless faith in its own innocence. As a rule, its products are held to be hugely influential in fostering progress, but when things go awry they are reclassified as harmless fun. Thus, Bowie’s announcement of his bisexuality is said to have significantly boosted gay rights while his Hitler fantasies amounted to boyish tomfoolery. In the same way, we tend not to talk about pop’s relationship with monopoly capitalism. The concentration of media power in the hands of a small circle of ubiquitous superstars mirrors economic inequality. Tellingly, with some honorable exceptions, élite celebrities have been circumspect about the trashing of democracy in the MAGA era. Many of them may dislike Trump, but politics is divisive and eats into profits. Although the twilight of the pop gods is probably a long way off, a counter-swing toward a more decentered, democratic marketplace is something to dream about, particularly as offshoots of quasi-socialism take hold here and there. We need something like a redistribution of cultural wealth. At some point, fans may tire of paying hundreds or even thousands of dollars to see, at the distance of a Hail Mary pass, Taylor Swift earning her second billion. Whether or not anything political hangs on the outcome, mass worship is irrational at heart, as pop’s wiser practitioners know. Bob Dylan once sang, “Never did get the message, don’t even know if one was sent to me / About the madness of becoming what one was never meant to be.” ♦
